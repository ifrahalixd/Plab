Mastoiditis & Cholesteatoma

Emergency: Suspected acute mastoiditis, sepsis, facial weakness or meningism requires urgent hospital assessment.

Overview

Acute mastoiditis is a serious complication of acute otitis media involving mastoid air cells. Cholesteatoma is a collection of keratinising squamous epithelium in the middle ear or mastoid that can progressively erode bone.

PLAB application: Differentiate acute infection from chronic destructive ear disease.

Mastoid anatomy

The mastoid air cells communicate with the middle ear through the aditus. Infection may extend from the middle ear to the mastoid and beyond.

PLAB application: Explain why untreated middle-ear infection can spread.

Acute mastoiditis presentation

Fever, persistent otalgia, otorrhoea, postauricular erythema, tenderness and swelling; the pinna may protrude forwards and downwards.

PLAB application: Postauricular swelling after AOM is a red flag.

Who is at risk?

Mastoiditis is more common in children but can occur at any age. A preceding AOM episode is common; symptoms can progress despite treatment.

PLAB application: Do not exclude mastoiditis because antibiotics were already prescribed.

Mastoiditis examination

Assess observations, sepsis, hydration, neurological status, mastoid tenderness, postauricular swelling and both ears by otoscopy if safe.

PLAB application: Look for pinna displacement and systemic illness.

Mastoiditis investigations

Urgent ENT assessment is central. Blood tests and cultures may support management; contrast CT temporal bone/head is considered when complications, diagnostic uncertainty or surgery are concerns, guided by ENT.

PLAB application: Do not delay urgent referral to obtain imaging.

Mastoiditis management

Hospital admission, IV antibiotics according to local policy, analgesia, fluids and ENT review are typical. Myringotomy or mastoid surgery may be needed if abscess, complications or poor response.

PLAB application: This is not a routine outpatient ear infection.

Mastoiditis complications

Subperiosteal abscess, facial nerve palsy, labyrinthitis, meningitis, intracranial abscess and sigmoid sinus thrombosis are important complications.

PLAB application: Headache, neck stiffness, altered consciousness or focal deficits are emergencies.

What is cholesteatoma?

A non-cancerous but locally destructive mass of keratinising squamous epithelium. It can be congenital or acquired, often associated with tympanic membrane retraction or perforation.

PLAB application: Benign does not mean harmless.

Cholesteatoma presentation

Persistent or recurrent often foul-smelling otorrhoea, conductive hearing loss, recurrent infections and sometimes tinnitus or dizziness.

PLAB application: Think cholesteatoma with persistent unilateral offensive discharge.

Otoscopy clues

A retraction pocket, attic crust, pearly white debris or marginal perforation may suggest cholesteatoma. Avoid aggressive probing or irrigation.

PLAB application: Refer suspected cholesteatoma to ENT.

Cholesteatoma investigations

ENT microscopic examination, audiometry and imaging where indicated (CT for bony anatomy; diffusion-weighted MRI in selected cases) guide management.

PLAB application: Normal-looking external ear does not rule it out.

Cholesteatoma management

Definitive treatment is usually surgical removal, with techniques selected by ENT. Ear cleaning and topical treatment may control discharge but do not remove the lesion.

PLAB application: Explain surgery aims to create a safe, dry ear and preserve hearing when possible.

Cholesteatoma complications

Ossicular erosion, conductive or mixed hearing loss, vertigo from labyrinthine fistula, facial palsy and intracranial infection can occur.

PLAB application: New vertigo, facial weakness or neurological symptoms need urgent assessment.

Differentials

Otitis externa causes tragal tenderness and inflamed canal. AOM causes acute otalgia and bulging tympanic membrane. Chronic suppurative otitis media also causes persistent discharge.

PLAB application: Use history and otoscopy to distinguish causes of otorrhoea.

PLAB 2 and safety-netting

Ask about duration, discharge, hearing, fever, prior AOM, vertigo, facial weakness and neurological symptoms. Examine ears and mastoid, assess observations, explain referral and address concerns.

PLAB application: Urgently escalate mastoid swelling, sepsis, meningism, facial palsy or severe vertigo.

PLAB 2 OSCE: ear discharge and mastoid pain

Introduce yourself, obtain consent, assess pain, discharge, hearing, fever, preceding AOM and red flags. Examine the ear and mastoid; explain the suspected diagnosis, need for ENT referral, treatment options and safety-netting. Escalate immediately if systemically unwell.

Deep Dive

Spread from middle ear

The mastoid air cells communicate with the middle ear; infection may extend into bone or adjacent intracranial structures.

Why the pinna protrudes

Postauricular inflammation and subperiosteal collections can displace the pinna.

Why cholesteatoma erodes bone

Accumulating keratin and local inflammatory processes can damage ossicles and surrounding bone.

Hearing loss

Ossicular damage usually causes conductive hearing loss; labyrinthine complications may cause sensorineural components.

Imaging choices

CT helps assess bony structures and complications; diffusion-weighted MRI may help assess cholesteatoma in selected cases.

Shared decision-making

Explain surgery, recurrence risk, hearing goals and the need for ongoing specialist follow-up.
