Mental capacity and best interests

Learning outcomes: Apply the five Mental Capacity Act principles; assess capacity for a specific decision; recognise fluctuating capacity; use the best-interests process; distinguish capacity law from mental health detention law.

1. The legal setting

The Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA) applies to people aged 16 and over in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have different legal frameworks; do not assume MCA rules apply throughout the UK. This lesson focuses on England and Wales unless stated otherwise.

2. Five statutory principles

  1. Presume capacity unless established otherwise.
  2. Take all practicable steps to support a person to decide before treating them as unable.
  3. An unwise decision does not by itself establish incapacity.
  4. Any act or decision for someone lacking capacity must be in their best interests.
  5. Choose the least restrictive effective option.

3. The two-stage test

Stage 1: Is there an impairment or disturbance in the functioning of the mind or brain (temporary or permanent)? Stage 2: Because of it, is the person unable to understand, retain, use or weigh relevant information, or communicate their decision by any means? The impairment must cause the inability. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific.

Inability to retain information only briefly does not necessarily preclude capacity. A patient can communicate through writing, gestures, interpreters or assistive technology.

4. Support decision-making

Use plain language, an interpreter when needed, visual aids, a quiet setting, treatment of pain or delirium, and a suitable time of day. Do not equate aphasia, learning disability, dementia or disagreement with incapacity.

5. Best interests

If the person lacks capacity for this decision, consider all relevant circumstances, the person's past and present wishes, beliefs and values, and views of those appropriately involved. Involve the person as far as possible. Consider whether capacity may return and whether a non-urgent decision can wait. Never decide solely on age, appearance, diagnosis or behaviour.

6. Legal authority and safeguards

Check for a valid and applicable advance decision to refuse treatment (ADRT), a health-and-welfare lasting power of attorney with relevant authority, or a court-appointed deputy. Life-sustaining treatment has additional safeguards. An IMCA may be required for certain serious decisions when there is no appropriate person to consult. Deprivation of liberty requires a separate lawful authorisation; best interests alone is not sufficient.

PLAB trap: A relative is not automatically the decision-maker. Family views can inform best interests, but legal authority must be checked.

Capacity and best-interests decision pathway

Step 1 · Define the decision

Exactly what is being decided, and when? Assess the relevant risks and information.

Step 2 · Optimise support

Address pain, confusion, communication, hearing, language and timing; provide information accessibly.

Step 3 · Apply the two-stage capacity test

Mind/brain impairment or disturbance? If yes, does it cause inability to understand, retain, use/weigh, or communicate the decision?

Step 4A · Capacity present

Respect the informed, voluntary decision, including refusal of life-saving treatment. Document discussion.

Step 4B · Capacity absent

Check advance decisions and authorised proxies; consult appropriately, determine best interests and choose the least restrictive option.

Step 5 · Review and document

Record decision, support offered, evidence for each test element, reasoning, consultees, legal authority and review plan.

Emergency: If urgent treatment is needed and the patient lacks capacity, provide necessary care under the applicable legal framework while checking for any known binding refusal; seek senior help.

Four clinical reasoning cases

Case 1 · Delirium after surgery

An 82-year-old with sepsis is disorientated and tries to remove IV access. He cannot explain the purpose of antibiotics despite support.

Approach: Treat reversible causes; assess capacity for this specific decision; if absent, use a best-interests decision and the least restrictive safe approach. Reassess as delirium resolves.

Case 2 · Unwise refusal

A 48-year-old with capacity declines an amputation despite the risk of death.

Approach: Confirm understanding and voluntariness, explore reasons and alternatives, respect refusal and document. An unwise choice is not incapacity.

Case 3 · Dementia and consent

A woman with mild dementia can explain the proposed blood test, its purpose and alternatives.

Approach: Dementia alone does not establish incapacity. She may have capacity for this decision even if she lacks capacity for a complex financial decision.

Case 4 · Family demands treatment

A son demands feeding-tube insertion for his mother, who lacks capacity. No legal proxy has been identified.

Approach: Check ADRT/proxy/court orders; consult family for her values, evaluate clinical benefits and burdens, and make a properly documented best-interests decision. The son cannot automatically consent on her behalf.

PLAB 2 simulated station · Capacity assessment

Station: A 76-year-old inpatient with pneumonia refuses IV antibiotics. Nursing staff report fluctuating confusion. You have 8 minutes to assess and explain the next steps.

Suggested structure

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and obtain permission to talk.
  2. Clarify the precise treatment decision and the patient's understanding.
  3. Explore reasons, values, concerns and possible coercion.
  4. Explain diagnosis, proposed treatment, alternatives and consequences of refusal.
  5. Optimise hearing, language, comfort and attention.
  6. Assess understanding, retention, use/weighing and communication; identify possible delirium.
  7. Explain whether the patient has capacity for this decision now; seek senior input as appropriate.
  8. If capacity absent, consider prior wishes, relevant authority and best interests; use the least restrictive approach.
  9. Document and plan reassessment.

Practice checklist

Avoid saying “The patient is confused, so they cannot consent.” Demonstrate the functional test.
