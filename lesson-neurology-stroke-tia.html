Stroke & Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)

PLAB priority: Stroke is time critical: check glucose, establish last-known-well, perform urgent brain imaging and activate the stroke team.

01. Recognise stroke immediately

Sudden facial droop, arm weakness or speech disturbance are typical; also consider sudden visual loss, ataxia, vertigo with focal signs and posterior circulation symptoms.

PLAB application: Use FAST, establish last-known-well time and activate the local stroke pathway.

02. ABCDE and immediate priorities

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Check capillary glucose, oxygen saturation, temperature, blood pressure and cardiac rhythm.

PLAB application: Hypoglycaemia can mimic stroke; correct it immediately.

03. Stroke versus TIA

Stroke causes acute focal neurological dysfunction due to infarction or haemorrhage. TIA is transient focal ischaemia without acute infarction.

PLAB application: Resolved symptoms still require urgent specialist assessment.

04. Ischaemic versus haemorrhagic stroke

Most strokes are ischaemic; intracerebral haemorrhage may cause headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness or focal deficits. Clinical features alone cannot reliably distinguish them.

PLAB application: Urgent brain imaging is essential before antithrombotic treatment.

05. Focused history

Record exact onset/last known well, evolution, anticoagulants, trauma, previous events, vascular risks, headache, seizure, infection and baseline function.

PLAB application: Ask witnesses and review medication timings, especially DOACs.

06. Neurological examination

Assess GCS, pupils, visual fields, gaze, facial movements, speech/language, limb power, sensation, coordination and neglect; NIHSS is used by trained staff.

PLAB application: Do not let a detailed examination delay imaging or emergency treatment.

07. Urgent investigations

Check glucose, FBC, electrolytes, renal function, coagulation where indicated, ECG and urgent non-contrast CT brain. Consider CT angiography for suspected large-vessel occlusion.

PLAB application: CT excludes haemorrhage; early ischaemia may not be visible.

08. Acute ischaemic stroke treatment

Specialist teams consider IV thrombolysis in eligible patients within a time window, typically 4.5 hours, and mechanical thrombectomy for selected large-vessel occlusions, sometimes later with imaging selection.

PLAB application: Treatment eligibility depends on imaging, timing, contraindications and local protocol.

09. Antiplatelets and anticoagulants

After imaging excludes haemorrhage, aspirin 300 mg is generally given promptly in acute ischaemic stroke when appropriate; avoid antiplatelets immediately after thrombolysis until protocol reassessment.

PLAB application: Do not automatically anticoagulate acute stroke; AF anticoagulation timing is individualised.

10. Intracerebral haemorrhage

Urgently involve stroke/neurosurgical teams; control blood pressure per protocol, reverse anticoagulation when indicated and manage raised intracranial pressure.

PLAB application: Never give thrombolysis or aspirin for confirmed intracerebral haemorrhage.

11. TIA emergency pathway

Suspected TIA requires immediate aspirin 300 mg unless contraindicated and specialist assessment within 24 hours. Do not use ABCD2 to determine urgency.

PLAB application: Consider alternative diagnoses but never dismiss resolved focal deficits.

12. Stroke mimics

Hypoglycaemia, seizure with Todd paresis, migraine aura, functional neurological disorder and vestibular conditions may mimic stroke.

PLAB application: Check glucose and seek specialist assessment; mimics do not justify delaying stroke evaluation.

13. Swallow and supportive care

Keep patients nil by mouth until swallow screening; manage hydration, temperature, glucose, mobility, pressure injury and VTE risk.

PLAB application: Avoid aspiration and unnecessary delays to rehabilitation.

14. Secondary prevention

Address hypertension, smoking, diabetes, lipids and atrial fibrillation. Antiplatelets for non-cardioembolic stroke; anticoagulation often for AF-related stroke when safe.

PLAB application: Carotid imaging and intervention may be relevant for symptomatic stenosis.

15. Rehabilitation and complications

Early multidisciplinary rehabilitation addresses mobility, dysphagia, aphasia, cognition, mood, continence and discharge planning.

PLAB application: Assess post-stroke depression and caregiver needs.

16. PLAB 2 communication

Explain suspected stroke clearly, establish onset, obtain consent where possible, involve relatives appropriately and safety-net transient symptoms.

PLAB application: Any new stroke symptoms: call emergency services immediately.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Cerebral blood flow

In ischaemic stroke an occluded artery produces an infarct core surrounded by potentially salvageable penumbra. Faster reperfusion can preserve brain tissue.

Why onset matters

Reperfusion eligibility depends on time since last-known-well, imaging and bleeding risk. Some patients with unknown onset or later presentation may qualify through advanced imaging selection.

Large-vessel occlusion

CT angiography identifies proximal arterial blockage. Mechanical thrombectomy can benefit selected patients, with imaging and specialist criteria determining eligibility.

TIA mechanism

Transient arterial occlusion can resolve before tissue infarction, but recurrent events may follow quickly. Urgent investigation includes rhythm and carotid assessment as indicated.

Haemorrhage versus infarction

Clinical presentation overlaps. Non-contrast CT distinguishes haemorrhage; management diverges sharply because thrombolysis and antithrombotics can worsen bleeding.

Communication and rehabilitation

Assess aphasia separately from cognition, use short clear phrases and communication aids, involve speech and language therapy and support patient autonomy.
