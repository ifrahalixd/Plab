Full Musculoskeletal Examination, GALS & Joint Assessment

PLAB priority: GALS screens; Look–Feel–Move–Function examines. Recognise the acutely hot swollen joint as an emergency.

01. Purpose and structure

Musculoskeletal examination identifies pain, inflammation, deformity, weakness and loss of function. Start with introduction, consent, hand hygiene, exposure, pain check and a chaperone when appropriate. Compare sides and examine the joint above and below.

PLAB application: State what you are examining and ask about pain before movement.

02. GALS screening

GALS means Gait, Arms, Legs and Spine. Ask three screening questions: Do you have pain or stiffness in muscles, joints or back? Can you dress yourself completely without difficulty? Can you walk up and down stairs without difficulty? A positive screen prompts a regional examination.

PLAB application: GALS is a screening tool, not a substitute for focused joint examination.

03. Gait

Observe walking, turning, balance, stride, arm swing and use of aids. Look for antalgic, Trendelenburg, shuffling, high-stepping or spastic gait. Consider falls risk and safety.

PLAB application: Antalgic gait shortens stance on the painful side.

04. Arms screen

Inspect hands, nails and muscle bulk; ask patient to put hands behind head, straighten elbows, extend arms, turn palms up and down, make a fist and squeeze fingers. Look for swelling, deformity and limited movement.

PLAB application: DIP involvement suggests osteoarthritis or psoriatic arthritis; MCP/PIP synovitis suggests inflammatory arthritis.

05. Legs screen

Inspect alignment, swelling and muscle wasting. Assess knee flexion/extension and hip internal rotation as tolerated. Look at feet and ankles for deformity, swelling, calluses and weight-bearing difficulty.

PLAB application: Assess function as well as range of motion.

06. Spine screen

Inspect posture and spinal curvature from behind and side. Assess cervical movement, lumbar flexion and lateral flexion as tolerated; look for asymmetry or restricted movement. Neurological red flags require urgent assessment.

PLAB application: Inflammatory back pain improves with exercise and may wake patients in the second half of the night.

07. Regional examination sequence

Use Look, Feel, Move, Function, and special tests where relevant. Inspect from all sides; palpate temperature, tenderness, effusions and bony landmarks; assess active then passive range of movement; test strength and neurovascular status when indicated.

PLAB application: Describe precise joint, laterality, distribution and whether limitation is painful or mechanical.

08. Hand and wrist examination

Inspect nails, thenar/hypothenar wasting, MCP/PIP/DIP swelling, ulnar deviation, swan-neck and boutonnière deformities. Palpate MCPs, PIPs and wrists; assess grip, pinch, wrist flexion/extension and functional tasks.

PLAB application: RA classically affects MCP/PIP and wrists; OA often affects DIP, PIP and first CMC.

09. Shoulder and elbow examination

Inspect for asymmetry and wasting. Palpate acromioclavicular joint and epicondyles. Test active and passive shoulder abduction, flexion, rotation; elbow flexion/extension and pronation/supination. Painful arc can suggest subacromial pathology, but no single test confirms it.

PLAB application: Marked loss of both active and passive shoulder movement suggests capsular restriction.

10. Hip examination

Observe gait and leg length where relevant. With appropriate exposure and consent, examine hip flexion, internal/external rotation, abduction and adduction. Hip disease may present as groin, thigh or knee pain.

PLAB application: Reduced internal rotation is an early sign of hip joint pathology.

11. Knee examination

Inspect alignment, scars, swelling, erythema and quadriceps wasting. Palpate warmth, joint line and patella; assess effusion, flexion/extension and gait. Perform ligament/meniscal tests only when indicated and safe.

PLAB application: A hot swollen knee requires septic arthritis consideration, even with known gout.

12. Ankle and foot examination

Inspect footwear, skin, ulcers, deformity, arches, toes and gait. Palpate joints, Achilles tendon and plantar fascia. Test ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion and subtalar movement; assess pulses and sensation when indicated.

PLAB application: A diabetic patient with a red, hot, swollen foot may have Charcot neuroarthropathy.

13. Inflammatory versus mechanical patterns

Inflammatory arthritis commonly causes prolonged morning stiffness, warmth, synovitis and improvement with movement; mechanical disease commonly worsens with use and has shorter stiffness. Patterns overlap; fever, trauma and neurological signs change priorities.

PLAB application: A normal CRP or negative rheumatoid factor does not rule out inflammatory arthritis.

14. Emergency red flags

Hot swollen joint, systemic illness, inability to bear weight, suspected fracture, acute neurovascular compromise, new bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle anaesthesia or progressive neurological deficit need urgent escalation. Suspected septic arthritis needs prompt hospital evaluation and joint aspiration when feasible.

PLAB application: Do not reassure a patient with a hot swollen joint solely because they have gout.

15. Investigations and interpretation

Tests depend on history and examination: inflammatory markers, FBC, urate, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, cultures, joint aspirate, ultrasound, X-ray or MRI. Joint aspiration can assess microscopy, crystals and culture; crystals do not exclude infection. Routine imaging is unnecessary for typical uncomplicated OA.

PLAB application: Explain why each investigation changes management rather than ordering a generic panel.

16. PLAB 2 completion and presentation

Thank the patient, ensure comfort, wash hands, and summarise findings clearly: affected joints, symmetry, swelling, warmth, range, function and red flags. Suggest relevant neurovascular assessment, other joints, investigations and escalation. Provide empathetic explanation and safety-netting.

PLAB application: Use an OSCE summary: I found symmetrical MCP/PIP synovitis with reduced grip, consistent with inflammatory polyarthritis; I would refer urgently.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

GALS: screening versus diagnosis

GALS rapidly identifies functional problems but is not designed to diagnose specific diseases. A positive screen should lead to a focused regional examination and targeted history.

Synovitis versus bony enlargement

Boggy, tender soft-tissue swelling suggests synovitis; hard enlargement can reflect osteophytes. Compare sides and consider ultrasound when clinical uncertainty affects management.

Active and passive movement

Loss of active movement with preserved passive movement suggests weakness or tendon pathology; restriction of both suggests joint or capsular pathology, though pain can confound findings.

Joint patterns and localisation

Symmetric small-joint synovitis suggests RA; DIP involvement and nail pitting suggest psoriatic disease; first CMC and DIP bony enlargement suggest OA. No pattern alone proves a diagnosis.

Why septic arthritis cannot wait

Joint infection may rapidly damage cartilage and cause sepsis. Fever or raised inflammatory markers may be absent; prompt aspiration and specialist treatment are crucial.

How to score in a PLAB 2 station

Use patient-centred communication, systematic examination, accurate interpretation and an explicit safe management plan. Explain abnormal findings in plain language and mention urgent escalation where necessary.
