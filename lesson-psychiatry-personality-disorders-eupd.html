Personality Disorders & Emotionally Unstable Personality Disorder (EUPD)

PLAB priority: Assess self-harm without stigma, distinguish EUPD from bipolar episodes, use structured psychological care and know NICE medication guidance.

01. What is a personality disorder?

An enduring pattern of difficulties in how a person relates to self and others, regulates emotions, thinks and behaves, associated with distress or impairment. Traits vary across context and time; avoid defining a person by a label.

PLAB application: Use a non-judgemental, formulation-led assessment and consider whether symptoms reflect trauma, neurodevelopmental differences or another mental illness.

02. Diagnostic approach

Explore longstanding patterns, onset, relationships, functioning, coping, trauma and current symptoms. Diagnosis requires a comprehensive longitudinal assessment, not a single crisis encounter.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose EUPD solely because a patient self-harms or attends emergency care repeatedly.

03. Personality disorder clusters

Traditional descriptions group paranoid, schizoid and schizotypal traits in cluster A; antisocial, borderline, histrionic and narcissistic in B; avoidant, dependent and obsessive-compulsive in C. These are descriptive groupings, not substitutes for individual assessment.

PLAB application: Recognise classic examination descriptions while using person-centred language.

04. EUPD / borderline pattern

Features may include emotional instability, intense fear of abandonment, unstable relationships, impulsivity, identity disturbance, chronic emptiness, anger, dissociation and recurrent self-harm. Not everyone has all features.

PLAB application: Assess the specific difficulty and its impact rather than relying on stereotypes.

05. Differentiating bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder involves distinct episodes of mania/hypomania or depression with sustained changes in mood, energy and sleep. EUPD-related mood changes may be rapid and interpersonal-context linked, but conditions can coexist.

PLAB application: Ask about clear episodes of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, increased goal-directed activity and duration.

06. Self-harm and suicide risk

Self-harm may occur with or without suicidal intent. Ask directly about thoughts, plans, means, previous attempts, protective factors, intoxication, current supports and immediate safety. Risk is dynamic.

PLAB application: Do not use a low/medium/high risk score as a substitute for clinical formulation or to decide discharge.

07. Crisis assessment

Assess physical injury or overdose first; use a calm, validating approach, collaborative safety planning and a clear follow-up arrangement. Consider safeguarding and capacity where relevant.

PLAB application: Avoid punitive responses, dismissing distress or assuming repeated presentations are not serious.

08. Psychological treatments

Structured psychological care is central. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) may be considered particularly for women with borderline personality disorder where reducing recurrent self-harm is a priority; other structured approaches include mentalisation-based treatment.

PLAB application: Explain that therapies develop emotion regulation, interpersonal and distress-tolerance skills.

09. Medication and NICE guidance

NICE CG78 advises against drug treatment specifically for borderline personality disorder or its individual symptoms/behaviours; antipsychotics should not be used for medium- or long-term treatment of BPD. Treat coexisting illnesses according to their own guidance.

PLAB application: Do not offer an SSRI or antipsychotic as a cure for EUPD itself.

10. Short-term crisis prescribing

In a crisis, NICE allows cautious short-term sedative medication in exceptional circumstances with agreed target symptoms, minimum effective dose, limited quantity and duration no longer than one week. Review all medications after crisis resolution.

PLAB application: Prioritise psychosocial support and medication safety; avoid polypharmacy.

11. Comorbidity

Depression, PTSD, substance use, eating disorders, anxiety, ADHD and bipolar disorder can coexist. Explore trauma carefully and do not presume causation.

PLAB application: Manage treatable comorbidities while maintaining continuity of care.

12. Other personality presentations

Antisocial traits can involve disregard for others and impulsivity; avoidant traits involve social inhibition and fear of criticism; obsessive-compulsive personality traits involve rigidity and perfectionism, distinct from OCD obsessions/compulsions.

PLAB application: Avoid stigmatising labels and distinguish personality patterns from episodic illness.

13. Therapeutic communication

Validate emotion without endorsing harmful behaviour; maintain predictable boundaries, collaborative decisions and consistent team messages. Ask what has helped previously.

PLAB application: Try: “I can see this feels overwhelming. Can we work out how to keep you safe tonight?”

14. PLAB 2 mental state examination

Assess appearance, behaviour, speech, mood/affect, thought, perception, cognition, insight and capacity as indicated. Examine dissociation and transient stress-related paranoia sensitively.

PLAB application: Complete a physical assessment if intoxication, overdose, injury or medical illness is suspected.

15. Referral and continuity

Arrange appropriate mental health team assessment for persistent impairment, recurrent crises, diagnostic uncertainty or complex comorbidity. Share a jointly agreed crisis plan across services with consent and within information-sharing rules.

PLAB application: Clear follow-up, named contact and crisis access are essential after discharge.

16. Stigma, prognosis and recovery

Many people improve substantially with structured support over time. Use respectful terminology, explain diagnosis collaboratively and focus on goals, strengths and recovery.

PLAB application: Never describe a patient as manipulative or untreatable; assess each presentation on its merits.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Emotion regulation and threat sensitivity

Intense emotional reactions can be influenced by stress, prior experiences and difficulties returning to baseline. This is not a moral failing or evidence of deliberate behaviour.

Attachment and relationships

Fear of rejection and unstable expectations can make interpersonal events feel threatening. Explore patterns with curiosity rather than attributing intent.

DBT mechanism

Skills training helps patients recognise emotional escalation, tolerate distress safely, regulate responses and communicate needs.

Why medication is not first-line

Evidence for treating the core borderline pattern pharmacologically is limited, while adverse effects, interactions and overdose risk matter. Coexisting disorders are assessed separately.

Dynamic suicide risk

Intent and access to means can change quickly. Clinical assessment and a jointly constructed plan are more useful than assigning a fixed risk category.

Differential and stigma

Bipolar disorder, PTSD, ADHD, autism and substance effects can overlap with personality difficulties. Formulations should be revisited and documented without pejorative language.
