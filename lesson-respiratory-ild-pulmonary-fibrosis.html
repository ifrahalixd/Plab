Interstitial Lung Disease & Pulmonary Fibrosis: diagnosis, airway clearance and exacerbations

Emergency reminder: Major haemoptysis, severe breathlessness, confusion or sepsis requires emergency assessment and escalation. This lesson is educational, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

Interstitial lung disease: overview

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) describes a diverse group of disorders affecting the lung interstitium and often alveoli. Inflammation and/or fibrosis can impair gas exchange and reduce lung compliance.

Clinical application: Think of ILD in progressive exertional breathlessness, persistent dry cough, fine inspiratory crackles and restrictive physiology.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)

IPF is a chronic progressive fibrosing interstitial pneumonia of unknown cause, typically affecting older adults. Diagnosis requires multidisciplinary assessment and exclusion of known causes.

Clinical application: Fine bibasal 'Velcro' crackles and clubbing may be present. IPF is not synonymous with all pulmonary fibrosis.

Causes and associations

ILD may relate to connective tissue diseases, occupational/environmental exposures (asbestos, silica), hypersensitivity pneumonitis, sarcoidosis, medications, radiation or no identifiable cause.

Clinical application: Ask about birds, mould, farming, dust, medications such as amiodarone or nitrofurantoin, autoimmune symptoms and family history.

History and examination

Assess onset, progression, cough, exertional limitation, oxygen needs, smoking, systemic symptoms, exposure and occupational history.

Clinical application: Look for clubbing, fine late-inspiratory crackles, resting/exertional desaturation, signs of pulmonary hypertension and connective tissue disease.

Pulmonary function tests

Fibrotic ILD commonly produces a restrictive pattern with reduced FVC and total lung capacity and reduced gas transfer (DLCO/TLCO). Spirometry may be near normal early in disease.

Clinical application: A low FVC is not diagnostic alone; full lung function and gas transfer help characterise severity and progression.

Chest X-ray and HRCT

Chest radiography can show bilateral reticular opacities but may be insensitive. High-resolution CT is central to ILD pattern recognition, including usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP).

Clinical application: UIP patterns may show basal/subpleural reticulation, traction interstitial lung disease and honeycombing; interpretation requires expert radiology and clinical context.

Multidisciplinary diagnosis

ILD multidisciplinary teams integrate clinical features, imaging, physiology and sometimes tissue sampling to distinguish IPF from other ILDs.

Clinical application: Avoid labelling all fibrotic changes as IPF or starting immunosuppression before the diagnosis is clear.

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

Immune-mediated lung disease from inhaled antigens can be inflammatory, fibrotic or mixed. Common exposures include birds, mould and certain workplaces.

Clinical application: A careful exposure history and antigen avoidance are important; specialist assessment guides treatment.

Connective tissue disease–associated ILD

Rheumatoid arthritis, systemic sclerosis, myositis and other autoimmune conditions may involve the lungs.

Clinical application: Ask about Raynaud phenomenon, joint pain, skin thickening, muscle weakness and sicca symptoms; arrange targeted autoimmune testing.

Sarcoidosis

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem granulomatous disorder that often affects the lungs and hilar lymph nodes. Presentation ranges from incidental bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy to cough, breathlessness and systemic symptoms.

Clinical application: Look for eye, skin and cardiac involvement; treatment depends on organ involvement and severity.

IPF treatment and antifibrotics

Specialist antifibrotic therapy, including nintedanib or pirfenidone in eligible patients, can slow decline in lung function; it does not reverse established scarring.

Clinical application: Eligibility and monitoring depend on current NICE technology appraisals and specialist assessment. Review liver function, interactions and adverse effects.

Supportive care and rehabilitation

Smoking cessation, vaccinations, pulmonary rehabilitation, exercise advice, symptom management and psychosocial support are important across ILDs.

Clinical application: Assess oxygen needs formally when hypoxaemia is present, and discuss supportive/palliative care early when appropriate.

Acute deterioration

Acute worsening of breathlessness in ILD may be due to infection, pulmonary embolism, heart failure, pneumothorax or acute exacerbation of fibrotic ILD.

Clinical application: Assess ABCDE, oxygenation, imaging and urgent specialist input; acute exacerbation of IPF can be life-threatening.

Pulmonary hypertension and complications

Chronic fibrotic lung disease can cause hypoxaemia and pulmonary hypertension; complications include respiratory failure, infection and reduced quality of life.

Clinical application: Watch for worsening exertional syncope, oedema and severe desaturation; refer for specialist assessment.

Follow-up and prognosis

Monitor symptoms, FVC, DLCO, exercise desaturation, imaging when indicated and patient priorities. The course varies considerably by ILD subtype.

Clinical application: Shared decision-making should cover treatment benefits, side effects, transplant referral when appropriate and advance care planning.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that 'scarring of the lungs' can make oxygen transfer and breathing more difficult, while emphasising that different ILDs have different causes and treatments.

Clinical application: Discuss investigations, MDT review, medication monitoring, pulmonary rehabilitation, oxygen assessment and urgent red flags.

PLAB 2: ILD counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself and explore symptoms, function, concerns and exposures.
  2. Explain that several different lung diseases can cause inflammation or scarring.
  3. Discuss lung function tests, HRCT and specialist MDT review.
  4. Explain that treatment varies by subtype; discuss rehabilitation and monitoring.
  5. Safety-net urgent worsening breathlessness, chest pain, cyanosis or confusion.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Fibrosis and compliance

Collagen deposition and architectural distortion stiffen lungs, increasing work of breathing and reducing lung volumes.

Gas transfer

Alveolar-capillary injury and vascular abnormalities reduce DLCO and cause exertional desaturation.

UIP versus NSIP

UIP often shows heterogeneous basal subpleural fibrosis and honeycombing; NSIP is commonly more uniform with ground-glass change. Patterns overlap and require MDT interpretation.

Antifibrotics

Nintedanib inhibits multiple tyrosine kinases; pirfenidone has antifibrotic actions. Both can slow FVC decline in appropriate patients but require monitoring.

Why immunosuppression is not universal

Some inflammatory ILDs respond to immunosuppression, but IPF is biologically distinct. Treatment must follow the established subtype.

Acute exacerbation

An acute respiratory decline with new bilateral alveolar abnormality may reflect acute exacerbation of fibrotic ILD, but infection and other causes must be evaluated.
