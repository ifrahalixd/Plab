Paediatric surgical abdomen — a time-critical approach

Red flags: bilious (green) vomiting in a neonate/infant is bowel obstruction until proven otherwise. Urgently involve paediatric surgery; do not wait for a classic presentation. Shock, peritonitis, bloody stool, abdominal distension or poor perfusion require immediate escalation.

1 · First assessment and stabilisation

Use paediatric ABCDE, weight-based observations and glucose. Assess hydration, perfusion, pain, fever, vomiting colour, stool passage, meconium timing, abdominal distension, groin swellings, birth history and antenatal scans. Keep nil by mouth, obtain IV/IO access if indicated, give appropriate isotonic fluid resuscitation for shock, correct hypoglycaemia/electrolytes, provide analgesia, consider nasogastric decompression and seek senior paediatric and surgical help early. Monitor urine output and repeat examinations. Do not delay resuscitation for imaging.

2 · Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis

Typically 2–8 weeks of age: progressively forceful non-bilious projectile vomiting after feeds, persistent hunger, weight loss/dehydration; a palpable olive is not always present. Classic labs show hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, but early cases may not. Ultrasound confirms pyloric muscle thickening/elongation according to local radiology criteria. Resuscitate and correct chloride, potassium and alkalosis before surgery; definitive treatment is Ramstedt pyloromyotomy. It is not a crash operation before correction of dehydration.

3 · Intussusception

Usually 3 months–3 years; telescoping bowel, commonly ileocolic. Intermittent severe colicky pain with crying and leg-drawing, pallor/lethargy, vomiting; child may seem well between episodes. 'Redcurrant jelly' stool and palpable sausage mass are late/inconsistent. Ultrasound: target/doughnut sign. If stable and without perforation/peritonitis, image-guided air/contrast enema can diagnose and reduce. Surgical intervention is needed for failed enema, perforation, peritonitis or instability. IV access, analgesia and surgical/radiology coordination first.

4 · Malrotation with midgut volvulus

Bilious vomiting, sudden pain/distension, shock or bloody stool, often in the neonatal period but possible later. Volvulus compromises mesenteric blood supply and causes rapid bowel necrosis. Immediate paediatric surgical consultation, resuscitation, NBM and NG decompression. Upper GI contrast series is often used when stable and diagnosis uncertain; do not delay theatre in an unstable child with suspected volvulus. Ladd procedure with detorsion is definitive.

5 · Duodenal atresia

Early neonatal bilious vomiting (occasionally non-bilious depending on obstruction level); antenatal polyhydramnios; abdominal radiograph 'double bubble'. Associated with trisomy 21. Stabilise, NG decompression and surgical repair. Distinguish from volvulus, which may be immediately ischaemic.

6 · Jejunoileal atresia and meconium ileus

Neonatal bilious vomiting, distension and failure to pass meconium. Jejunoileal atresia results in multiple dilated loops; requires surgery. Meconium ileus is associated with cystic fibrosis; specialist contrast enema may relieve uncomplicated obstruction, while complicated cases need surgery.

7 · Hirschsprung disease

Aganglionosis of distal bowel: delayed meconium passage beyond 48 hours, distension, bilious vomiting and chronic constipation; some children present later. Diagnosis by rectal suction biopsy demonstrating absent ganglion cells. Contrast enema may show transition zone. Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis presents with fever, explosive diarrhoea, distension and shock: emergency resuscitation, IV antibiotics, rectal washouts by specialists and urgent surgical input. Definitive treatment is pull-through surgery.

8 · Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC)

Primarily preterm or medically vulnerable neonates: feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, bloody stools, temperature instability and sepsis. X-ray may show pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas or free air. Stop feeds, NG decompression, IV fluids, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics per neonatal protocol, intensive monitoring and urgent neonatal/surgical discussion; surgery for perforation or necrotic bowel.

9 · Paediatric appendicitis

May present atypically, especially under five years; fever, vomiting, abdominal pain and guarding. Ultrasound often first-line imaging in children; repeat clinical assessment and specialist review if uncertainty. Treat with appropriate fluids, analgesia and paediatric surgical assessment. Perforation risk increases with delay.

10 · Incarcerated inguinal hernia

Infant with tender irreducible groin/scrotal/labial swelling, irritability, vomiting or abdominal distension. Prompt paediatric surgical review. Avoid forceful reduction when strangulation suspected. Surgery is urgent for obstruction, strangulation or failed reduction.

11 · Anorectal malformations and oesophageal atresia

Absent/abnormally located anal opening or failure to pass meconium prompts neonatal surgical assessment; avoid blind rectal instrumentation. Oesophageal atresia/tracheo-oesophageal fistula causes drooling, choking and cyanosis with feeds; stop oral feeds, suction upper pouch as directed and urgent neonatal surgery.

12 · Communication and safeguarding

Explain uncertainty and time sensitivity to carers in plain language, obtain age-appropriate assent and parental consent, provide weight-based analgesia and safety-net for bilious vomiting, worsening pain, reduced wet nappies, lethargy, blood in stool and fever. Consider non-accidental injury where presentation warrants, without delaying emergency care.

Clinical algorithms & quick revision

Bilious vomiting in a newborn

ABCDE → NBM + IV fluids/glucose check + NG decompression → immediate paediatric surgery → consider malrotation/volvulus, atresia, Hirschsprung, NEC → imaging if stable, theatre if indicated.

2–8-week infant + non-bilious projectile vomiting

Suspect pyloric stenosis → electrolytes/VBG and ultrasound → correct dehydration and metabolic alkalosis → pyloromyotomy once optimised.

Infant + episodic colicky pain and lethargy

Suspect intussusception → resuscitate and ultrasound → air enema if stable and no peritonitis/perforation → surgery if unstable, complicated or failed reduction.

Delayed meconium + distension

Consider Hirschsprung, atresia, meconium ileus → NBM, NG, surgical review → rectal biopsy for Hirschsprung; urgent antibiotics and washouts if enterocolitis.

Premature neonate + bloody stool + distension

Suspect NEC → stop feeds, NG, IV antibiotics and fluids, abdominal X-ray, neonatal ICU and paediatric surgery; perforation requires urgent operation.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Vomiting after every feed

A 5-week-old boy has progressive projectile non-bilious vomiting, still hungry, fewer wet nappies and low chloride. Diagnosis: pyloric stenosis. Action: IV rehydration and electrolyte correction, ultrasound, then pyloromyotomy.

Case 2 — Episodic screaming and pallor

A 10-month-old draws knees up during brief painful episodes, then appears lethargic; ultrasound shows target sign. Diagnosis: intussusception. Action: surgical/radiology review and image-guided enema if stable without perforation.

Case 3 — Green vomit in a neonate

A 2-day-old develops bilious vomiting, abdominal distension and poor perfusion. Diagnosis to exclude immediately: malrotation with midgut volvulus. Action: resuscitate, NG, NBM and emergency paediatric surgery.

Case 4 — Delayed meconium and fever

A 6-day-old who passed no meconium for 3 days has distension, fever and explosive diarrhoea. Diagnosis: Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis. Action: neonatal sepsis care, IV antibiotics, specialist rectal washouts and urgent surgical consultation.

PLAB 2 OSCE — bilious vomiting in an infant

Station: Parent brings a 3-day-old with green vomiting and reduced feeding. Explain urgent assessment and initial management.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm child details and parent relationship; assess immediately for shock and breathing difficulty.
  2. Ask vomiting colour, timing, stool/meconium, distension, urine output, fever, birth and antenatal history.
  3. Explain: “Green vomit may mean the intestine is blocked or twisted, and blood supply could be at risk.”
  4. Arrange immediate paediatric/surgical review, ABCDE, glucose, IV access, fluids as needed, NBM and NG decompression.
  5. Discuss urgent imaging if stable; explain emergency surgery may be necessary without delay if unstable.
  6. Keep parent informed, provide analgesia, obtain appropriate consent and document escalation.

Exam pitfall: never reassure or discharge a neonate with bilious vomiting simply because the abdomen is initially soft.

Rapid review — high-yield PLAB points

  1. Green/bilious vomiting in a neonate is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise.
  2. Malrotation with volvulus can cause bowel infarction rapidly.
  3. Pyloric stenosis: 2–8 weeks, non-bilious projectile vomiting and hypochloraemic alkalosis.
  4. Correct fluid and electrolyte abnormalities before pyloromyotomy.
  5. Intussusception: episodic pain, pallor/lethargy and ultrasound target sign.
  6. Redcurrant jelly stool is a late and unreliable requirement for diagnosis.
  7. Stable uncomplicated intussusception may be reduced by image-guided enema.
  8. Duodenal atresia: double bubble; association with Down syndrome.
  9. Hirschsprung: delayed meconium; rectal biopsy confirms absent ganglion cells.
  10. Hirschsprung enterocolitis is a potentially fatal emergency.
  11. NEC: preterm infant, distension, blood in stool, pneumatosis intestinalis.
  12. Incarcerated infant hernia requires prompt surgical review.
