Menopause, HRT & Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

Red flag: Postmenopausal bleeding must be assessed; do not assume it is caused by HRT or vaginal atrophy.

Definitions and stages

Menopause is diagnosed retrospectively after 12 months without menstruation when not explained by another cause. Perimenopause is the transition with cycle changes and symptoms; postmenopause follows the final period. Average UK menopause age is about 51.

PLAB application: Distinguish perimenopause from menopause and consider pregnancy if relevant.

Physiology

Declining ovarian follicular activity reduces oestradiol and progesterone; FSH often rises, but fluctuates considerably in perimenopause. Vasomotor and urogenital symptoms relate to changing oestrogen exposure.

PLAB application: Do not use one fluctuating FSH value to diagnose menopause in people aged 45 or older.

Symptoms

Hot flushes, night sweats, sleep disruption, mood changes, vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms, reduced libido and joint aches can occur. Ask about impact on daily life and work.

PLAB application: Explore symptom burden, goals, and red flags rather than assuming all symptoms are menopausal.

Clinical diagnosis at 45 and older

NICE recommends identifying perimenopause clinically in otherwise healthy people aged 45 or older with vasomotor symptoms and menstrual changes, and menopause after 12 months without periods if not using hormonal contraception.

PLAB application: Routine FSH, oestradiol or AMH testing is not needed for diagnosis in this group.

When to investigate

For ages 40–45 with symptoms and menstrual changes, FSH may be considered; under 40, investigate suspected premature ovarian insufficiency. Exclude pregnancy where possible, and consider thyroid disease, anaemia or other causes according to symptoms.

PLAB application: Hormonal contraception can obscure bleeding patterns and influence interpretation.

Premature ovarian insufficiency

POI is loss of ovarian function before age 40, with menstrual disturbance and menopausal symptoms. NICE uses elevated FSH on two samples taken 4–6 weeks apart for diagnosis, alongside clinical history; follow current local specialist guidance.

PLAB application: Refer for specialist assessment and discuss fertility, bone and cardiovascular health.

POI causes and evaluation

Causes include idiopathic, genetic, autoimmune, ovarian surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Assessment may include pregnancy exclusion, endocrine tests and cause-directed investigations with specialist input.

PLAB application: Avoid implying POI means pregnancy is impossible: intermittent ovarian activity may occur.

HRT benefits and shared decisions

Systemic HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and can improve sleep and quality of life; it also helps prevent bone loss while taken. Discuss individual benefits, risks, preferences and alternatives.

PLAB application: HRT is not contraception and treatment decisions are individual.

HRT regimen and uterus

If the uterus is present, systemic oestrogen needs adequate progestogen to protect the endometrium. After hysterectomy, oestrogen-only HRT is usually appropriate. Sequential regimens are often used in perimenopause; continuous combined regimens after menopause.

PLAB application: Unopposed systemic oestrogen with an intact uterus increases endometrial cancer risk.

Routes and risk

Oral and transdermal oestrogen are available. Transdermal oestrogen is generally preferred where VTE risk is increased because oral HRT increases VTE risk; individual assessment still matters.

PLAB application: Ask about previous VTE, stroke, breast cancer, liver disease and unexplained bleeding.

Breast cancer and HRT

Combined systemic HRT is associated with an increased breast cancer risk that depends on type and duration; risk differs for oestrogen-only HRT. Explain absolute risk using current NICE decision aids and individual factors.

PLAB application: Do not describe HRT as universally safe or universally contraindicated.

Vaginal symptoms and local treatment

Vaginal oestrogen can improve genitourinary symptoms, often with minimal systemic absorption. Moisturisers and lubricants can help. NICE supports vaginal oestrogen for genitourinary symptoms, including in many people also taking systemic HRT.

PLAB application: Persistent postmenopausal bleeding needs assessment even if vaginal dryness is present.

Non-hormonal options

Menopause-specific CBT can help vasomotor symptoms, sleep and mood. Lifestyle measures may improve comfort. Some prescription non-hormonal options exist, but suitability and availability vary; check current UK guidance and BNF.

PLAB application: Discuss patient preference and interactions; do not recommend unregulated supplements as proven therapy.

Contraception and menopause

Pregnancy remains possible during perimenopause. HRT is not contraception. Contraception cessation depends on age, method and clinical context; FSH-guided decisions apply only to selected users of progestogen-only contraception aged over 50.

PLAB application: Give method-specific advice or seek sexual-health guidance.

Long-term health

Low oestrogen contributes to bone loss. Discuss weight-bearing exercise, adequate calcium/vitamin D, smoking cessation and cardiovascular risk factors. In POI, hormone replacement is generally recommended until around the usual age of menopause unless contraindicated.

PLAB application: Assess fracture risk when indicated and arrange appropriate follow-up.

Red flags and follow-up

Postmenopausal bleeding, new breast lump, unexplained weight loss or severe mood symptoms require assessment. Review HRT after about 3 months and then at least annually, checking symptom control, adverse effects and bleeding.

PLAB application: Urgent evaluation of postmenopausal bleeding is important to exclude endometrial pathology.

PLAB 2 — Menopause consultation

Open with an empathetic, non-assumptive question about the patient’s main concerns. Ask about hot flushes, sleep, mood, vaginal and urinary symptoms, menstrual changes, contraception, past medical history, breast cancer and VTE risk. Explain diagnosis, benefits and risks of treatment, the importance of progestogen if the uterus is present, and follow-up. Check understanding and safety-net for unexpected bleeding or thromboembolic symptoms.

Deep Dive

Clinical diagnosis versus FSH

In otherwise healthy people aged 45 or older, use symptoms and cycle changes rather than routine laboratory testing. Consider FSH at ages 40–45 or for suspected POI under 40; contraceptive hormones complicate interpretation.

HRT and endometrial protection

Systemic oestrogen stimulates the endometrium. An intact uterus therefore requires adequate progestogen; regimens depend on menopausal stage and patient preference. A levonorgestrel intrauterine system may provide endometrial protection when appropriately licensed and in date.

Route-specific thrombosis risk

Oral systemic oestrogen is associated with increased VTE risk, while transdermal routes are often preferable in people with elevated VTE risk. Previous VTE or other high-risk conditions require specialist individualised decisions.

Breast cancer risk discussion

Use current NICE decision aids to explain absolute risks. Combined HRT increases breast cancer risk with duration of use; risk profiles differ for oestrogen-only HRT. Avoid presenting a single number as universally applicable.

POI and future fertility

POI can be associated with intermittent ovarian activity. Offer specialist fertility discussion, mental health support and assessment of bone and cardiovascular health; consider hormone replacement until usual menopause age if appropriate.

Unexpected bleeding and follow-up

Unexpected bleeding with HRT may need evaluation depending on timing and risk factors. Postmenopausal bleeding warrants assessment, and heavy or persistent bleeding requires prompt review. Review HRT at 3 months and annually.
