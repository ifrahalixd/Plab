Shock: Hypovolaemic, Septic, Cardiogenic, Obstructive & Anaphylactic Shock

Emergency principle: Shock may exist without hypotension. Start ABCDE, summon help, identify reversible causes and reassess repeatedly.

Shock: recognition and physiology

Shock is failure of tissue oxygen delivery or utilisation. Look for tachycardia, altered mental state, cool or mottled skin, delayed capillary refill, oliguria and elevated lactate. Hypotension may be late; normal blood pressure does not rule out shock.

PLAB application: Begin ABCDE, call senior/resuscitation support, obtain IV or intraosseous access and reassess after each intervention.

The five shock categories

Hypovolaemic shock results from lost circulating volume; distributive shock includes sepsis and anaphylaxis; cardiogenic shock is pump failure; obstructive shock is impaired cardiac filling or outflow. Mixed mechanisms are common.

PLAB application: Identify the cause while treating immediate threats: fluids are not equally safe in every category.

First five minutes: ABCDE

Assess airway patency, respiratory effort and oxygenation, pulses and perfusion, disability including capillary glucose, and exposure for bleeding, infection, rash or injury. Attach monitoring, obtain ECG, measure temperature and consider blood gas/lactate.

PLAB application: Call for help early, give oxygen when indicated and secure access without delaying life-saving treatment.

Hypovolaemic shock: haemorrhage

Trauma, gastrointestinal bleeding, ruptured ectopic pregnancy and postpartum haemorrhage can cause life-threatening blood loss. External bleeding may be absent; assess abdomen, pelvis and potential concealed sites.

PLAB application: Control bleeding immediately, activate major haemorrhage protocol when appropriate, and seek definitive haemostasis.

Hypovolaemic shock: non-haemorrhagic

Vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, third spacing and diuresis can deplete intravascular volume. Signs include dry mucosa, reduced urine output and orthostatic symptoms, though examination is imperfect.

PLAB application: Give appropriate isotonic crystalloid with frequent reassessment; correct ongoing losses and the underlying cause.

Septic shock: early recognition

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction due to infection. Suspect shock with infection plus poor perfusion, altered mentation, low blood pressure or raised lactate. Fever may be absent, especially in older or immunocompromised people.

PLAB application: Obtain cultures before antibiotics if this does not delay treatment; escalate immediately and follow current NICE/local sepsis pathway.

Septic shock: treatment principles

Priorities include timely broad-spectrum IV antibiotics when high risk, source control, measured fluid resuscitation and frequent reassessment. Persistent hypotension despite appropriate fluids may require vasopressors in critical care.

PLAB application: Do not give repeated unassessed fluid boluses, especially in patients with heart failure or renal impairment.

Cardiogenic shock: recognition

Acute myocardial infarction, severe arrhythmia, myocarditis or mechanical complications can impair cardiac output. Look for chest pain, cool peripheries, raised jugular venous pressure, crackles and pulmonary oedema; presentations vary.

PLAB application: Obtain ECG and urgent senior/cardiology input; large fluid boluses may worsen pulmonary oedema.

Cardiogenic shock: management

Treat the cause urgently, including reperfusion for acute coronary occlusion or rhythm management when indicated. Use focused echocardiography and bedside imaging where available; inotropes/vasopressors require specialist oversight.

PLAB application: Do not assume every hypotensive patient needs large volumes of fluid.

Obstructive shock: tension pneumothorax

A tension pneumothorax can cause severe respiratory distress, unilateral reduced breath sounds and cardiovascular collapse. Tracheal deviation is a late or unreliable sign.

PLAB application: In an unstable patient, decompress immediately using trained emergency technique; do not wait for a chest X-ray.

Obstructive shock: tamponade and pulmonary embolism

Cardiac tamponade impairs ventricular filling and may present with hypotension, tachycardia and raised JVP; Beck triad is insensitive. Massive pulmonary embolism can cause sudden dyspnoea, syncope, hypoxia and shock.

PLAB application: Urgent echocardiography and specialist intervention; treat suspected high-risk PE or tamponade using emergency pathways.

Anaphylactic shock: recognition

Anaphylaxis is a rapidly evolving systemic hypersensitivity reaction with airway, breathing or circulation problems, usually with skin/mucosal findings but sometimes without a rash. Triggers include food, drugs and insect venom.

PLAB application: Give intramuscular adrenaline immediately into the anterolateral thigh; do not wait for skin changes.

Anaphylaxis: immediate treatment

Stop exposure if possible, call for help, position appropriately, give IM adrenaline according to Resuscitation Council UK age-specific guidance and repeat after 5 minutes if ABC problems persist. Provide oxygen, IV fluids when indicated and monitor.

PLAB application: Antihistamines and steroids do not replace adrenaline for life-threatening ABC problems.

Investigations without delaying resuscitation

Use ECG, bedside glucose, blood gas/lactate, full blood count, electrolytes, renal function, cultures if infection suspected, and targeted imaging. Point-of-care ultrasound may identify cardiac dysfunction, free fluid or tamponade.

PLAB application: Treat the patient, not the lactate alone; trends and clinical perfusion matter.

Fluid challenges and reassessment

The right fluid strategy depends on the shock mechanism and comorbidity. After any fluid challenge, review heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, crackles, mental status, urine output and perfusion. Escalate for persistent shock.

PLAB application: Use local adult/paediatric protocols; this lesson does not provide a universal bolus volume.

PLAB 2 communication, handover and safety

State concerns plainly, explain urgent treatments, check allergies and medicines, obtain relevant history from witnesses, and document response. Use SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation.

PLAB application: Say: “I am concerned this patient is in shock; I need the resuscitation team now.”

PLAB 2 — 60-second shock station opening

“I am concerned this patient is critically unwell. I would call for senior and resuscitation help, assess airway, breathing and circulation, attach monitoring, establish access, check glucose and treat immediate threats. I will reassess continuously and hand over using SBAR.”

Deep Dive — Shock clinical reasoning

Distributive versus low-output shock

Distributive shock typically involves inappropriate vasodilation and relative hypovolaemia; cardiogenic and obstructive shock often reduce forward cardiac flow. Clinical patterns overlap and can evolve.

Lactate and perfusion

Raised lactate can reflect tissue hypoperfusion but also other processes. A normal lactate does not exclude serious shock. Serial assessment of consciousness, skin, urine output and vital signs is essential.

Sepsis treatment priorities

Follow current NICE sepsis risk stratification and local antimicrobial policy. Take blood cultures if feasible without treatment delay, give indicated IV antibiotics promptly, seek source control and reassess fluid responsiveness.

When fluids may harm

Pulmonary oedema, cardiogenic shock and some obstructive states may worsen with indiscriminate fluids. A focused examination, ECG, bedside echo and response to a small supervised challenge can guide treatment.

Anaphylaxis pitfalls

Skin signs may be absent. IM adrenaline is first-line for airway, breathing or circulatory involvement. Keep the patient appropriately positioned; sudden standing may precipitate collapse. Observe for recurrence according to local guidance.

Critical handover

Use SBAR with shock type suspected, observations, treatments already delivered, response, allergies, pending tests and explicit request for critical care or definitive intervention.
