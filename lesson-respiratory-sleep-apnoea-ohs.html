OSAHS and obesity hypoventilation: diagnosis, treatment and emergencies

Safety: Severe drowsiness, hypoxaemia or suspected acute hypercapnic respiratory failure requires urgent assessment. Do not drive with uncontrolled excessive sleepiness.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

Overview

Obstructive sleep apnoea/hypopnoea syndrome (OSAHS) causes repeated partial or complete upper airway obstruction during sleep, with intermittent hypoxaemia and sleep fragmentation.

Clinical application: Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS) involves obesity, awake hypercapnia and sleep-disordered breathing after other causes of hypoventilation are excluded.

Risk factors

Obesity, large neck circumference, male sex, increasing age, craniofacial anatomy and alcohol or sedative exposure can increase OSAHS risk.

Clinical application: OSAHS can affect people without obesity; avoid excluding it based on appearance.

Symptoms

Loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas, gasping or choking, unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness, morning headaches and impaired concentration are typical.

Clinical application: Ask about driving, near misses, work safety and effects on relationships.

Consequences

Untreated OSAHS is associated with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, impaired quality of life and road traffic risk from sleepiness.

Clinical application: Evaluate comorbidities without claiming OSAHS is the cause of every cardiovascular condition.

Screening and history

Epworth Sleepiness Scale assesses subjective daytime sleepiness; STOP-Bang is a screening tool rather than a diagnostic test.

Clinical application: Take a sleep history from the patient and bed partner where possible, and ask about sedatives and shift work.

Diagnostic testing

NICE NG202 recommends sleep studies such as home respiratory polygraphy for suspected OSAHS; other tests may be needed if results are inconclusive.

Clinical application: The apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) helps grade OSAHS but treatment decisions also depend on symptoms and clinical context.

AHI severity

Common AHI bands are mild 5–14.9, moderate 15–29.9 and severe at least 30 events per hour.

Clinical application: Do not rely on AHI alone to assess sleepiness, hypoxaemia or driving risk.

Lifestyle measures

Weight management, regular activity, reducing alcohol, avoiding sedatives when possible and smoking cessation can help selected patients.

Clinical application: Positional therapy may be useful in position-dependent OSAHS under specialist advice.

CPAP treatment

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) splints the upper airway open during sleep and is commonly offered for moderate or severe symptomatic OSAHS.

Clinical application: Assess mask fit, adherence, nasal symptoms, dryness and patient preferences during follow-up.

Mandibular advancement devices

A custom mandibular advancement splint can be considered for selected patients, including those unable to tolerate CPAP.

Clinical application: Dental suitability and follow-up matter; device choice should follow NICE and specialist assessment.

Obesity hypoventilation syndrome

OHS is generally defined by BMI at least 30 kg/m² and awake arterial PaCO2 above 6.0 kPa (45 mmHg), with no other explanation for hypoventilation.

Clinical application: Evaluate oxygenation, blood gases and other causes of chronic hypercapnia; OHS is not simply severe OSAHS.

OHS treatment

Weight management and positive airway pressure are central. CPAP may be used when OHS coexists with severe OSAHS; non-invasive ventilation (NIV) is used for selected patients.

Clinical application: Specialist teams choose CPAP versus NIV based on phenotype, gas exchange and response.

Oxygen cautions

Supplemental oxygen alone does not treat upper airway obstruction or hypoventilation and can worsen CO2 retention in susceptible patients.

Clinical application: Use oxygen only when clinically indicated, with monitoring and specialist guidance.

Acute decompensation

OHS may present with acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure, drowsiness, hypoxaemia or respiratory acidosis.

Clinical application: Assess ABCDE, blood gases, precipitating illness and need for monitored NIV or critical-care input.

Driving and safety

Excessive sleepiness can make driving unsafe. UK DVLA guidance depends on symptom control and treatment, and some drivers must notify DVLA.

Clinical application: Advise patients with uncontrolled excessive sleepiness not to drive and to check current DVLA rules.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that breathing repeatedly narrows or stops during sleep, fragmenting sleep and causing daytime symptoms.

Clinical application: Discuss sleep testing, CPAP demonstration, weight management, follow-up, and driving advice without stigma.

PLAB 2: Sleep apnoea counselling OSCE

  1. Ask about snoring, witnessed apnoeas, fatigue, sleepiness and driving.
  2. Explain the purpose of a home sleep study and the difference between OSAHS and OHS.
  3. Discuss weight management, CPAP or alternatives and adherence.
  4. Explain follow-up, driving restrictions and when to seek urgent help.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why the airway collapses

During sleep, reduced upper-airway dilator muscle tone can allow a susceptible pharynx to narrow or collapse. Repeated arousals fragment sleep.

Why sleepiness matters

Sleep fragmentation can impair attention, memory, mood and driving performance even if the patient does not remember waking.

AHI limitations

AHI counts apnoeas and hypopnoeas per hour, but does not fully capture event duration, oxygen burden or functional impact.

OSAHS versus OHS

OSAHS is obstructive events during sleep; OHS requires obesity and awake hypercapnia after other causes are excluded. They often coexist.

CPAP versus NIV

CPAP maintains upper airway patency; NIV provides inspiratory pressure support to assist ventilation when hypoventilation persists or acute respiratory failure occurs.

Oxygen and carbon dioxide

Supplemental oxygen may improve saturation without correcting alveolar hypoventilation; assess blood gases when CO2 retention is suspected.
