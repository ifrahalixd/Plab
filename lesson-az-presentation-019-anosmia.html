Anosmia: an organised approach to smell loss

Anosmia is a complete loss of smell; hyposmia is reduced smell. Parosmia means a familiar odour smells distorted, while phantosmia is perceiving a smell without a source. These symptoms affect nutrition, quality of life and everyday safety. The two broad mechanisms are failure of odorants to reach olfactory epithelium (conductive) and injury to sensory neurons, the olfactory bulb or central pathways (sensorineural/central).

1. Why smell disappears

Odour molecules normally reach the olfactory cleft at the roof of the nasal cavity. Olfactory receptor neurons transmit signals through the cribriform plate to the olfactory bulb and onward to limbic and orbitofrontal networks. Viral illnesses can cause epithelial inflammation and neuronal dysfunction; allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps can obstruct access to the cleft; head trauma may shear olfactory fila near the cribriform plate.

Conductive lossNasal obstruction, allergic rhinitis, chronic rhinosinusitis, nasal polyps or structural blockage. Often congestion or fluctuating symptoms coexist.
Sensorineural / central lossPost-infectious dysfunction, head injury, medications, neurodegeneration and rare intracranial pathology. Nasal examination can be unremarkable.

2. History: seven clinical questions

  1. Onset and timeline: sudden after a cold/COVID-19, gradual over months, fluctuating, or following trauma? Ask about recovery and parosmia.
  2. Laterality: unilateral symptoms warrant closer attention to an obstruction, structural lesion or neurological pathology.
  3. Nasal symptoms: congestion, discharge, facial pressure, sneezing, seasonal itch, epistaxis or unilateral blockage.
  4. Neurology: headache, seizures, focal deficits, cognition, gait changes, tremor or visual symptoms.
  5. Exposures: medicines, intranasal products, smoking, occupational chemicals, prior surgery or radiotherapy.
  6. Distinguish taste: can the patient still detect sweet, sour, salty and bitter? Reduced flavour is often due to impaired retronasal olfaction, not loss of basic gustation.
  7. Impact and safety: appetite, weight, mood, gas alarms, smoke detectors and ability to notice spoiled food.

3. Examination

Assess observations and neurological status when indicated. Inspect nose for inflammation, discharge, obvious polyps or unilateral lesions. Examine oral cavity and cranial nerves, including facial sensation, vision and ocular movements if red flags arise. A normal anterior nasal exam does not exclude pathology higher in the olfactory cleft; ENT may perform nasal endoscopy.

PLAB bedside distinction: Patient reports food has 'no taste' but recognises sweet and salty solutions: think smell impairment affecting flavour. True isolated taste loss is less common and needs a separate history and medication review.

5. Management and follow-up

  1. Treat reversible nasal disease: saline care and appropriately selected intranasal corticosteroids for inflammatory rhinitis or chronic rhinosinusitis; manage polyps/structural disease with ENT input. Steroid treatment is not universally indicated for isolated post-viral anosmia.
  2. Olfactory training: repeatedly smell several distinct, familiar odours (commonly rose, lemon, clove, eucalyptus) twice daily over several months. Benefits vary; this is low-risk and has the best supportive evidence for post-infectious dysfunction.
  3. Explain prognosis: many people improve gradually; some have persistent loss or parosmia. Do not guarantee recovery or a fixed timeframe.
  4. Safety: working smoke and gas detectors, caution around cooking and spoiled food, and nutritional monitoring.
  5. Refer: act urgently on red flags and follow current local ENT pathway for persistent unexplained symptoms or failure of initial management.
Do not overinvestigate or overtreat. NICE NG127 advises against routine referral for smell or taste loss immediately after a head injury in isolation; the patient still requires assessment for any head-injury emergency features. UK ENT referral guidance distinguishes uncomplicated post-viral loss from red-flag presentations.

6. Five worked clinical cases

Case A — Post-viral anosmia

A 29-year-old lost smell abruptly with a respiratory illness six weeks ago; nasal examination is normal and there are no red flags. Likely: post-infectious olfactory dysfunction. Explain recovery uncertainty, offer smell training, safety advice and review based on local pathway.

Case B — Chronic nasal blockage

A 46-year-old has bilateral congestion, reduced smell and a history of asthma. Nasal examination suggests polyps. Likely: chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps. Assess severity, treat inflammation appropriately and consider ENT referral.

Case C — One-sided warning signs

A 61-year-old develops progressive unilateral obstruction, intermittent epistaxis and reduced smell. Do not label post-viral: requires prompt ENT assessment for possible serious local disease.

Case D — After head trauma

A 33-year-old reports isolated loss of smell after a minor fall, with no other concerning features on trauma assessment. Approach: complete head injury safety assessment, then counsel and follow up; isolated smell loss does not automatically require neurological referral.

Case E — Neurological presentation

A 71-year-old has gradual smell loss with progressive bradykinesia, asymmetric rigidity and resting tremor. Consider: parkinsonism rather than isolated ENT disease, and arrange appropriate specialist neurological evaluation.

7. PLAB 2 counselling script

“Your sense of smell can change after infections or nasal inflammation. I want to check for any warning symptoms first. We can look for treatable nasal causes and discuss smell training. I cannot promise when it will recover, but we can make a follow-up plan. Until then, please check your smoke and gas alarms and take extra care with food freshness.”

Deep Dive · neuroanatomy / regeneration / sensory science

Why olfaction differs from other senses

1. The sensory pathway

Olfactory receptor neurons in the superior nasal cavity extend cilia into mucus. Each expresses an odorant receptor family; signals pass through the cribriform plate (CN I), synapse in olfactory bulb glomeruli and reach primary olfactory cortex, amygdala and entorhinal structures. Orbitofrontal integration contributes to identifying flavour.

2. Conductive versus sensorineural mechanisms

Airflow obstruction prevents odour molecules reaching olfactory receptors, while viral injury can alter the epithelium, supporting cells and neuronal signalling even without a blocked nose. A normal nasal examination cannot confirm intact olfactory neurons.

3. Parosmia during recovery

Odour distortion may arise as olfactory networks recover or reorganise. Patients describe coffee or perfume as foul or burnt. Explain the symptom non-judgementally and assess its effects on eating and mood.

4. Why trauma can impair smell

Sudden acceleration/deceleration may damage olfactory nerve fibres at the cribriform plate, the bulb or central pathways. Assessment depends on the entire head-injury presentation, not anosmia alone.

5. Why taste seems absent

Much of perceived food flavour results from retronasal olfaction. Basic gustation (sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami) is supplied by CN VII, IX and X. A patient may still identify salt or sugar while reporting that food is tasteless.

6. Smell training rationale

Repeated focused exposure to distinct odours may help rehabilitation through sensory learning and neural plasticity. It is not guaranteed to restore smell, and medical investigation should not be delayed when red flags exist.

7. PLAB traps

  • Do not confuse isolated smell loss with acute stroke without associated neurological signs.
  • Do not attribute persistent unilateral bleeding or blockage to uncomplicated viral infection.
  • Do not order routine brain MRI for every case of typical post-viral anosmia.
  • Do not claim corticosteroids are routinely effective for isolated post-viral smell loss.
  • Remember safety planning and the psychological impact of lost smell.
