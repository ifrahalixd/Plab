Nephrotic Syndrome & Nephritic Syndrome

PLAB focus: Distinguish protein-leak nephrotic syndrome from inflammatory nephritic syndrome; identify rapidly progressive GN and pulmonary–renal emergencies.

01. The two glomerular syndromes

Nephrotic syndrome is predominantly increased glomerular protein permeability; nephritic syndrome is predominantly glomerular inflammation with haematuria and impaired filtration. They can overlap.

PLAB application: Recognise the pattern before naming a disease.

02. Nephrotic syndrome: clinical picture

Heavy proteinuria, low serum albumin and oedema are characteristic. Frothy urine, periorbital or dependent swelling, weight gain and hyperlipidaemia may occur.

PLAB application: Look for oedema and quantify urine protein.

03. Nephritic syndrome: clinical picture

Glomerular haematuria (tea- or cola-coloured urine), hypertension, reduced urine output, rising creatinine and variable proteinuria. Red-cell casts support a glomerular source.

PLAB application: Haematuria plus AKI or hypertension warrants urgent evaluation.

04. History and examination

Ask about onset of oedema or dark urine, infection, sore throat, rash, joint pain, hearing changes, medications including NSAIDs, diabetes, systemic illness and family history. Check BP, volume status, rash and lung signs.

PLAB application: Pulmonary haemorrhage plus renal injury suggests pulmonary–renal syndrome.

05. First-line investigations

Urine dipstick for blood and protein; urine ACR or PCR; urine microscopy; U&Es, creatinine/eGFR, albumin, FBC and lipids. Measure BP and fluid balance. Exclude infection and pregnancy when relevant.

PLAB application: Dipstick protein alone does not quantify protein loss.

06. Quantifying proteinuria

Nephrotic-range proteinuria is classically >3.5 g/day; spot urine PCR about ≥300–350 mg/mmol is commonly used as an approximate equivalent. Interpret using local laboratory units and nephrology advice.

PLAB application: Severe albuminuria does not alone prove a specific histological diagnosis.

07. Minimal change disease

Often causes nephrotic syndrome, especially in children; can also occur in adults. Light microscopy may appear normal while electron microscopy shows podocyte foot-process effacement.

PLAB application: Adults usually need specialist assessment and often biopsy.

08. Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis

FSGS may be primary or secondary (for example obesity, reduced nephron mass, HIV or genetic disease). Often presents with proteinuria, sometimes nephrotic syndrome.

PLAB application: Investigate secondary causes rather than assuming primary disease.

09. Membranous nephropathy

An important adult cause of nephrotic syndrome. Anti-PLA2R antibodies can support diagnosis in appropriate patients; evaluate for associated conditions and malignancy according to specialist assessment.

PLAB application: Diagnosis and immunosuppression decisions are nephrology-led.

10. Post-infectious and IgA nephropathy

Post-streptococcal GN typically follows infection after a latent interval and may show low complement. IgA nephropathy often produces visible haematuria during or shortly after an upper respiratory infection.

PLAB application: Timing of haematuria relative to infection is a useful clue, not proof.

11. Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis

Rapid deterioration in renal function over days to weeks with active urinary sediment suggests crescentic GN. Causes include ANCA vasculitis, anti-GBM disease and immune-complex GN.

PLAB application: Urgent same-day nephrology discussion: kidney function can be lost quickly.

12. Immune and serological work-up

Depending on presentation: C3/C4, ANA, anti-dsDNA, ANCA, anti-GBM, hepatitis B/C, HIV, immunoglobulins and serum protein studies. Renal ultrasound evaluates size and obstruction.

PLAB application: Choose tests based on syndrome and specialist advice.

13. Renal biopsy

Biopsy can establish the histological cause and guide treatment in many adult glomerular presentations. Assess bleeding risk, BP, anticoagulants and contraindications. Not every patient requires biopsy.

PLAB application: Do not delay urgent referral while arranging investigations.

14. Nephrotic complications

Patients are at risk of venous thrombosis, infection, dyslipidaemia, AKI and oedema-related problems. Assess VTE risk and discuss thromboprophylaxis individually with specialists.

PLAB application: Sudden pleuritic pain or breathlessness may indicate pulmonary embolism.

15. Management principles

Treat underlying disease with nephrology guidance. Control BP and proteinuria where appropriate (often ACE inhibitor or ARB with monitoring), use salt restriction and carefully monitored diuretics for oedema. Immunosuppression is disease-specific.

PLAB application: Avoid routine empirical steroids in undifferentiated adult nephrotic syndrome.

16. Emergencies and safety-netting

Escalate urgently for rapidly rising creatinine, oliguria, severe hypertension, pulmonary oedema, hyperkalaemia, haemoptysis, severe infection or suspected thrombosis. Monitor fluid balance and medicines.

PLAB application: The combination of haemoptysis and AKI is a time-critical red flag.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

The filtration barrier

Fenestrated endothelium, glomerular basement membrane and podocyte slit diaphragms restrict protein loss. Podocyte injury causes albumin leakage.

Inflammatory glomerular injury

Immune-complex or antibody-mediated inflammation damages capillary walls, causing red cells and casts in urine and reduced GFR.

Complement clues

Low C3/C4 can support selected immune-complex diseases; normal complement does not exclude important glomerulonephritis.

Thrombosis in nephrotic syndrome

Urinary loss of anticoagulant proteins, hepatic coagulation changes and immobility contribute to hypercoagulability.

Biopsy patterns

Minimal change: foot-process effacement; FSGS: focal segmental scars; membranous: subepithelial immune deposits; crescentic GN: extracapillary crescents.

PLAB 2 communication

Explain that protein or blood in urine can reflect kidney-filter inflammation or leakage; discuss urgent red flags, tests, possible biopsy and follow-up without assuming a diagnosis.
