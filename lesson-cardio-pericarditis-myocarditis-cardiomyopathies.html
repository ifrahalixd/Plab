Lesson 06 · Pericarditis, Myocarditis & Cardiomyopathies

Learning objectives

Inflammation, effusions and myocardial disease

Recognise dangerous chest-pain presentations; distinguish pericarditis from myocarditis; identify tamponade; classify cardiomyopathies; choose safe investigations, referral and initial management.

Emergency rule: Chest pain with instability, syncope, shock, malignant arrhythmia or acute heart failure requires urgent emergency assessment. Do not use this educational page as a clinical protocol.

Browser text-to-speech, if supported.

Acute pericarditis

Inflammation of the pericardium causes sharp pleuritic chest pain, often worse lying flat and relieved by sitting forward. A pericardial friction rub may be heard. ECG may show widespread concave ST elevation and PR depression, although these findings are not universal.

Assessment and management: Assess vitals, ECG, troponin, inflammatory markers and echocardiography where indicated. Exclude ACS, pulmonary embolism and aortic dissection. Typical first-line treatment is an NSAID plus colchicine, with gastroprotection and attention to contraindications; restrict strenuous exercise until recovery.

Pericardial effusion and tamponade

Fluid in the pericardial sac can impair ventricular filling. Tamponade is a life-threatening obstructive shock: tachycardia, hypotension, raised JVP, pulsus paradoxus and sometimes muffled heart sounds. Beck's triad is not reliably present.

Assessment and management: Urgent senior/critical-care involvement and bedside echocardiography. Haemodynamically unstable tamponade requires urgent drainage by an experienced team. Avoid delaying definitive care for a complete set of textbook signs.

Constrictive pericarditis

A rigid pericardium restricts diastolic filling, leading to peripheral oedema, ascites, raised JVP and exertional intolerance. Kussmaul sign may occur.

Assessment and management: Investigate with echo and specialist cross-sectional imaging or haemodynamic assessment. Treat the underlying cause and refer to cardiology; selected chronic cases may require pericardiectomy.

Myocarditis

Inflammation of the myocardium may follow a viral illness and present with chest pain, palpitations, breathlessness, arrhythmia, syncope or new heart failure. ECG and troponin can be abnormal but neither alone confirms the diagnosis.

Assessment and management: Check ECG, troponin, inflammatory markers, echocardiography and consider cardiac MRI via cardiology. Assess for ACS and other emergencies. Admit or urgently refer patients with instability, ventricular arrhythmia, syncope or impaired LV function. Avoid strenuous exercise and obtain specialist advice on return to sport.

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM)

Ventricular dilation with systolic dysfunction can cause fatigue, orthopnoea, oedema and arrhythmia. Causes include genetic disease, alcohol, cardiotoxic drugs, tachycardia-mediated disease and prior myocarditis.

Assessment and management: Echo assesses LV size and ejection fraction; investigate reversible causes and family history. Treat HFrEF according to current UK guidance, and consider device therapy and genetic/family assessment where appropriate.

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)

Unexplained myocardial hypertrophy may cause exertional dyspnoea, chest pain, syncope and sudden cardiac death. Dynamic LV outflow obstruction may produce an ejection systolic murmur that becomes louder with Valsalva or standing.

Assessment and management: ECG and echo are initial tests; cardiac MRI and specialist risk assessment may follow. Refer to inherited cardiac conditions services for family screening. Avoid assuming every patient has obstruction; treatment depends on phenotype and symptoms.

Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy

Often inherited, with fibrofatty replacement and ventricular arrhythmias; classically involves the right ventricle but may affect either ventricle. Presentations include palpitations, syncope or sudden cardiac arrest.

Assessment and management: Specialist evaluation includes ECG, ambulatory monitoring, imaging and genetic/family assessment. Exercise advice and ICD decisions require specialist risk stratification.

Restrictive cardiomyopathy

Stiff ventricles cause impaired filling and congestion, sometimes with preserved EF. Causes include amyloidosis, iron overload and infiltrative disease.

Assessment and management: Investigate the underlying cause with echo, cardiac MRI and targeted tests. Management is cause-specific; careful diuresis and specialist input are important.

Takotsubo syndrome

Acute stress-related ventricular dysfunction can closely mimic ACS with chest pain, ECG changes and raised troponin.

Assessment and management: Treat as possible ACS until assessed appropriately. Coronary assessment and cardiac imaging help distinguish it; monitor for heart failure, arrhythmia and shock.

PLAB 2: focused OSCE approach

  1. Assess ABCDE and observations; call for help for unstable patients.
  2. Clarify chest pain, recent infection, exertional symptoms, syncope, family sudden death and drug/alcohol exposure.
  3. Examine pulse, BP, JVP, heart sounds, lung bases and peripheral oedema.
  4. Explain ECG, blood tests and echocardiography, and why ACS/PE may need exclusion.
  5. Communicate red flags, admission or follow-up, exercise advice and family screening when relevant.

Mechanisms and higher-order reasoning

Deep Dive

Why pericarditis causes positional pain

Inflamed parietal pericardial surfaces are pain-sensitive. Movement and respiratory excursion can worsen discomfort; sitting forward often reduces contact and symptoms.

Why tamponade can cause shock

Rising intrapericardial pressure limits diastolic chamber filling, reducing stroke volume. The rate of fluid accumulation matters: a rapid small effusion can be dangerous while a slowly accumulating large effusion may initially be tolerated.

Why troponin cannot diagnose myocarditis alone

Troponin signals myocardial injury, not its cause. ACS, myocarditis, tachyarrhythmia and other processes can elevate it. Interpret with symptoms, ECG, imaging and clinical risk.

Myocarditis vs pericarditis

Pericarditis predominantly affects the pericardium; myocarditis involves the muscle and may impair pumping or electrical stability. Overlap syndromes occur.

Cardiomyopathy phenotypes

Dilated: chamber enlargement with systolic dysfunction. Hypertrophic: increased wall thickness with possible dynamic obstruction. Restrictive: stiff ventricles with impaired filling. Arrhythmogenic: myocardial disease with ventricular arrhythmia risk.

Why HCM obstruction changes with preload

Reduced LV cavity size can intensify systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve and dynamic outflow obstruction, which explains a murmur that may become louder with standing or Valsalva.

Inherited disease and family implications

Some cardiomyopathies are genetic. A three-generation family history, specialist genetic counselling and cascade assessment may be appropriate; not every phenotype is inherited.

Medication caution

Choice of NSAIDs, colchicine, beta blockers, HF medicines or anticoagulants depends on diagnosis, renal function, haemodynamics, pregnancy, interactions and specialist guidance. Avoid a one-size-fits-all prescription.
