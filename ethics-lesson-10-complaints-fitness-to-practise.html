Complaints, Fitness to Practise & GMC Investigations

Learning objectives: Respond constructively to patient complaints; distinguish local complaint processes from GMC fitness-to-practise procedures; recognise when conduct, performance or health may put patients at risk; communicate openly and preserve accurate records.

1. When a patient complains

Listen without defensiveness. Acknowledge the concern and the impact on the patient, check for immediate clinical harm, offer a sincere apology when appropriate, explain the complaints route and arrange follow-up. An apology is not, by itself, an admission of legal liability. Do not promise an outcome you cannot control.

2. Professional duty of candour

When something has gone wrong with a patient's care and caused, or has the potential to cause, harm or distress, be open and honest, explain what is known, apologise, offer appropriate support and record the discussion. Do not wait for a formal complaint before addressing harm.

3. Local complaints versus regulatory concerns

Local complaints concern a patient's experience and are usually handled by the provider's complaints process. GMC fitness to practise concerns whether a doctor's ability to practise safely and effectively may be impaired; not every complaint warrants GMC action. Criminal, employment and regulatory processes are distinct and may run in parallel.

4. Fitness to practise: possible concerns

Serious or persistent clinical performance issues; dishonesty; breaches of professional boundaries; criminal conduct; serious misconduct; or health concerns that impair safe practice may warrant assessment. A diagnosis or disability alone is not evidence of impaired fitness to practise.

5. The doctor's responsibilities

Cooperate with appropriate investigations; provide accurate and complete information; preserve contemporaneous notes; reflect and remediate where needed; seek support and professional advice. Do not alter records retrospectively to conceal mistakes, retaliate against complainants or mislead investigators.

6. UK context and limits

Complaint pathways and independent review arrangements differ across England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. In England, the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman can consider eligible unresolved NHS complaints. GMC decisions depend on evidence and the applicable regulatory framework, not a simple 'one complaint = suspension' rule.

PLAB trap: The first priority in a complaint involving an unsafe patient is to assess and manage clinical risk—not merely hand over a complaints leaflet.

HEAR → ACT → RECORD

  1. H — Hear: Listen privately, clarify concerns, remain respectful and non-defensive.
  2. E — Empathise: Acknowledge distress; apologise where appropriate without speculation or blame.
  3. A — Assess: Check immediate safety and whether duty of candour is triggered.
  4. R — Route: Explain local complaints support, relevant escalation and expected next contact.
  5. ACT: Take clinical action, raise safety concerns and arrange review or investigation.
  6. RECORD: Document factual events, conversations, actions and follow-up transparently.

For possible serious impairment: protect patients → seek senior/medical director advice → follow local reporting and appropriate regulatory guidance → cooperate and remediate.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Missed diagnosis complaint

A patient says their cancer was diagnosed late and wants to complain. Best response: assess current clinical needs, listen, apologise for distress, explain what is known without speculation, arrange senior review and provide complaints information. Consider duty of candour where applicable.

Case 2 · Demand to erase notes

A doctor notices an incorrect entry after a complaint. Best response: retain the original audit trail, add a clearly dated and attributed correction in line with record-keeping policy; never secretly overwrite the record.

Case 3 · Repeated unsafe practice

Several patients complain that a colleague repeatedly prescribes dangerous doses. Best response: safeguard patients immediately, escalate evidence through appropriate senior and safety channels; consider regulatory referral according to seriousness and guidance.

Case 4 · Doctor with a health condition

A colleague has well-managed epilepsy with no impact on clinical performance. Best response: do not assume impairment or discriminate; focus on actual patient-safety risk, reasonable support and occupational health advice when appropriate.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Handling a formal complaint

Station: A patient is upset because a hospital discharge summary contained the wrong medication and says, 'I want to report you to the GMC.'

Suggested consultation structure

  1. Introduce yourself; ensure privacy and invite the patient's account without interruption.
  2. Validate the concern and apologise for the error and distress.
  3. Check what medication was actually taken, symptoms and immediate safety; arrange treatment if needed.
  4. Explain what is known and what needs checking; avoid guessing or blaming a colleague.
  5. Explain the hospital complaints route and that the patient may contact the GMC independently; do not discourage a complaint.
  6. Escalate the prescribing error, document objectively, arrange review and safety follow-up.
  7. Summarise, check understanding and provide a clear contact plan.

Rapid revision slides

Slide 1 · Complaints

Listen, acknowledge, apologise where appropriate, assess safety and explain the process.

Slide 2 · Candour

Be open when care has gone wrong and harm or distress has occurred or may occur; explain, apologise, support, record.

Slide 3 · GMC role

The regulator addresses potential impairment of fitness to practise; it does not adjudicate every customer-service complaint.

Slide 4 · Records

Accurate, timely notes; transparent corrections; never conceal or fabricate evidence.

Slide 5 · Remediation

Insight, reflection, supervision, training and demonstrable changes can be relevant to safe practice.

Slide 6 · Examination traps

Do not ignore clinical harm, assume a complaint proves misconduct, or assume illness automatically equals impairment.
