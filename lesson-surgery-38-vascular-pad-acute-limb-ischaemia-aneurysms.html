Vascular Surgery: Peripheral Arterial Disease, Acute Limb Ischaemia & Aneurysms

Surgery · Lesson 38 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Vascular surgery — UK PLAB high-yield lesson

Never miss: acute limb ischaemia, ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), an infected/ischaemic diabetic foot, or threatened limb from critical perfusion loss. Immediate ABCDE, analgesia, IV access, senior/vascular team involvement and time-critical treatment are essential.

1 · Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)

Atherosclerotic narrowing causes exertional calf, thigh or buttock pain relieved by rest (intermittent claudication). Risks: smoking, diabetes, age, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, CKD and established cardiovascular disease. Examine pulses, skin temperature, colour, capillary refill, trophic changes, ulcers and bruits. Exclude neurogenic claudication (relief with flexion/sitting) and venous disease.

2 · Ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI)

ABPI = highest ankle systolic pressure / highest brachial systolic pressure using Doppler. ≤0.90 supports PAD; 0.91–0.99 borderline; 1.00–1.40 usually normal; >1.40 suggests non-compressible calcified vessels (e.g., diabetes/CKD) and may require toe pressures or vascular testing. A normal resting ABPI does not fully exclude exertional PAD; consider exercise testing or referral. Interpret in clinical context.

3 · PAD management

Smoking cessation, supervised exercise programme, cardiovascular risk reduction, blood pressure/diabetes/lipid management and antiplatelet therapy when appropriate. NICE CG147 recommends clopidogrel as first-line antiplatelet for PAD where indicated; use individual bleeding-risk assessment. Offer high-intensity statin in line with cardiovascular prevention guidance. Revascularisation (angioplasty/stent or bypass) for lifestyle-limiting symptoms despite optimal care or threatened limb, based on vascular imaging and specialist assessment.

4 · Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI)

Persistent ischaemic rest pain, non-healing ulceration or gangrene suggests CLTI. Pain often worse with leg elevation and relieved by dependency; examine foot wounds, infection, pulses and neurological status. Urgent vascular referral for limb salvage, assessment of perfusion and revascularisation. Do not use compression for suspected severe arterial disease without vascular assessment. Foot infection with systemic illness also requires urgent sepsis care.

5 · Acute limb ischaemia (ALI)

Sudden perfusion loss: classic 6 Ps — pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (cold limb). Sensory loss and motor weakness indicate a threatened limb; paralysis is a late ominous sign. Causes include embolism (atrial fibrillation), thrombosis on PAD, graft occlusion and trauma. Compare both limbs; document symptom onset, Doppler arterial/venous signals, motor/sensory function and viability.

6 · ALI immediate management

Call vascular surgery immediately, ABCDE, oxygen only if indicated, IV access, analgesia, blood tests (FBC, U&E, coagulation, lactate, group and save), ECG, NBM and IV fluids if appropriate. Start IV unfractionated heparin unless contraindicated under local protocol/senior guidance. Urgent vascular imaging (often CT angiography) if it will not delay limb-saving intervention. Treatment may involve embolectomy, catheter-directed thrombolysis, thrombectomy or bypass. Irreversibly ischaemic limb may require amputation. Monitor for reperfusion injury, compartment syndrome, hyperkalaemia and AKI.

7 · AAA

Abdominal aortic aneurysm is focal aortic dilation, typically ≥3.0 cm. Risk factors include older age, male sex, smoking and family history. Often incidental on ultrasound; may cause abdominal/back pain or pulsatile mass. In the UK, the NHS AAA screening programme offers a one-off ultrasound to eligible men in the year they turn 65. Surveillance and elective repair thresholds depend on size, sex, growth, symptoms and specialist evaluation; NICE NG156 generally recommends considering repair for ≥5.5 cm, rapid growth (>1 cm/year for aneurysms >4 cm) or symptomatic aneurysm.

8 · Ruptured AAA

Sudden severe abdominal/back pain, collapse or shock in an at-risk patient is a vascular emergency, even without a palpable mass. Activate major haemorrhage/vascular pathways, two large-bore IV lines, blood products and urgent transfer to a vascular centre. In suspected rupture, permissive hypotension may be used under specialist protocol while preserving consciousness/perfusion; avoid delaying surgery for unnecessary investigations. Bedside ultrasound can identify AAA but cannot reliably exclude rupture; CT angiography is for stable enough patients when appropriate.

9 · Aortic dissection versus AAA

Dissection often causes abrupt tearing chest/back pain, pulse/BP differential or neurological signs. Suspect ascending dissection with chest pain and haemodynamic compromise; urgent aortic team/CT angiography and controlled heart rate/BP under senior management. A ruptured AAA typically causes abdominal/back pain and haemodynamic instability. Both require emergency specialist care.

10 · Other vascular surgical problems

Carotid stenosis: TIA/stroke symptoms require immediate stroke pathway; selected symptomatic stenoses benefit from expedited carotid endarterectomy. Popliteal aneurysm: may thrombose or embolise and threaten the leg. Acute DVT: swelling/tenderness with venous thrombosis is distinct from ALI; use Wells score and NICE NG158 diagnostic pathway. Diabetic foot: assess perfusion, infection and neuropathy; urgent multidisciplinary diabetic foot/vascular review for gangrene or limb-threatening infection.

Clinical decision pathways

Exertional leg pain

Claudication history → pulse and foot examination → Doppler ABPI → cardiovascular risk treatment, smoking cessation and supervised exercise → vascular assessment if disabling despite treatment.

ABPI interpretation

≤0.90: PAD likely. 0.91–0.99: borderline. 1.00–1.40: generally normal. >1.40: non-compressible vessels; request toe pressures/other testing. Do not ignore strong symptoms with normal ABPI.

Acute cold painful limb

6 Ps → assess motor/sensation and Doppler signals → immediate vascular call, analgesia, IV access, NBM, ECG/bloods → IV heparin if no contraindication → urgent revascularisation decision. Do not delay treatment for routine imaging.

Rest pain / ulcer / gangrene

Suspect CLTI → urgent vascular referral → infection and sepsis assessment → perfusion testing and revascularisation planning → wound care and risk reduction.

Suspected ruptured AAA

Older patient + sudden abdominal/back pain ± shock → major haemorrhage/vascular pathway → resuscitation with blood products and urgent definitive repair; imaging only when appropriate to stability and local pathway.

Post-revascularisation monitoring

Check limb warmth, colour, pulses/Doppler, sensation, motor function, urine output, potassium and CK as indicated; severe pain on passive stretch suggests compartment syndrome.

Four clinical cases with reasoning

Case 1 — Sudden cold leg

A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation develops sudden severe right-leg pain, pallor, absent pulses and reduced toe sensation. Diagnosis: acute limb ischaemia, likely embolic. Next: immediate vascular referral, analgesia, IV access, NBM, IV unfractionated heparin if not contraindicated, urgent revascularisation assessment. Do not wait for routine outpatient ultrasound.

Case 2 — Calf pain after walking

A 61-year-old smoker gets calf pain after 150 m that resolves after resting. ABPI is 0.72. Diagnosis: PAD causing intermittent claudication. Plan: smoking cessation, supervised exercise, antiplatelet/statin and cardiovascular risk control; assess suitability for vascular referral if symptoms remain limiting.

Case 3 — Back pain and collapse

A 76-year-old man collapses with severe abdominal and back pain, BP 75/45 mmHg. Concern: ruptured AAA. Next: call vascular/major haemorrhage teams, urgent blood-product resuscitation and definitive repair pathway. Do not delay transfer for unnecessary tests.

Case 4 — Diabetic foot ulcer with rest pain

A 68-year-old with diabetes has a non-healing toe ulcer, night pain relieved by dangling the leg and absent pedal pulses. Diagnosis: chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, with possible infection. Next: urgent vascular and diabetic foot team assessment, evaluate sepsis/infection and perfusion, plan revascularisation and offloading/wound care.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Suspected acute limb ischaemia

Station: A 72-year-old develops sudden painful, cold left foot.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, assess immediate stability and request urgent help.
  2. Take focused history: exact onset, pain, weakness, numbness, previous claudication, AF, anticoagulants, vascular procedures, trauma, bleeding risk.
  3. Perform ABCDE and compare both legs for 6 Ps; document motor/sensory deficits and Doppler signals.
  4. Explain calmly: “I am worried the blood supply to your leg has suddenly become blocked. This needs emergency treatment to prevent permanent damage.”
  5. Escalate immediately to vascular surgery; arrange IV access, blood tests, ECG, analgesia and NBM.
  6. Discuss IV heparin with senior clinician and check contraindications; prepare for urgent imaging or intervention without delay.
  7. Explain possible embolectomy, thrombolysis or bypass depending on vascular assessment, with consent and uncertainty.
  8. Monitor pain, sensation, motor function and reperfusion complications; document and hand over using SBAR.

A threatened limb is not a routine outpatient referral. Revascularisation is time critical.

Rapid revision — 12 must-know facts

  1. Intermittent claudication is reproducible exertional muscle pain relieved by rest.
  2. ABPI ≤0.90 supports PAD; >1.40 suggests non-compressible vessels.
  3. PAD management includes smoking cessation, supervised exercise and cardiovascular risk reduction.
  4. Rest pain, ulcer or gangrene suggests chronic limb-threatening ischaemia.
  5. Acute limb ischaemia: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis and poikilothermia.
  6. New sensory loss or motor weakness indicates a threatened limb.
  7. Call vascular surgery immediately for ALI and give IV heparin if not contraindicated under protocol.
  8. Do not delay limb-saving treatment for nonessential imaging.
  9. AAA is usually defined as aortic diameter ≥3.0 cm.
  10. Suspected ruptured AAA requires immediate vascular/major haemorrhage response.
  11. After reperfusion, watch for compartment syndrome, hyperkalaemia and AKI.
  12. UK AAA screening offers a one-off ultrasound to eligible men in their 65th year.
