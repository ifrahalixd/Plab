Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease, Peptic Ulcer Disease & Gastric Cancer

Surgery · Lesson 06 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Upper GI conditions: from reflux to malignancy

PLAB red flags: Dysphagia, persistent vomiting, weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia, haematemesis, melaena or an abdominal mass demand prompt assessment. Sudden severe abdominal pain with guarding suggests perforation: resuscitate and refer urgently.

1 · Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)

Reflux of gastric contents causes retrosternal burning, acid regurgitation and symptoms worse after meals or lying flat. Differential diagnoses include cardiac chest pain, peptic ulcer disease and oesophageal malignancy. Assess alarm symptoms before empiric therapy.

Typical presentationApproach
Uncomplicated heartburn/regurgitationReview triggers, weight, medicines, lifestyle; offer an appropriate full-dose PPI trial for 4–8 weeks per NICE CG184 and review response.
Persistent or recurrent symptomsCheck adherence, timing, diagnosis and need for endoscopy; use lowest effective treatment when appropriate.
Dysphagia or suspected cancerFollow NICE NG12 urgent suspected-cancer pathway; do not simply repeat PPI courses.

Discuss weight reduction when appropriate, smoking cessation, avoiding personal triggers and not eating close to bedtime. Long-term reflux can cause oesophagitis, strictures and Barrett's oesophagus; Barrett's increases oesophageal adenocarcinoma risk.

2 · Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)

Gastric or duodenal mucosal ulcers are most commonly associated with Helicobacter pylori infection and NSAIDs. Epigastric pain, dyspepsia, nausea or bleeding may occur; symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish gastric from duodenal ulcers.

  1. Assess instability, bleeding, perforation, obstruction and cancer warning features.
  2. Review NSAIDs, aspirin, antiplatelets, anticoagulants, previous ulcers and H. pylori treatment.
  3. Use non-invasive H. pylori testing where appropriate (urea breath test or stool antigen); withhold PPI for 2 weeks before testing where clinically safe, and follow antibiotic washout recommendations.
  4. For H. pylori-positive ulcer disease, offer guideline/local-resistance-informed eradication therapy; confirm eradication in indicated circumstances, particularly gastric ulcer.
  5. Stop NSAIDs if possible; prescribe acid suppression and assess the need for endoscopy.

3 · Complications of peptic ulcer disease

ComplicationKey clue and management
BleedingHaematemesis/melaena, syncope or shock → ABCDE, crossmatch, urgent endoscopy after resuscitation.
PerforationSudden severe pain, rigid abdomen, free gas may be present → NBM, IV access, fluids, antibiotics according to local protocol, urgent surgery.
Gastric outlet obstructionEarly satiety, persistent large-volume vomiting, dehydration → fluids/electrolytes, NG decompression if indicated, urgent specialist investigation.

4 · Gastric cancer

Risk factors include H. pylori, smoking, older age, certain hereditary syndromes and chronic atrophic gastritis. Symptoms may be subtle: progressive weight loss, early satiety, persistent epigastric discomfort, anorexia, vomiting or iron-deficiency anaemia. A palpable epigastric mass is a late sign.

Investigation: upper GI endoscopy with biopsy confirms histology. Staging commonly uses CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, with endoscopic ultrasound or staging laparoscopy in selected patients through an upper GI multidisciplinary team. Treatment depends on stage, fitness and patient preference: endoscopic resection for selected very early lesions, surgery with perioperative systemic therapy for appropriate resectable disease, or palliative systemic/supportive interventions for advanced disease.

5 · NICE suspected cancer referrals

NICE NG12 recommends suspected-cancer pathway referral for people with dysphagia, or people aged 55 or over with weight loss and upper abdominal pain, reflux or dyspepsia for assessment of oesophageal/stomach cancer. Other combinations can warrant direct-access upper GI endoscopy; check the full age- and symptom-specific criteria.

Exam traps: Dysphagia is not 'simple reflux'. A negative initial test does not rule out cancer if symptoms persist. Never ignore perforation or bleeding while planning routine dyspepsia management. H. pylori testing while on PPIs can give false-negative results.

Decision frameworks & rapid slides

Pathway A — Dyspepsia or reflux

History + medication review + red flags → if unstable/bleeding/perforation, emergency pathway → if cancer criteria, urgent referral/endoscopy → otherwise lifestyle advice, H. pylori test-and-treat or empirical PPI as appropriate → reassess response.

Pathway B — Suspected peptic ulcer

Assess for NSAIDs/H. pylori → endoscopy when indicated → PPI and remove precipitating drugs where possible → eradicate H. pylori if positive → reassess, and follow up gastric ulcers appropriately.

Pathway C — Suspected gastric cancer

Alarm features → urgent upper GI endoscopy + biopsy → staging and MDT discussion → stage-appropriate curative or palliative treatment with shared decision-making.

Slide 1 — Typical GORD

Heartburn + regurgitation; evaluate alarm features before treatment.

Slide 2 — PUD causes

Think H. pylori and NSAIDs; recognise bleeding, perforation and obstruction.

Slide 3 — Cancer warning features

Dysphagia, weight loss, early satiety, anaemia, vomiting; use NG12 referral criteria.

Four integrated clinical cases

Case 1 — Uncomplicated reflux

A 32-year-old has burning retrosternal pain after meals and sour regurgitation without dysphagia, weight loss or bleeding. Approach: assess alternative causes, discuss triggers, offer a time-limited PPI and review; safety-net for alarm symptoms.

Case 2 — H. pylori ulcer

A 45-year-old with recurrent epigastric discomfort uses ibuprofen frequently. Stool antigen is positive for H. pylori. Approach: assess complications and need for endoscopy; stop NSAID where feasible, treat H. pylori using current local regimen and provide PPI as indicated.

Case 3 — Perforated ulcer

A 61-year-old develops sudden severe epigastric pain with guarding, tachycardia and hypotension. Approach: ABCDE, urgent senior surgical/anaesthetic help, NBM, IV resuscitation, antibiotics according to protocol and urgent operative assessment; do not delay in instability.

Case 4 — Suspected gastric cancer

A 67-year-old reports 6 kg weight loss, early satiety, persistent dyspepsia and iron-deficiency anaemia. Approach: urgent suspected-cancer assessment, upper GI endoscopy with biopsy, staging and MDT planning; explain concerns clearly without premature diagnosis.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute dyspepsia and alarm symptoms station

Brief: A 59-year-old attends GP with worsening indigestion, reflux and unintentional weight loss. Take a focused history, explain concerns and propose next steps.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and patient concerns.
  2. Explore onset, site, relation to meals, reflux/regurgitation, severity and impact.
  3. Ask specifically about dysphagia, odynophagia, vomiting, bleeding, melaena, weight loss and early satiety.
  4. Ask about NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants, smoking, alcohol, H. pylori, family history and prior endoscopy.
  5. Assess observations and examination needs; recognise emergency symptoms.
  6. Explain why urgent upper GI investigation is appropriate under NG12 criteria.
  7. Explain endoscopy and biopsy in plain language; avoid saying cancer is confirmed.
  8. Use shared decision-making, safety-netting, documentation and clear follow-up.

Suggested explanation: “Indigestion is often caused by acid reflux, but your weight loss means we should check for other causes promptly. I would arrange an urgent specialist assessment and a camera test to look at the upper digestive tract.”
