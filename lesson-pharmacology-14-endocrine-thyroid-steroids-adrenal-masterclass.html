Endocrine Pharmacology

Thyroid medicines, corticosteroids, adrenal emergencies and hormonal therapies · PLAB clinical masterclass.

Deep Dive · Mechanisms, feedback and clinical calculations

1. Feedback-loop interpretation

In primary thyroid failure the pituitary responds to low thyroid hormone with increased TSH. In central hypothyroidism the signal is inadequate, so TSH may appear “normal” despite low FT4. This distinction determines which marker can guide levothyroxine titration. In primary adrenal failure ACTH is high and mineralocorticoid deficiency may produce hyperkalaemia; secondary adrenal insufficiency usually preserves aldosterone.

2. Molecular pharmacology of thyroid drugs

Levothyroxine (T4) is deiodinated to T3, which binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptors to alter transcription, metabolic rate and adrenergic sensitivity. Thyroid peroxidase oxidises iodide and catalyses iodination/coupling in thyroglobulin. Carbimazole/methimazole inhibit this enzyme, so existing stored hormone can continue to circulate for a time; beta-blockade may improve symptoms while synthesis inhibition takes effect. PTU also inhibits peripheral conversion, but liver toxicity limits routine use.

3. Glucocorticoid receptors and circadian biology

Unbound cortisol enters cells and binds cytosolic glucocorticoid receptors. The receptor complex translocates to the nucleus and alters gene expression, including suppression of pro-inflammatory signalling. Cortisol secretion normally peaks around waking; replacement attempts to approximate this pattern. Exogenous glucocorticoids suppress ACTH and can lead to adrenal atrophy, explaining the danger of abrupt withdrawal after sustained exposure.

4. Steroid dose equivalence — principle, not a prescription

Different corticosteroids have very different anti-inflammatory potencies and biological durations. A nominal milligram dose of dexamethasone is not equivalent to the same milligram dose of hydrocortisone. Conversion also depends on indication and mineralocorticoid needs. Always check a current BNF/local equivalence resource; do not extrapolate emergency hydrocortisone doses to chronic prednisolone replacement.

5. Why fludrocortisone matters in primary Addison disease

Aldosterone increases epithelial sodium-channel and Na+/K+-ATPase activity in distal nephron cells, supporting sodium retention and potassium excretion. Destruction of the adrenal cortex removes this signal; fludrocortisone replaces it. In pituitary ACTH deficiency the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone axis often remains intact, so fludrocortisone is generally unnecessary.

6. The renal collecting duct and desmopressin

AVP binds V2 receptors (Gs-coupled), increasing cAMP and apical aquaporin-2 insertion. Water reabsorption rises when a medullary gradient exists. Desmopressin mimics this action in central AVP deficiency. Sodium changes reflect water balance, not simply sodium administration: both missed doses and excessive fluid intake can cause serious, opposite problems.

7. Red-flag diagnostic reasoning

Fever on carbimazole → suspect neutropenia. Hypotension and vomiting in known Addison disease → treat adrenal crisis without waiting for tests. New atrial fibrillation in a patient with excess levothyroxine → consider over-replacement. Confusion or seizures on desmopressin → urgent sodium assessment. These are pattern-recognition emergencies, but stabilisation and broad differential assessment still matter.

8. Prescribing in special populations

Older patients with coronary disease are more vulnerable to excessive thyroid hormone replacement. Pregnancy changes thyroid-binding proteins and hormone requirements and alters antithyroid drug risk. CYP inhibitors or inducers can alter systemic steroid exposure. Kidney disease raises hyperkalaemia risk with mineralocorticoid antagonists. Individualise monitoring and involve specialist teams where required.

9. PLAB 2 clinical communication

For adrenal insufficiency, demonstrate sick-day planning, emergency injection escalation and what to do if vomiting prevents tablets. For carbimazole, ask the patient to repeat back the fever/sore-throat instruction. For long-term prednisolone, discuss monitoring and withdrawal without frightening the patient or suggesting abrupt cessation. Document shared decisions and safety-netting.

10. Final synthesis

Each endocrine prescription must answer four questions: Which feedback loop is abnormal? Is the medicine replacing, suppressing or blocking a hormone? What harms arise from under- or overtreatment? Which clinical symptoms or biochemical tests trigger urgent action? Use the answers to structure clinical cases and safe prescribing.

1. Clinical framework: treat the hormone disorder, not just a laboratory value

Endocrine drugs either replace a missing hormone, suppress excessive synthesis, block a receptor or modify feedback. Begin by distinguishing primary gland disease from pituitary or hypothalamic disease. Interpret TSH alongside free T4, clinical features, illness, pregnancy and treatment history. Check indication, formulation, interacting medicines, baseline tests and follow-up plan. A stable outpatient dose is not the same as an emergency replacement regimen.

2. Hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis

TRH stimulates pituitary TSH, which drives thyroidal synthesis and release of T4 and T3. Circulating free hormones exert negative feedback. T4 is a prohormone converted by deiodinases to active T3 in tissues. Primary hypothyroidism typically has raised TSH and low free T4; central hypothyroidism may have a low or inappropriately normal TSH with low free T4. In central disease, TSH alone is unreliable for dose titration. Always consider adrenal insufficiency before starting thyroid hormone in suspected pituitary disease.

3. Levothyroxine: replacement and practical administration

Levothyroxine is synthetic T4, with a long half-life that allows once-daily administration. Dose is individualised according to age, body mass, cardiovascular disease, pregnancy and degree of hypothyroidism; frail patients or those with coronary disease often require a cautious starting dose. Take consistently, usually on an empty stomach, and separate from iron or calcium supplements because absorption is reduced. PPIs, coeliac disease, adherence and formulation changes can alter requirements. Monitor TSH after sufficient time for a new steady state, commonly around 6–8 weeks after a dose change, then at suitable intervals. In central hypothyroidism, follow free T4 and specialist guidance rather than TSH.

4. Hypothyroidism: common traps and emergencies

Symptoms include fatigue, constipation, cold intolerance, dry skin and bradycardia; biochemistry establishes the diagnosis. Avoid automatically escalating levothyroxine for fatigue when thyroid tests are normal. Excess replacement may cause palpitations, atrial fibrillation and bone loss. Myxoedema coma is a rare life-threatening decompensation with hypothermia, altered consciousness, hypoventilation and cardiovascular instability: obtain emergency senior/endocrine/critical-care support, treat precipitants and use specialist-guided thyroid hormone plus stress-dose glucocorticoid coverage until adrenal insufficiency has been addressed.

5. Hyperthyroidism: mechanisms and treatment goals

Graves disease is driven by TSH-receptor antibodies; toxic nodules produce hormone autonomously, whereas thyroiditis releases stored hormone and may not benefit from antithyroid synthesis inhibitors. Assess free T4/T3, TSH, symptoms, heart rate, pregnancy and aetiology. Options include antithyroid drugs, radioiodine and surgery depending on cause and circumstances. Beta-blockers reduce adrenergic symptoms but do not correct the underlying hormone excess. Antithyroid drug dosing and follow-up require current local/endocrine guidance.

6. Carbimazole and propylthiouracil

Carbimazole is converted to methimazole, which inhibits thyroid peroxidase and new hormone synthesis. Propylthiouracil (PTU) also reduces peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion at higher doses but has a clinically important risk of severe liver injury. Choice in pregnancy is trimester- and patient-dependent and should be made with specialist advice; antithyroid drugs can cause fetal thyroid effects. Counsel every patient about agranulocytosis: stop the drug and obtain urgent full blood count if fever, sore throat or mouth ulcers develop. Seek urgent review for jaundice or dark urine because hepatotoxicity is possible.

7. Thyroid storm and severe thyrotoxicosis

Thyroid storm is a clinical emergency with severe systemic illness, often fever, marked tachycardia, agitation, gastrointestinal symptoms and cardiac failure. Call senior, emergency and critical-care teams immediately; assess ABCDE, treat triggers and provide supportive care. Specialist protocols combine control of adrenergic effects, suppression of synthesis, measures to inhibit hormone release and glucocorticoids, with careful sequencing and contraindication checks. Do not wait for an extreme hormone result to act. A beta-blocker may be unsafe in decompensated heart failure or shock.

8. Adrenal physiology: cortisol, aldosterone and ACTH

The hypothalamus releases CRH, the pituitary ACTH, and the adrenal cortex cortisol. Cortisol maintains vascular responsiveness, glucose homeostasis and the stress response; aldosterone regulates sodium and potassium handling in the distal nephron. Primary adrenal insufficiency can cause cortisol and aldosterone deficiency, hyperkalaemia and hyperpigmentation; secondary adrenal insufficiency often preserves aldosterone because the renin–angiotensin system remains active. A normal sodium level does not exclude adrenal insufficiency.

9. Hydrocortisone replacement and fludrocortisone

Physiological glucocorticoid replacement often uses oral hydrocortisone in divided doses that mimic daytime cortisol exposure; exact regimens are individualised by endocrine teams. Primary adrenal insufficiency commonly also needs fludrocortisone for mineralocorticoid replacement, guided by blood pressure, postural symptoms, electrolytes and renin where appropriate. Secondary adrenal insufficiency usually does not require fludrocortisone. Patients need steroid emergency identification, sick-day education, access to emergency injection where indicated and a clear plan for vomiting or inability to take tablets.

10. Adrenal crisis: do not delay treatment

Suspect adrenal crisis in a patient with known adrenal insufficiency or steroid suppression who has collapse, hypotension, vomiting, severe illness, hypoglycaemia or electrolyte disturbance. Take blood for cortisol/ACTH if immediately feasible, but NEVER delay emergency hydrocortisone. Adult UK emergency guidance commonly uses 100 mg hydrocortisone IV or IM immediately, followed by 200 mg over 24 hours by infusion or 50 mg IV/IM every 6 hours, alongside rapid isotonic saline, glucose monitoring and treatment of the precipitant. These are emergency adult teaching figures; follow current local protocols and adapt to the patient. Arrange urgent hospital assessment and endocrine input.

11. Systemic corticosteroids: receptors and effects

Glucocorticoids cross cell membranes and bind intracellular receptors, changing gene transcription and suppressing multiple inflammatory pathways. Prednisolone, dexamethasone and hydrocortisone differ in glucocorticoid potency, duration and mineralocorticoid effects. Steroids are used for selected inflammatory, autoimmune, oncological and emergency indications, but not as interchangeable doses. Common harms include hyperglycaemia, mood changes, infection risk, hypertension, osteoporosis, cataracts and skin fragility; higher dose and longer exposure generally increase risk.

12. Steroid withdrawal and HPA-axis suppression

Exogenous glucocorticoids suppress CRH and ACTH. Abrupt cessation after significant exposure can precipitate adrenal insufficiency; withdrawal planning depends on dose, duration, formulation, repeated courses, intercurrent illness and clinical context. Some short courses can be stopped without tapering, whereas prolonged or high-risk treatment may need gradual reduction and assessment of adrenal recovery. Do not use a single universal taper rule. During significant illness, surgery or vomiting, patients at risk may need stress-dose coverage under local protocols.

13. Long-term steroid prescribing safety

Document the indication, dose, duration, review date and lowest effective regimen. Assess blood pressure, weight, glycaemia, infection, bone health, eye symptoms and mental health. Consider fracture-risk assessment and bone protection where indicated. Discuss vaccination and exposure to infections, especially varicella or measles in susceptible immunosuppressed patients. Check drug interactions and avoid routine NSAID combinations when GI bleeding risk is high. Inhaled and topical corticosteroids may also have systemic effects with high exposure, but are not automatically equivalent to oral treatment.

14. Cushing syndrome and pharmacological treatment

Excess cortisol may cause proximal weakness, bruising, purple striae, hypertension, diabetes and osteoporosis. Endogenous Cushing syndrome needs biochemical confirmation and aetiological work-up, often specialist-led. Definitive treatment may be surgery; selected patients require drugs that inhibit adrenal steroidogenesis, such as metyrapone or osilodrostat, with close specialist monitoring for adrenal insufficiency, electrolyte disturbance and drug-specific toxicities. Exogenous steroid exposure is a common cause of Cushingoid features; do not abruptly stop necessary steroids without a plan.

15. Mineralocorticoid antagonists and hyperaldosteronism

Spironolactone blocks the mineralocorticoid receptor, reducing sodium retention and potassium loss. It is used for selected heart failure and hyperaldosteronism indications, with monitoring of potassium and renal function. Adverse effects include hyperkalaemia and endocrine effects such as gynaecomastia; eplerenone is more receptor-selective but still requires electrolyte monitoring. In suspected primary aldosteronism, investigate the cause and consider surgery or medical treatment according to specialist assessment.

16. Diabetes insipidus and desmopressin

Central diabetes insipidus (arginine vasopressin deficiency) causes large volumes of dilute urine and thirst. Desmopressin is a vasopressin V2-receptor agonist that increases collecting-duct water reabsorption. Incorrect dosing or excess fluid intake can cause water retention and dangerous hyponatraemia, including seizures. In hospital, missed desmopressin in a patient with central diabetes insipidus can also be dangerous; assess fluid balance and sodium urgently. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus does not respond reliably to desmopressin and requires cause-specific management.

Water balance schematic

Low AVP → collecting duct cannot conserve water → high-volume dilute urine → hypernatraemia if drinking cannot match losses. Desmopressin → V2 receptor activation → aquaporin insertion → increased water reabsorption. Excess desmopressin + excess fluid → dilutional hyponatraemia.

17. Sex hormones, contraception and hormone therapy

Combined hormonal contraception suppresses ovulation through oestrogen/progestogen feedback; progestogen-only methods act through ovulation suppression and/or cervical mucus and endometrial effects. Choice depends on migraine with aura, venous thromboembolism risk, smoking, age, postpartum status, interacting enzyme-inducing drugs and patient preference. Menopausal hormone therapy can improve vasomotor symptoms but requires individualised assessment of VTE, breast and endometrial risks; systemic oestrogen generally needs endometrial protection in patients with a uterus. Refer to current UKMEC, FSRH, NICE and BNF guidance.

18. Drug interactions across endocrine pharmacology

Iron and calcium reduce levothyroxine absorption; enzyme-inducing medicines can alter hormone exposure. Antithyroid drugs require interaction and monitoring checks, including when anticoagulation is used because thyroid status can alter warfarin response. Systemic steroids can raise blood glucose and interact with enzyme inhibitors/inducers. Spironolactone with ACE inhibitors, ARBs or potassium supplements increases hyperkalaemia risk. Desmopressin plus excess fluid intake raises hyponatraemia risk. Reconcile OTC medicines and supplements.

19. Pregnancy, fertility and breastfeeding

Thyroid hormone requirements often increase in pregnancy; uncontrolled hypo- or hyperthyroidism can harm mother and fetus. Antithyroid drug choice and dosing need specialist guidance, and radioactive iodine is contraindicated in pregnancy. Women with adrenal insufficiency require coordinated endocrine/obstetric care and a delivery stress-dose plan. Contraception and hormonal therapies have method-specific eligibility criteria. Check current UK guidance rather than extrapolating nonpregnant doses or risks.

20. Clinical case 1 — fever while taking carbimazole

A 29-year-old with Graves disease taking carbimazole reports fever, sore throat and mouth ulcers. The priority is possible agranulocytosis, not an uncomplicated viral infection. Tell them to stop carbimazole and obtain an urgent full blood count and same-day clinical assessment. If neutropenic or septic, escalate immediately. Explain that routine monitoring does not replace symptom-triggered action.

21. Clinical case 2 — collapse after gastroenteritis

A 45-year-old with Addison disease has persistent vomiting, weakness and BP 78/46 mmHg. Treat as adrenal crisis: urgent ABCDE assessment, IV/IM hydrocortisone immediately, isotonic saline and glucose/electrolyte monitoring, and treat the infection or other trigger. Do not wait for cortisol results. After recovery, teach sick-day rules, injection technique and emergency identification.

22. Clinical case 3 — levothyroxine and iron

A 67-year-old with stable hypothyroidism develops a raised TSH after starting iron tablets. Ask about missed doses, changes in administration, supplements and malabsorption before escalating levothyroxine. Iron can bind and reduce absorption; counsel on separation and consistency, then repeat thyroid tests after an appropriate interval. In a patient with coronary disease, avoid rapid empiric dose increases.

23. Clinical case 4 — postoperative polyuria

A patient with known central diabetes insipidus has missed desmopressin after surgery and develops marked polyuria and rising sodium. This is a time-critical water-balance problem. Escalate urgently, assess fluid status, electrolytes and urine output, and reinstate specialist-guided desmopressin and fluids while avoiding overly rapid sodium correction.

24. PLAB 2 station — counselling on long-term prednisolone

Start by exploring indication and concerns. Explain the intended benefit, dose schedule, and that the patient should not abruptly stop a long course. Discuss infection, glucose, mood, blood pressure and bone effects, monitoring, and when urgent assessment is needed. Ask about OTC NSAIDs, vaccination and steroid alert identification where appropriate. Use teach-back: “What would you do if you were vomiting and could not keep your tablets down?” Tailor advice to the actual regimen.

25. Prescribing checklist and exam synthesis

Before prescribing, identify primary versus central endocrine disease; confirm pregnancy, comorbidities and drug interactions; document formulation, dose, route, monitoring and review; counsel about critical red flags (carbimazole fever, adrenal crisis, desmopressin-associated hyponatraemia); and distinguish chronic outpatient care from resuscitation. Use current BNF/NICE and specialist protocols for patient-specific decisions.

    Starting or reviewing levothyroxine

    Confirm diagnosis and primary versus central disease → assess pregnancy, age, cardiac history, adherence and interacting supplements → choose individualised replacement → counsel consistent administration → recheck appropriate biochemical marker after adequate interval → adjust carefully and reassess symptoms.

    Suspected adrenal crisis

    Recognise collapse, hypotension, vomiting or severe illness in at-risk patient → call emergency team, ABCDE → hydrocortisone immediately IV/IM, without waiting for tests → isotonic saline, glucose/electrolytes and precipitant management → urgent hospital/endocrine follow-up and future prevention education.

    Fever on carbimazole

    Stop carbimazole → urgent same-day FBC and clinical assessment → assess for sepsis/neutropenia → senior/endocrine input for alternative thyroid control → explain clear return precautions. Jaundice or dark urine also needs urgent review for liver injury.

    Long-term steroid safety

    Confirm ongoing indication → record dose/duration and HPA suppression risk → monitor infection, glucose, BP, mood, bone health and interactions → agree lowest effective dose and planned taper when appropriate → provide illness/surgery coverage plan and steroid emergency information.
