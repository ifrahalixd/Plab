Blood Transfusion, Reactions & Complications

Emergency rule: Any suspected serious transfusion reaction: STOP transfusion, assess ABCDE, call for help and inform the blood bank.

Why transfuse?

Transfusion replaces a deficient blood component when expected benefit outweighs risk. Red cells improve oxygen-carrying capacity; platelets and plasma address selected haemostatic problems.

PLAB application: Identify the clinical indication before ordering a component.

Consent and patient identification

Explain indication, benefits, common and serious risks, alternatives and any special requirements. Obtain and document consent according to local policy. Confirm identity using approved identifiers at sampling and bedside.

PLAB application: Wrong-blood-in-tube and wrong-patient errors are preventable hazards.

ABO and RhD compatibility

ABO incompatibility can cause severe acute intravascular haemolysis. Red cell compatibility depends on recipient antibodies and donor red cell antigens. RhD status is especially relevant to pregnancy and sensitisation.

PLAB application: Do not rely on memory: follow blood-bank compatibility reports.

Group and screen versus crossmatch

Group and screen determines ABO/RhD and checks for clinically significant red-cell antibodies. Crossmatching selects compatible units when transfusion is needed. Emergency uncrossmatched blood follows hospital protocols.

PLAB application: Never delay life-saving resuscitation while routine testing is pending; activate emergency pathway.

Red cell transfusion thresholds

In stable adults without major haemorrhage or acute coronary syndrome, NICE NG24 recommends considering a restrictive threshold of 70 g/L and a target of 70–90 g/L after transfusion. Different thresholds apply to selected groups, including acute coronary syndrome.

PLAB application: Treat the patient and context, not the haemoglobin number alone.

Single-unit red cell strategy

For non-bleeding adults, consider one red-cell unit at a time and reassess symptoms and haemoglobin before additional units. Active bleeding requires a different approach.

PLAB application: Document reassessment rather than automatically prescribing two units.

Platelet transfusion

Platelets may be indicated for bleeding with thrombocytopenia or before procedures, depending on count, procedure and clinical context. Routine prophylactic platelet transfusion can be harmful or inappropriate in some disorders such as TTP and HIT.

PLAB application: Use NICE NG24 and specialist guidance for thresholds.

Fresh frozen plasma and cryoprecipitate

FFP replaces multiple coagulation factors in selected patients with bleeding and abnormal coagulation tests. Cryoprecipitate supplies fibrinogen. Neither is routine volume replacement.

PLAB application: Identify the haemostatic deficit and recheck response.

Before and during transfusion

Record baseline observations, ensure venous access and appropriate administration equipment, verify the patient and component at bedside, and monitor according to policy. Early observations are particularly important.

PLAB application: A new symptom during transfusion must be taken seriously.

Acute haemolytic transfusion reaction

ABO-incompatible red cells may cause fever, rigors, back/flank pain, hypotension, dark urine, DIC and acute kidney injury. It can be rapidly fatal.

PLAB application: Stop transfusion immediately and escalate.

Febrile non-haemolytic and allergic reactions

Febrile reactions cause fever or chills but are diagnoses of exclusion: infection and haemolysis must first be considered. Allergic reactions may cause itching or urticaria; anaphylaxis can cause airway compromise and shock.

PLAB application: Do not assume mild fever is harmless without assessment.

TRALI

Transfusion-related acute lung injury typically presents with acute hypoxaemia and non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema during or within 6 hours of transfusion, without circulatory overload as the main explanation.

PLAB application: Stop transfusion, give oxygen and seek urgent senior support.

TACO

Transfusion-associated circulatory overload causes respiratory distress and pulmonary oedema due to volume overload, often with hypertension or positive fluid balance. Risk rises with cardiac/renal disease and rapid infusion.

PLAB application: Prevent with risk assessment, appropriate rate and careful volume planning.

Bacterial contamination and sepsis

Contaminated components, particularly platelets, can cause fever, rigors and septic shock. Stop transfusion, urgently assess and follow local sepsis and transfusion-reaction protocols.

PLAB application: Preserve the bag and giving set for investigation.

Delayed reactions and long-term complications

Delayed haemolytic reactions may occur days to weeks later, with falling Hb or jaundice. Repeated transfusions can cause alloimmunisation and iron overload. Some patients need irradiated or antigen-matched components.

PLAB application: Ask about previous reactions, antibodies and transfusion history.

Immediate response to a suspected reaction

STOP the transfusion; keep IV access with normal saline using new tubing per protocol; assess ABCDE, call for help, recheck identity and labels, notify blood bank, send requested samples and return the component/set. Do not restart unless explicitly authorised after assessment.

PLAB application: Manage anaphylaxis, shock or sepsis immediately according to emergency guidance.

PLAB 2 — Explain a blood transfusion

Introduce yourself and confirm identity. Explore symptoms, previous transfusions or reactions, beliefs and concerns. Explain why transfusion is considered, alternatives, expected benefits, compatibility checks and potential risks. Obtain informed consent, invite questions, and safety-net for fever, rash, breathlessness, dark urine or feeling suddenly unwell during or after transfusion.

Deep Dive

TRALI versus TACO

Both can cause acute hypoxia. TRALI is an acute lung injury not primarily explained by overload; TACO reflects hydrostatic pulmonary oedema. Clinical assessment, fluid balance, observations and investigations help distinguish them.

Why ABO errors are dangerous

Preformed antibodies can activate complement and cause intravascular haemolysis, shock, DIC and kidney injury. Identity checks at sample collection and bedside are critical.

Component choice

Red cells treat selected anaemia or blood loss; platelets treat selected platelet-related bleeding; FFP supplies multiple clotting factors; cryoprecipitate concentrates fibrinogen. Components are not interchangeable.

Restrictive thresholds have exceptions

Thresholds differ in major haemorrhage, acute coronary syndrome, chronic transfusion programmes and certain specialist contexts. NICE NG24 provides recommendations but clinical judgement and local protocols remain essential.

Recognition of delayed complications

Delayed haemolytic reactions can occur after discharge. Repeat transfusions can produce red-cell alloantibodies and iron loading; record antibody history and follow specialist plans.

Safe reporting

Follow the hospital transfusion policy, preserve samples and component, inform the transfusion laboratory, document observations and report serious hazards through established UK haemovigilance channels.
