Urticaria, Angioedema & Anaphylaxis

PLAB priority: Recognise anaphylaxis immediately, give IM adrenaline for ABC compromise, and distinguish histamine-mediated urticaria from bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

01. Recognise the three syndromes

Urticaria causes transient itchy wheals; angioedema causes deeper swelling; anaphylaxis is a rapidly developing systemic hypersensitivity reaction with airway, breathing or circulation compromise.

PLAB application: A rash alone is not anaphylaxis; assess ABCDE immediately if systemic symptoms occur.

02. Urticaria morphology

Raised, erythematous or skin-coloured itchy wheals change shape and location and usually resolve within 24 hours without bruising.

PLAB application: Individual lesions lasting longer than 24 hours, painful or bruising suggest urticarial vasculitis.

03. Acute versus chronic urticaria

Acute urticaria lasts less than six weeks; chronic urticaria persists or recurs for six weeks or longer. Chronic spontaneous urticaria often has no identifiable external allergen.

PLAB application: Avoid indiscriminate food allergy testing without a suggestive history.

04. Triggers and history

Ask about new drugs, foods, insect stings, infection, exercise, heat, cold, pressure and timing. NSAIDs may exacerbate urticaria; ACE inhibitors can cause bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

PLAB application: Ask about previous reactions, adrenaline auto-injector use and asthma.

05. Assess for anaphylaxis

Look for sudden tongue or throat swelling, stridor, hoarse voice, wheeze, hypoxia, hypotension, collapse or rapidly progressive symptoms after exposure. Skin findings may be absent.

PLAB application: Do not delay intramuscular adrenaline while waiting for rash or laboratory confirmation.

06. Immediate anaphylaxis management

Call emergency help; ABCDE; give intramuscular adrenaline 1 mg/mL (1:1000) into anterolateral thigh at age-appropriate dose, repeat after 5 minutes if airway, breathing or circulation problems persist.

PLAB application: For adults, UK guidance uses 500 micrograms IM; follow current Resuscitation Council UK age-based guidance.

07. Position, oxygen and fluids

Lay the patient flat with legs raised if tolerated; if breathing is difficult, they may sit with legs extended; pregnant patients lie on left side. Avoid sudden standing. Give oxygen and IV fluids when indicated.

PLAB application: Positioning and resuscitation are important alongside adrenaline.

08. What not to prioritise

Antihistamines may relieve residual skin symptoms but do not treat airway or circulatory compromise. Corticosteroids are not routinely recommended for emergency anaphylaxis treatment.

PLAB application: Never substitute chlorphenamine or hydrocortisone for IM adrenaline.

09. Monitoring and discharge

Observe according to severity and risk of biphasic reaction; assess response, need for repeated adrenaline, asthma and access to care. Provide allergy referral, written action plan and auto-injector education when indicated.

PLAB application: Explain when to use an auto-injector and call emergency services (999 in UK).

10. Uncomplicated urticaria treatment

Use a regular second-generation non-sedating H1 antihistamine such as cetirizine or loratadine, with trigger avoidance when clear. Chronic refractory symptoms need clinician-led escalation and specialist advice.

PLAB application: Avoid long-term oral corticosteroids for chronic urticaria.

11. Histamine-mediated angioedema

Often accompanies itchy wheals and may involve eyelids or lips. Treat associated urticaria with antihistamines; evaluate airway symptoms urgently.

PLAB application: Swelling with wheals suggests histamine-mediated disease, but airway safety comes first.

12. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema

ACE-inhibitor and hereditary angioedema may cause swelling without wheals or itch, sometimes with abdominal pain. Conventional antihistamines, steroids and adrenaline may be ineffective for bradykinin-mediated attacks.

PLAB application: Stop suspected ACE inhibitor and obtain emergency airway and specialist management.

13. Hereditary angioedema

Recurrent non-itchy swelling, abdominal attacks or family history suggest C1-inhibitor deficiency/dysfunction. Test C4 and C1 inhibitor level/function through specialist services.

PLAB application: Airway attacks are emergencies; specialist acute treatments include C1-inhibitor replacement or bradykinin-targeted therapies.

14. Investigations

Urticaria is usually a clinical diagnosis. Serum mast-cell tryptase can support suspected anaphylaxis if collected at appropriate times but must not delay treatment; normal tryptase does not exclude it.

PLAB application: Document the timeline and suspected trigger; refer after suspected anaphylaxis.

15. Important differentials

Consider contact dermatitis, mastocytosis, urticarial vasculitis, cellulitis, hereditary angioedema and panic symptoms; distinguish airway compromise from isolated itch.

PLAB application: Painful persistent purpuric wheals warrant evaluation for vasculitis.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety net

Explain wheals versus deep swelling, medicine use, trigger diary and avoidance of known allergens. Demonstrate auto-injector technique and emergency action plan after anaphylaxis.

PLAB application: Use clear language: breathing trouble, faintness or throat swelling requires immediate emergency help.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Mast cells and wheals

Histamine and other mast-cell mediators increase vascular permeability, producing transient superficial oedema and itch.

Why anaphylaxis threatens life

Mediator release can cause laryngeal oedema, bronchospasm, vasodilation and capillary leak, rapidly impairing oxygenation and perfusion.

Why adrenaline works

Alpha-adrenergic effects counter vasodilation and oedema; beta-adrenergic effects support cardiac output and bronchodilation.

Histamine versus bradykinin

Bradykinin-mediated angioedema usually lacks urticaria and often responds poorly to conventional allergy medications; airway management is paramount.

Biphasic reactions

Symptoms can recur after apparent recovery without re-exposure; observation duration should reflect severity, response and individual risk.

Testing and interpretation

Tryptase timing and baseline comparison can support mast-cell activation, but clinical diagnosis and treatment take priority; chronic urticaria rarely requires broad indiscriminate allergy panels.
