Deep Vein Thrombosis, Pulmonary Embolism & Anticoagulation

Emergency: Suspected PE with shock, collapse or severe hypoxaemia requires immediate resuscitation and senior help.

01. Clinical overview

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). A PE can be fatal; prompt recognition and risk assessment matter.

PLAB application: Assess ABCDE first if unstable.

02. Risk factors

Recent surgery or immobilisation, active cancer, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen therapy, previous VTE, inherited thrombophilia, obesity and prolonged travel increase risk. VTE can also be unprovoked.

PLAB application: Ask about provoking factors and bleeding risks.

03. DVT presentation

Unilateral leg swelling, pain, tenderness, warmth and erythema suggest DVT, but symptoms are nonspecific. Compare both legs and assess for alternative causes such as cellulitis or Baker cyst.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose or exclude DVT from symptoms alone.

04. Pulmonary embolism presentation

PE may cause sudden breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, haemoptysis, syncope or hypoxia. Examination can be normal. Shock or persistent hypotension indicates potentially high-risk PE.

PLAB application: Unstable suspected PE requires immediate senior-led resuscitation.

05. Wells score for DVT

Use the two-level DVT Wells score (likely ≥2; unlikely ≤1). In likely DVT arrange proximal leg vein ultrasound promptly; if imaging is delayed, follow NICE interim anticoagulation and repeat-imaging rules.

PLAB application: Use validated scoring rather than intuition alone.

06. D-dimer

A negative D-dimer can help rule out VTE in appropriate low-probability patients. A positive result is nonspecific. Age-adjusted thresholds may be considered in patients over 50 using validated assays and local protocols.

PLAB application: Do not rely on D-dimer to exclude high-probability VTE.

07. DVT imaging

Proximal leg vein ultrasound is first-line. If initial scan is negative but D-dimer positive, NICE recommends repeat proximal ultrasound 6–8 days later in relevant pathways.

PLAB application: Check the exact NG158 algorithm and timing.

08. Wells score for PE

Use the two-level PE Wells score: likely >4; unlikely ≤4. For likely PE, offer CTPA immediately where possible; if delayed, offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation when appropriate.

PLAB application: PE unlikely usually starts with D-dimer testing.

09. PE imaging

CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is common first-line imaging. Consider ventilation/perfusion scanning when contrast is unsuitable, such as severe renal impairment or contrast allergy, guided by local practice.

PLAB application: Pregnancy needs a tailored imaging pathway.

10. Immediate management

Assess ABCDE, oxygen only if hypoxaemic, IV access and monitoring as appropriate. Start therapeutic anticoagulation for confirmed VTE and while awaiting delayed imaging when indicated, after assessing contraindications.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency escalation in shock.

11. Anticoagulant selection

NICE commonly recommends apixaban or rivaroxaban for suitable adults with confirmed proximal DVT or PE. Alternatives include LMWH followed by dabigatran/edoxaban or LMWH with a vitamin K antagonist. Consider kidney function, interactions, pregnancy and antiphospholipid syndrome.

PLAB application: DOACs are not suitable for everyone.

12. Duration and review

Treat confirmed proximal DVT or PE for at least 3 months, then reassess recurrence risk, provoking factors, bleeding risk and patient preferences. Unprovoked VTE may warrant longer treatment.

PLAB application: Distal DVT and special populations need individualised plans.

13. High-risk PE and reperfusion

PE with haemodynamic instability requires urgent specialist-led management; systemic thrombolysis may be considered when appropriate. Routine thrombolysis is not recommended for haemodynamically stable PE.

PLAB application: Major bleeding risk must be assessed.

14. Special situations

In pregnancy, LMWH is generally preferred and DOACs are avoided. Active cancer, severe renal impairment, triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome and extremes of body weight require tailored anticoagulant decisions.

PLAB application: Discuss with haematology or specialist teams.

15. Anticoagulation safety

Ask about bleeding, medicines (including NSAIDs), falls, kidney/liver disease, adherence and procedures. Counsel on bruising, haematuria, melaena, severe headache, and when to seek emergency help. Warfarin requires INR monitoring.

PLAB application: Never stop anticoagulants without a clinical plan.

16. PLAB 2 OSCE approach

Introduce yourself and assess severity; take a focused VTE and bleeding history; examine appropriately; explain Wells score, D-dimer and imaging; discuss anticoagulant benefits/risks; check understanding and safety-net.

PLAB application: Escalate chest pain, syncope, haemoptysis or severe breathlessness.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Virchow triad

Venous stasis, vascular endothelial injury and hypercoagulability interact to promote venous clot formation.

Why D-dimer is nonspecific

Fibrin degradation rises with clot turnover but also with infection, inflammation, surgery, pregnancy and malignancy. Pre-test probability determines usefulness.

Why PE causes hypoxia

Obstructed pulmonary arteries create ventilation–perfusion mismatch and can strain the right ventricle; large PE can impair cardiac output.

Why anticoagulants work

They limit thrombus extension and recurrent embolism while endogenous fibrinolysis gradually resolves clot; they do not immediately dissolve a clot.

Why duration is individualised

Recurrence risk differs between transiently provoked, unprovoked and persistent-risk VTE. Balance benefit of extended anticoagulation against bleeding.

Explain the suspected diagnosis, tests, medication adherence, interaction checks, bleeding symptoms, follow-up and emergency return precautions in clear language.
