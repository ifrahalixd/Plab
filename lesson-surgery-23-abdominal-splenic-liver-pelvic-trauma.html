Abdominal and pelvic trauma: recognition, resuscitation and definitive care

Critical rule: Haemodynamically unstable trauma with suspected intra-abdominal bleeding needs immediate senior trauma and surgical involvement. Do not delay haemorrhage control for CT.

1 · Mechanisms and first assessment

Blunt trauma (road collisions, falls, assault) causes splenic, liver, mesenteric, renal and pelvic injury. Penetrating trauma may injure multiple organs and vessels. Begin <C>ABCDE, call major trauma team, control external haemorrhage, maintain spinal precautions when indicated, obtain two large-bore IV or intraosseous access, monitor ECG/SpO₂/BP, prevent hypothermia and reassess continuously. A soft abdomen does not exclude major internal bleeding, especially early.

2 · Haemorrhagic shock

Look for tachycardia, cool peripheries, altered mentation, hypotension (often late), reduced urine output and rising lactate/base deficit. Activate major haemorrhage protocol when indicated, give warmed blood products according to local balanced resuscitation policy, avoid excessive crystalloid, monitor calcium, temperature and coagulation, and arrange urgent definitive haemorrhage control. Give tranexamic acid promptly when indicated for major traumatic bleeding, ideally within 3 hours; do not give after 3 hours unless specific evidence of hyperfibrinolysis and specialist direction.

3 · FAST, eFAST and CT

FAST evaluates free fluid in peritoneal/pericardial spaces; eFAST adds pleural assessment. Positive FAST in an unstable patient supports urgent operative/haemorrhage-control planning. Negative FAST does not rule out bleeding, retroperitoneal injury or hollow-viscus damage. Haemodynamically stable patients with suspected significant abdominal trauma generally undergo contrast-enhanced CT abdomen/pelvis according to trauma protocol. Never send an unstable patient to CT if this delays life-saving intervention.

4 · Splenic injury

Typically follows blunt left upper quadrant or lower chest trauma; may cause LUQ tenderness, left shoulder-tip (Kehr) pain, shock or delayed haemorrhage. Stable patients: CT to grade injury, serial observations/haemoglobin, specialist monitoring, and possible splenic artery embolisation for vascular injury or ongoing bleeding. Unstable patients with suspected splenic haemorrhage require urgent surgery/haemorrhage control; splenic preservation is preferred when safely feasible. After splenectomy, arrange appropriate vaccination, infection-prevention counselling and antibiotic prophylaxis according to UK/local asplenia guidance.

5 · Liver injury

Blunt right upper quadrant trauma can cause hepatic laceration, haemoperitoneum, vascular injury and biliary leak. Stable patients may be managed non-operatively with close monitoring, CT and interventional radiology embolisation where indicated. Instability or peritonitis may require urgent surgery. Delayed complications include bile leak/biloma, abscess and secondary haemorrhage.

6 · Pelvic fracture and retroperitoneal bleeding

High-energy pelvic injury can cause life-threatening venous, arterial or bony haemorrhage, often with little visible blood. Suspect after major mechanism, pelvic pain, deformity or shock. Apply a pelvic binder over the greater trochanters early when pelvic fracture with bleeding is suspected; avoid repeated pelvic springing. Resuscitate with blood products, request trauma/orthopaedic/interventional radiology input, and arrange pelvic packing, angioembolisation or other definitive haemorrhage control as indicated. Examine for urethral injury (blood at meatus, perineal bruising, inability to void) before catheterisation; seek urology advice if suspected.

7 · Hollow viscus and mesenteric injury

Seatbelt marks, abdominal guarding, free intraperitoneal air, unexplained pain or deterioration may suggest bowel perforation or mesenteric tear. CT can miss early injuries. Serial examination and surgical review are essential; peritonitis or deterioration warrants urgent exploration. Give antibiotics when bowel perforation/contamination is suspected according to local protocol.

8 · Renal and urinary tract injury

Flank trauma, haematuria or high-energy deceleration can indicate renal damage. Contrast CT in stable patients, often with delayed excretory phase if urinary tract injury suspected. Macroscopic haematuria is not a reliable marker of injury severity. Blood at urethral meatus or pelvic fracture with urinary retention suggests urethral injury; avoid blind urethral catheterisation pending assessment.

9 · Operative versus non-operative management

Decision hinges on haemodynamics, peritonitis, associated injuries, imaging and available expertise—not CT injury grade alone. Stable solid-organ injuries are frequently managed without surgery under close observation. Persistent instability, peritonitis, hollow-viscus injury or failed non-operative management calls for urgent operative/haemorrhage-control intervention. Damage-control surgery may involve packing, temporary closure and planned re-look.

10 · Secondary survey and follow-up

Perform head-to-toe exam, inspect back/flanks/perineum, reassess abdomen, record serial observations, urine output and haemoglobin trends. Consider occult bleeding, compartment syndrome and VTE prophylaxis timing after haemostasis. Provide clear return precautions for worsening abdominal pain, fainting, fever, vomiting or bleeding. Children and pregnant patients require tailored multidisciplinary pathways.

Clinical decision pathways

Unstable abdominal trauma

<C>ABCDE → major trauma call → major haemorrhage protocol → bedside eFAST if it does not delay treatment → immediate surgery/interventional haemorrhage control according to source → reassess.

Stable abdominal trauma

Primary survey and observations → contrast CT if significant injury suspected → surgical/trauma review → monitored non-operative management or targeted intervention → repeat examination.

Splenic injury

Stable → CT and close observation ± splenic embolisation. Unstable with ongoing bleeding → urgent operative control. Post-splenectomy → vaccines, prophylaxis and sepsis counselling.

Liver injury

Stable → monitored conservative management ± embolisation. Instability/peritonitis → urgent surgical review and control; watch for bile leak.

Suspected pelvic haemorrhage

Pelvic binder over greater trochanters → blood-product resuscitation → trauma/orthopaedic/IR input → pelvic packing/embolisation as indicated. Avoid repeated springing.

Negative FAST but ongoing shock

Do not rule out bleeding: reassess chest, pelvis, retroperitoneum, long bones and abdomen; escalate to definitive diagnosis/control.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Left-sided blunt trauma

A 22-year-old after a collision has LUQ pain, shoulder-tip pain and BP 80/45. Decision: suspect splenic haemorrhage, activate major trauma/haemorrhage pathways and arrange urgent haemorrhage control; do not delay for CT if unstable.

Case 2 — Stable liver laceration

A 35-year-old after a fall has RUQ tenderness but normal perfusion; CT shows a hepatic laceration without ongoing major bleed. Decision: monitored non-operative management with trauma/surgical input and serial reassessment; consider embolisation if vascular bleeding is identified.

Case 3 — Pelvic fracture and shock

A 46-year-old pedestrian struck by a car is hypotensive with pelvic pain. Decision: apply correctly positioned pelvic binder, activate major haemorrhage protocol and involve pelvic trauma/IR teams urgently.

Case 4 — Seatbelt sign and worsening pain

A 29-year-old has abdominal seatbelt bruising; initial CT is equivocal, then guarding and fever develop. Decision: suspect missed bowel/mesenteric injury; urgent repeat surgical assessment and possible exploration.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Abdominal trauma with shock

Station: A 27-year-old motorcyclist has severe left upper abdominal pain after collision, HR 132, BP 86/52 and cool hands.

  1. Introduce yourself briefly, recognise shock and call the trauma team and senior surgeon immediately.
  2. Perform <C>ABCDE; protect airway/cervical spine when indicated, assess ventilation and external bleeding.
  3. Obtain two large-bore IV/IO lines, bloods including FBC, coagulation, gas/lactate, group and crossmatch; activate major haemorrhage protocol.
  4. Use warmed blood products as indicated, prevent hypothermia, monitor and reassess; give TXA if indicated within appropriate timeframe.
  5. Suspect splenic or other intra-abdominal haemorrhage; use eFAST if rapidly available without delaying treatment.
  6. Explain that unstable internal bleeding requires urgent operative or interventional control; do not delay for CT.
  7. Consider pelvic binder if mechanism/exam suggests pelvic fracture; avoid repeated pelvic compression.
  8. Communicate concise SBAR handover, document interventions and reassess continuously.

Rapid revision — 12 essentials

  1. Start with <C>ABCDE and early trauma-team activation.
  2. Normal early blood pressure does not exclude major bleeding.
  3. FAST is a bedside adjunct, not a definitive rule-out test.
  4. Unstable suspected abdominal haemorrhage: do not delay control for CT.
  5. Stable significant abdominal trauma: contrast CT is usually appropriate.
  6. Splenic injury may cause LUQ pain and Kehr sign.
  7. Stable splenic and hepatic injuries often avoid laparotomy.
  8. Consider angioembolisation for selected arterial bleeding.
  9. Pelvic binders belong at the greater trochanters.
  10. Never repeatedly spring an injured pelvis.
  11. Blood at urethral meatus: assess for urethral injury before catheterisation.
  12. Seatbelt sign and evolving peritonitis raise concern for bowel injury.
