Spinal Cord Compression, Cauda Equina Syndrome & Spinal Emergencies

PLAB priority: New bladder, bowel or saddle sensory symptoms may indicate cauda equina syndrome. Suspected cord compression with neurological changes requires urgent imaging and specialist care.

01. Why spinal emergencies matter

Spinal cord compression and cauda equina syndrome (CES) can cause irreversible paralysis and bladder, bowel or sexual dysfunction. Time-critical recognition, immobilisation when instability is suspected, and specialist assessment protect neurological function.

PLAB application: Ask about progression, gait, sphincter symptoms and saddle sensation; escalate immediately.

02. Anatomy and localisation

The spinal cord normally ends around L1–L2 in adults. Below this, lumbar and sacral nerve roots form the cauda equina. Cord lesions cause upper motor neurone signs below the lesion; root lesions often cause lower motor neurone signs.

PLAB application: Differentiate cord-level myelopathy from cauda equina root dysfunction.

03. Malignant spinal cord compression (MSCC)

Metastatic disease can compress the cord or cauda equina, especially in patients with known cancer. New severe progressive back pain, night pain, radicular pain, gait change, weakness or sphincter symptoms are concerning. Cancer may be previously undiagnosed.

PLAB application: Suspected MSCC with neurological signs or symptoms is an oncological emergency.

04. Cauda equina syndrome red flags

CES may cause new urinary retention or altered urinary sensation, overflow incontinence, saddle or perineal sensory changes, bowel dysfunction, sexual dysfunction and bilateral or progressive leg symptoms. Presentations vary; no single symptom reliably rules CES out.

PLAB application: New saddle sensory change or bladder dysfunction with back/leg symptoms needs emergency assessment.

05. Cord compression versus CES

Cord compression may cause a sensory level, hyperreflexia, spasticity and extensor plantar responses; early spinal shock may temporarily reduce reflexes. CES may produce asymmetric radicular pain, leg weakness, reduced reflexes and saddle sensory loss.

PLAB application: Examine carefully, but a normal or incomplete exam must not delay MRI when suspicion is high.

06. Initial emergency assessment

Use ABCDE, assess vital signs and pain, obtain time course and relevant cancer, trauma, infection and anticoagulant history. Document lower limb power, tone, reflexes, sensation, gait when safe, perianal symptoms and bladder function. Seek urgent senior and spinal specialist input.

PLAB application: Do not delay escalation while waiting for a bladder scan or a detailed examination.

07. MRI and imaging urgency

MRI is the investigation of choice for suspected MSCC and CES. NICE NG234 recommends MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 hours for suspected MSCC with neurological signs or symptoms. Suspected CES needs emergency MRI under local spinal pathways; CT cannot reliably exclude it.

PLAB application: An emergency MRI is needed for suspected CES; do not falsely reassure from a normal plain X-ray.

08. Immediate MSCC treatment

In suspected MSCC with neurological signs or symptoms, NICE NG234 recommends dexamethasone 16 mg orally or equivalent parenteral dose as soon as possible, followed by 16 mg daily while awaiting surgery or radiotherapy; specialist review and glucose monitoring are essential.

PLAB application: Steroid advice applies to suspected MSCC, not automatically to every spinal emergency.

09. Definitive MSCC management

Urgent multidisciplinary decisions consider surgical decompression and stabilisation, radiotherapy, prognosis and spinal stability. The MSCC coordinator and oncology/spinal surgery teams coordinate care. Maintain spinal precautions if instability is suspected.

PLAB application: Surgery or radiotherapy is selected by specialists; do not assume radiotherapy for every case.

10. CES treatment

Compressive CES, commonly from a large lumbar disc herniation, requires emergency spinal surgical assessment and decompression when indicated. Earlier recognition reduces the risk of lasting disability.

PLAB application: Do not wait for complete urinary retention or bilateral weakness before referral.

11. Spinal epidural abscess

Consider epidural abscess with severe back pain, fever, neurological deficit, bacteraemia, immunosuppression, diabetes or intravenous drug use. The classic triad is often absent. Obtain urgent MRI, blood cultures and specialist-directed IV antibiotics; surgery may be required.

PLAB application: A patient can have an epidural abscess without fever.

12. Spinal trauma and instability

After trauma, protect the spine according to trauma protocols, perform repeated neurological assessments and arrange appropriate CT for bony injury and MRI when cord/ligament injury is suspected. Neurogenic shock can cause hypotension and bradycardia in high spinal injury.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary movement when an unstable spinal injury is possible.

13. Transverse myelitis and noncompressive myelopathy

Inflammatory cord disease may present with subacute bilateral weakness, sensory level and bladder symptoms. MRI excludes compression and may show cord inflammation; neurology determines further investigations and immunotherapy.

PLAB application: Exclude a compressive lesion before attributing symptoms to inflammatory myelitis.

14. Bladder assessment and examination limitations

Post-void residual volume can support assessment but a low residual does not exclude CES. Digital rectal examination and anal tone have limited sensitivity and must not be used alone to rule out CES. Document subjective urinary and perineal sensory changes.

PLAB application: Never use a single normal examination finding to dismiss suspected CES.

15. Recovery and complications

Potential outcomes include persistent weakness, neuropathic pain, spasticity, urinary retention, bowel dysfunction, sexual dysfunction and pressure injury. Rehabilitation involves physiotherapy, occupational therapy, bladder/bowel support and psychological care.

PLAB application: Early rehabilitation and a clear follow-up plan are important after acute treatment.

16. PLAB 2 approach and safety-netting

Ask sensitively about urinary stream, retention, altered sensation when wiping, genital numbness and sexual function. Explain that urgent MRI checks for pressure on nerves and that delay may cause permanent harm. For new red flags, advise immediate emergency department assessment.

PLAB application: Use plain language, preserve dignity and document explicit red-flag advice.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Cord versus root anatomy

The spinal cord ends near L1–L2, while cauda equina roots descend in the lumbar canal. Cord lesions often affect long tracts and produce a sensory level; root lesions may cause asymmetric radicular and sphincter symptoms.

Why CES symptoms are variable

Sacral root involvement can initially alter urinary sensation before frank retention. Symptoms and examinations vary, so a normal anal tone or low post-void residual cannot safely rule out CES.

Why malignant compression needs speed

Tumour or vertebral collapse can compromise blood flow and directly injure the cord. Early dexamethasone in neurologically symptomatic MSCC reduces oedema while definitive specialist treatment is organised.

MRI versus CT

MRI visualises neural structures, discs, epidural lesions and cord signal. CT is useful for bony injury, particularly trauma, but is not a reliable stand-alone exclusion test for CES.

Infection and inflammatory mimics

Epidural abscess may lack fever; transverse myelitis may resemble compression. Imaging and clinical context distinguish surgically treatable compression from inflammatory causes.

Communication and documentation

Ask about saddle sensation, urinary sensation, bowel and sexual function using neutral language. Record exact onset, progression, examination, escalation time and safety-net advice.
