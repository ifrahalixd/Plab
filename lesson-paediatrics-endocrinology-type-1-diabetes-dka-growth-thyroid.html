Paediatric Endocrinology: Type 1 Diabetes, DKA, Growth Disorders & Thyroid Disease

Emergency: Suspected new type 1 diabetes requires same-day paediatric assessment. Vomiting, Kussmaul breathing, altered consciousness or significant ketones suggest DKA and need emergency treatment.

Approach to paediatric endocrine presentations

Ask about growth trajectory, weight change, thirst, urine output, appetite, fatigue, pubertal timing, medication and family history. Review serial measurements rather than a single centile. Examine hydration, consciousness, growth pattern, thyroid and pubertal signs.

PLAB application: An unwell child with polyuria, polydipsia and weight loss needs immediate capillary glucose and ketone testing.

Type 1 diabetes: recognition

Autoimmune beta-cell destruction causes absolute insulin deficiency. Children may present with the four Ts: toilet (polyuria or bedwetting), thirsty, tired and thinner. Younger children may deteriorate rapidly, and diabetes can first present as ketoacidosis.

PLAB application: Do not label new bedwetting plus thirst as behavioural without checking glucose.

Initial investigation of suspected diabetes

Check capillary blood glucose and blood or urine ketones immediately; confirm and classify through paediatric diabetes services. Assess vital signs, hydration and mental state. A child with suspected new-onset type 1 diabetes requires same-day specialist assessment.

PLAB application: Do not delay referral while awaiting fasting blood tests or HbA1c.

Diabetic ketoacidosis: diagnostic principles

DKA involves hyperglycaemia, ketosis and metabolic acidosis; UK paediatric protocols use blood glucose, blood ketones and venous pH or bicarbonate to confirm and grade severity. Euglycaemic or near-euglycaemic ketoacidosis can occur in selected circumstances.

PLAB application: Use the current local BSPED/paediatric DKA guideline rather than adult DKA protocols.

DKA clinical presentation and red flags

Vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, tachycardia, deep Kussmaul breathing and fruity breath may occur. Reduced consciousness, headache, bradycardia or neurological deterioration raise concern for cerebral injury. Shock and severe electrolyte disturbance are emergencies.

PLAB application: DKA can mimic an acute abdomen or respiratory disease.

DKA immediate management

Escalate to senior paediatric and diabetes teams, perform ABCDE, establish IV access, check glucose, ketones, venous blood gas, electrolytes and ECG when indicated. Give carefully calculated fluids and IV insulin according to a paediatric protocol; monitor glucose, ketones, potassium and neurological status closely.

PLAB application: Avoid an adult fixed-rate DKA pathway and do not give an insulin bolus routinely.

Cerebral injury during DKA treatment

Cerebral injury may present with new headache, irritability, confusion, reduced GCS, abnormal breathing or focal signs. Escalate immediately, follow emergency neuroprotective measures and the local protocol for hyperosmolar therapy; do not delay treatment for imaging.

PLAB application: Frequent neurological observations are essential throughout DKA management.

Long-term type 1 diabetes care

Management includes basal-bolus insulin or pump systems, continuous glucose monitoring where suitable, carbohydrate counting, sick-day rules, ketone monitoring, hypoglycaemia prevention and multidisciplinary review. Families need school support and a clear emergency plan.

PLAB application: Insulin must not be stopped during illness without specialist advice.

Hypoglycaemia in children with diabetes

Symptoms include tremor, sweating, hunger, irritability, behavioural change, confusion and seizures. A conscious child able to swallow needs fast-acting carbohydrate according to their individual care plan; severe hypoglycaemia requires emergency glucagon or IV glucose by trained staff.

PLAB application: Never give oral food or drink to a child with impaired consciousness.

Growth assessment and growth faltering

Plot accurate serial height, weight and BMI on appropriate UK growth charts, accounting for prematurity where relevant. Calculate height velocity and compare with parental target height. Assess nutrition, chronic illness, psychosocial factors and pubertal stage.

PLAB application: Crossing height centiles or poor height velocity matters more than a single short measurement.

Short stature: differential and work-up

Familial short stature and constitutional delay are common variants; pathological causes include hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, growth hormone deficiency, chronic inflammation, renal disease and genetic syndromes such as Turner syndrome. Evaluate proportionality and dysmorphism; tests are guided by clinical assessment.

PLAB application: A short child with declining growth velocity needs paediatric evaluation.

Tall stature and abnormal growth patterns

Tall stature may be familial or associated with obesity, early puberty, genetic syndromes or rare endocrine excess. Consider growth velocity, body proportions, blood pressure, pubertal signs and family heights.

PLAB application: Accelerated linear growth with early secondary sexual characteristics warrants pubertal assessment.

Puberty: normal variation and early puberty

Pubertal development is assessed using Tanner stages, growth acceleration and age of onset. Precocious puberty is commonly considered when signs begin before 8 years in girls or 9 years in boys, but interpretation requires context and specialist review.

PLAB application: Progressive early puberty or neurological symptoms require referral.

Delayed puberty

Assess for constitutional delay, chronic illness, nutritional deficiency, primary gonadal failure and hypothalamic-pituitary disorders. Delayed puberty commonly prompts assessment if there is no breast development by age 13 in girls or testicular enlargement by age 14 in boys.

PLAB application: Ask about growth trajectory, family pubertal timing and chronic disease.

Hypothyroidism and congenital hypothyroidism

Congenital hypothyroidism is detected through newborn blood spot screening in the UK and requires prompt treatment to protect neurodevelopment. Acquired hypothyroidism may cause slowing of growth, fatigue, constipation, dry skin, cold intolerance and delayed puberty. Test TSH and free T4 when indicated.

PLAB application: Growth slowing with weight gain can suggest hypothyroidism.

Hyperthyroidism and endocrine counselling

Graves disease may cause weight loss despite appetite, tremor, tachycardia, heat intolerance, anxiety and goitre. Confirm with thyroid function tests and refer to paediatric endocrinology. Explain diagnosis, medication monitoring, school implications and safety-netting in child-friendly language.

PLAB application: Tachycardia, fever, agitation and systemic deterioration in suspected thyrotoxicosis require urgent care.

PLAB 2 — Explaining a new diabetes diagnosis

Introduce yourself, explore parent and child concerns, explain that type 1 diabetes is not caused by eating sugar, discuss insulin and glucose monitoring, explain the specialist team and school support, and safety-net for vomiting, ketones, drowsiness and severe hypoglycaemia. Use teach-back and offer written guidance.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Paediatric Endocrinology

DKA and potassium

Total body potassium is depleted even if serum potassium initially appears normal or elevated. Potassium replacement and ECG monitoring depend on renal function, potassium concentration and the current paediatric protocol.

DKA and cerebral injury

Neurological deterioration is a time-critical emergency. Treatment should not wait for CT. Use the institution’s paediatric DKA pathway and seek intensive-care advice.

Differential diagnosis of polyuria

Consider diabetes mellitus, diabetes insipidus, excessive fluid intake and renal concentrating defects. Glucose and ketones are first-line tests when diabetes mellitus is possible.

Growth charts and mid-parental height

Interpret height velocity, pubertal status and genetic target height together. A child may be within the population reference range but still be growing abnormally slowly.

Thyroid screening

Newborn screening identifies congenital hypothyroidism; a normal newborn screen does not exclude later acquired thyroid disease.

Sick-day rules

Families need individual written instructions for insulin, hydration, ketone testing and urgent contact thresholds; illness often increases insulin requirements.
