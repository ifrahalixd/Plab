Acute Rash: diagnose the pattern, protect the patient

A newly appearing rash may be harmless or the first visible sign of life-threatening infection, anaphylaxis or a severe drug reaction. The most useful approach is to assess stability first, then classify morphology and distribution. This presentation covers patients of all ages, with particular attention to children and immunocompromised people.

Article audio
Immediate red flags: airway swelling, wheeze, hypotension; fever and rapidly progressive non-blanching petechiae/purpura; painful blistering or peeling with mouth/eye/genital erosions; skin pain out of proportion, severe sepsis, rapidly spreading necrosis; or a sick infant with an unexplained rash. Treat and escalate first—do not delay for rash classification.

1. Approach the patient before the rash

Use ABCDE in anyone unwell. Record observations, assess perfusion and mental state, and identify possible sepsis. Consider anaphylaxis when there is sudden skin or mucosal involvement with airway, breathing or circulation problems (skin features may be absent). Check for fever, neck stiffness, photophobia, rapidly changing lesions, mucosal erosions and new medicines.

The glass test is supportive: petechiae or purpura which do not fade with pressure can indicate extravasated blood. A blanching eruption does not exclude early meningococcal disease. On darker skin, assess conjunctivae, palms/soles and mucosa carefully, and rely on systemic signs rather than visibility alone.

Describe site, symmetry, distribution, colour, shape, size, evolution, surface and mucosal involvement. Look for flexural versus extensor, dermatomal versus generalised, photo-exposed versus covered, and palm/sole involvement.

3. Focused history: a useful PLAB script

  1. Onset: sudden over minutes, days or weeks? Did fever precede the rash? Does each lesion last under 24 hours or persist?
  2. Symptoms: itch suggests eczema/urticaria; pain, tenderness, mucosal erosions raise concern for severe skin reactions or infection.
  3. Triggers: new prescribed, over-the-counter or herbal medicines in the last 1–8 weeks (including antibiotics, anticonvulsants and allopurinol); foods, stings, topical products or travel.
  4. Exposure: sick contacts, school/nursery, sexual exposures, scabies contacts, recent infections, immunisations, pregnancy and immunosuppression.
  5. Systemic symptoms: fever, breathlessness, stridor, headache, arthralgia, facial swelling, hypotension, dark urine or reduced urine output.
  6. Past episodes: atopy, psoriasis, autoimmune disease, drug allergies; establish photos and whether lesions recur in identical sites.

4. Examination: complete and considerate

Examine the skin with consent and dignity, including scalp, nail folds, web spaces, palms, soles, oral mucosa and eyes where relevant. Assess temperature, pulse, BP, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and hydration. Look for lymphadenopathy, synovitis, jaundice, or focal cellulitis, and examine the chest and abdomen as appropriate. In an ill patient, check serial observations and do not let a normal-looking rash override the clinical picture.

Pattern recognition: transient migratory wheals without systemic compromise suggest urticaria; a unilateral painful dermatomal vesicular eruption suggests shingles; honey-coloured crusting suggests impetigo; itchy web spaces with nocturnal pruritus and contacts suggest scabies; a widespread symmetrical maculopapular eruption after a new drug may be a drug exanthem—unless red flags suggest severe cutaneous adverse reaction.

5. Tests: choose according to risk

Well patient, typical benign rash: diagnosis is often clinical; routine blood tests are not obligatory. Suspected sepsis/meningococcal disease: immediate hospital assessment, cultures and relevant laboratory tests as per emergency guidance, without delaying time-critical treatment. Possible severe drug reaction: FBC with eosinophils, U&Es, LFTs, inflammatory markers and specialist dermatology assessment; cultures and biopsy where indicated. Petechiae/purpura: review platelet count and coagulation and consider vasculitis. Suspected bacterial infection: microbiology depends on severity and local protocol. Unclear blistering/vasculitic disease: specialist skin biopsy ± direct immunofluorescence.

6. Initial management by syndrome

Eczema / contact dermatitisIdentify irritants, provide appropriate emollients, consider topical corticosteroids of suitable potency for the site/age and evaluate secondary infection only when clinically indicated.
UrticariaIdentify triggers, assess for anaphylaxis; a non-sedating H1 antihistamine is often used for uncomplicated urticaria, with specialist follow-up for persistent disease.
ImpetigoCheck extent, bullae, systemic symptoms and local antimicrobial guidance. NICE NG153 advises targeted topical or oral treatment based on disease type/severity; avoid unnecessary combinations.
Cellulitis / erysipelasUsually unilateral warmth, tenderness, swelling and erythema; use NICE NG141/local antibiotic guidance. Rapid progression or systemic illness requires escalation; reconsider DVT, dermatitis or necrotising infection.
ScabiesTreat the patient and relevant close contacts simultaneously using local guidance; provide decontamination and post-treatment itch advice. Consider crusted scabies in immunocompromised patients.
Drug eruptionEstablish a precise medication timeline, examine for fever, pain and mucosal disease; stop suspected culprit only with appropriate clinical oversight, document reaction and arrange specialist advice when severe.

7. Dermatological and systemic emergencies

Meningococcal disease: a febrile, toxic patient with rapidly progressive petechiae or purpura needs emergency hospital transfer and time-critical management per NICE NG240. The rash may be late or absent.

Anaphylaxis: treat as a clinical emergency following current Resuscitation Council UK/ local guidance; intramuscular adrenaline is first-line for true anaphylaxis, not antihistamines alone.

Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis: often develops after a medicine exposure; painful erythematous or dusky lesions, blistering, epidermal detachment and mouth/eye/genital erosions require immediate hospital dermatology and acute care. DRESS: delayed drug reaction with rash, fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy, eosinophilia and possible liver/kidney injury; urgent evaluation is needed.

Necrotising soft-tissue infection: severe disproportionate pain, rapid progression, shock, skin discolouration or bullae are emergency surgical red flags; crepitus may be absent. Eczema herpeticum: rapidly spreading painful monomorphic vesicles with fever in eczema warrants urgent antiviral assessment, especially near the eyes.

8. Five worked clinical cases

Case A · fever and non-blanching rashA 4-year-old is lethargic, febrile, mottled, with new petechiae. Priority: suspect meningococcal disease/sepsis, initiate emergency pathway; no reassurance based on the rash alone.
Case B · itchy wheals after food24-year-old develops widespread moving wheals and lip swelling, then wheeze and dizziness. Priority: anaphylaxis and airway/circulatory assessment; prompt IM adrenaline per protocol.
Case C · rash after new medicine53-year-old started allopurinol weeks ago; now has fever, widespread painful rash and mouth ulcers. Priority: serious drug reaction including SJS/TEN; immediate hospital specialist assessment and medication review.
Case D · painful red hot leg68-year-old has unilateral warmth, spreading redness and fever. Reasoning: cellulitis likely; assess severity, treat appropriately and actively exclude necrotising infection if pain severe or progression rapid.
Case E · nocturnal itchy papulesSeveral household members have itchy wrists, web spaces and waistlines. Reasoning: scabies; examine distribution and treat contacts together with practical instructions.

9. PLAB 2 OSCE: explain the rash and safety-net

“A rash can have several causes. I first want to check whether you are unwell and whether you have any breathing problems, fever, blistering or mouth ulcers. Then I will look at the pattern, ask about recent medicines and possible contacts, and decide whether any tests or treatment are needed.” Offer a chaperone when appropriate, explain uncertainty, avoid assuming a skin colour-based diagnostic appearance, and check understanding.

Safety-net: urgent assessment for rapid worsening, fever with systemic illness, non-blanching rash, new breathing trouble, swelling of mouth or throat, skin pain, blistering or peeling, eye pain or mucosal sores.

Practical discriminators

Wheal versus fixed lesion: urticarial wheals are transient and migratory; fixed purpura, painful dusky plaques or blistering should prompt alternative diagnoses. Pruritus versus pain: itch is common in dermatitis and scabies, whereas skin pain with fever or mucosal involvement is concerning. Blanching versus non-blanching: non-blanching purpura raises concern for haemorrhage into skin; interpretation requires the clinical picture and platelet/vascular assessment. Drug timing: uncomplicated morbilliform eruptions and severe cutaneous adverse reactions have different timing and systemic features; never label the latter as a simple allergy.

Pathophysiology / advanced reasoning

Deep Dive: the biology behind rash patterns

1. Why does a rash blanch?

Erythema reflects increased blood flow within superficial cutaneous vessels; pressure temporarily empties these vessels, causing blanching. Petechiae and purpura arise from blood that has escaped vessels and therefore do not blanch. The distinction helps characterise lesions but never replaces assessment for sepsis.

2. Urticaria and anaphylaxis are not the same

Urticaria is transient dermal oedema caused by mast-cell mediator release, often including histamine. Anaphylaxis is a severe systemic hypersensitivity reaction involving airway, breathing and/or circulation compromise; it may occur with or without visible skin signs. Treating skin itch alone does not treat airway or circulatory collapse.

3. Hypersensitivity and serious drug reactions

Uncomplicated exanthematous drug eruptions are often delayed immune-mediated responses. SJS/TEN involves widespread keratinocyte death and epidermal detachment, commonly linked to medication exposure. DRESS is a delayed multi-organ drug hypersensitivity syndrome; liver, kidney or other involvement can be life-threatening. Distinguish these by timing, fever, pain, mucosa and organ dysfunction.

4. How pathogens create different morphology

Viruses can produce widespread immune-mediated exanthems; herpesviruses cause grouped vesicles; impetigo creates superficial pustules and crusts; scabies produces itchy papules and burrows. Invasive infection and coagulation abnormalities can cause purpura through vascular injury and haemorrhage.

5. Why skin tone matters

Erythema can appear red, violaceous, greyish or subtle depending on pigmentation and lighting. Distribution, warmth, swelling, texture, tenderness, systemic signs and mucosal or conjunctival findings often provide more reliable clues than colour alone. Ask patients whether the colour differs from their usual skin tone.

6. Diagnostic traps

A “viral rash” in an ill infant; a “drug allergy” with mucosal erosions; “cellulitis” when pain is disproportionate; or “simple hives” with respiratory compromise are dangerous anchoring errors. Continue reassessing if symptoms change or treatment fails.
