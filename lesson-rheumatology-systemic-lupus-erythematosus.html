Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

PLAB priority: Check urine, renal function and blood pressure in suspected SLE. Fever on immunosuppression and new organ dysfunction need urgent assessment.

01. What is SLE?

SLE is a chronic, relapsing autoimmune disease with autoantibodies, immune-complex inflammation and variable multi-organ involvement. Severity ranges from skin and joint symptoms to kidney, neurological or haematological disease.

PLAB application: Think multisystem disease in a young adult with inflammatory symptoms, rashes and cytopenias.

02. Epidemiology and risk

SLE occurs predominantly in women of childbearing age but affects all sexes and ages. Genetic predisposition, hormonal influences, ultraviolet light and certain medicines contribute.

PLAB application: Avoid ruling out SLE because the patient is male or older.

03. Clinical history

Ask about fatigue, fever, weight change, joint pain, photosensitivity, mouth ulcers, alopecia, pleuritic chest pain, breathlessness, oedema, frothy urine, seizures, thrombosis and miscarriages. Review medicines and family history.

PLAB application: Screen systematically for organ-threatening features.

04. Skin and mucosal signs

A malar rash often spares nasolabial folds. Other findings include discoid lesions, photosensitive rashes, painless oral or nasal ulcers and non-scarring alopecia. Rashes require clinical assessment because mimics are common.

PLAB application: Discuss sun protection and evaluate new rashes.

05. Musculoskeletal manifestations

Arthralgia and inflammatory, often symmetrical, non-erosive arthritis are common. Jaccoud arthropathy can cause reducible deformities. Persistent joint swelling may also reflect another arthritis.

PLAB application: Differentiate SLE from rheumatoid arthritis and infection.

06. Renal involvement

Lupus nephritis may cause proteinuria, haematuria, hypertension, falling eGFR or oedema, and can be clinically silent. Check urine dipstick, urine protein:creatinine ratio, creatinine and blood pressure. Kidney biopsy often guides specialist treatment.

PLAB application: Never rely on symptoms alone to exclude nephritis.

07. Haematological disease

Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, leukopenia, lymphopenia and thrombocytopenia can occur. Antiphospholipid antibodies may coexist and increase thrombotic or pregnancy risks.

PLAB application: Interpret FBC alongside clinical features and consider drug-related cytopenias.

08. Cardiopulmonary and neurological disease

Pleuritis, pericarditis and other cardiopulmonary disease occur. Seizures, psychosis, stroke and other neuropsychiatric syndromes require urgent assessment and evaluation for alternative causes.

PLAB application: Chest pain, new focal neurological signs or seizures warrant emergency assessment.

09. Antibodies and complement

ANA is sensitive but not specific: a positive ANA alone does not diagnose SLE. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Sm can support diagnosis; anti-dsDNA titres and low C3/C4 may help assess activity in context. Anti-Ro/SSA and antiphospholipid antibodies inform specific risks.

PLAB application: Interpret tests with the clinical picture, not as standalone diagnostic labels.

10. Initial investigations

Order FBC, renal function, liver tests, ESR/CRP, urinalysis, urine protein quantification, ANA with targeted autoantibodies and C3/C4 when indicated. Examine for infection, medication toxicity and organ involvement.

PLAB application: A normal CRP does not exclude active lupus; marked CRP elevation should prompt infection assessment.

11. Diagnosis and classification

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by serology and organ assessment. The 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria use a positive ANA as an entry criterion but were designed for classification, not to replace clinical diagnosis.

PLAB application: Refer suspected SLE to rheumatology rather than applying classification scores in isolation.

12. General management

Care is tailored to disease severity and organ involvement. Hydroxychloroquine is commonly recommended unless contraindicated, with appropriate eye monitoring. Minimise glucocorticoid exposure where possible; immunosuppressants and biologics are specialist-led.

PLAB application: Review adherence, infection risk, vaccination and medication monitoring.

13. Severe disease and lupus nephritis

Active nephritis or major CNS, pulmonary or haematological disease requires urgent specialist input. Treatment may involve glucocorticoids plus mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide or other specialist-selected therapies; choice depends on disease pattern and patient factors.

PLAB application: Do not manage suspected organ-threatening lupus solely in primary care.

14. Antiphospholipid syndrome

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) involves qualifying thrombosis or pregnancy morbidity with persistent antiphospholipid antibodies on repeat testing at least 12 weeks apart. Positive antibodies alone do not establish APS.

PLAB application: Ask about DVT, PE, stroke and recurrent pregnancy complications.

15. Pregnancy and contraception

Plan pregnancy during stable disease with preconception rheumatology and obstetric input. Hydroxychloroquine is often continued; methotrexate and mycophenolate are teratogenic and require specialist preconception changes. Anti-Ro/SSA and APS status may alter monitoring.

PLAB application: Never advise abruptly stopping prescribed treatment; arrange specialist medication review.

16. Monitoring, counselling and safety net

Monitor symptoms, blood pressure, urinalysis, kidney function, blood counts and relevant serology. Encourage sun protection, cardiovascular risk reduction, smoking cessation and bone health. Seek urgent care for chest pain, breathlessness, severe headache, seizures, reduced urine or fever while immunosuppressed.

PLAB application: In PLAB 2 explain relapsing disease, follow-up and urgent red flags in plain language.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Immune-complex pathology

Loss of self-tolerance leads to autoantibody production and immune-complex deposition. Complement activation and tissue inflammation can affect skin, kidneys, joints and serosal surfaces.

Lupus nephritis

Immune-mediated glomerular inflammation causes proteinuria, haematuria and sometimes renal impairment. Histological classification from kidney biopsy guides treatment; clinically silent disease is possible.

Interpreting serology

ANA has high sensitivity but low specificity. Anti-dsDNA, anti-Sm, C3/C4 and other tests support a clinical assessment; individual results cannot independently establish or rule out SLE.

Flare versus infection

Fever, fatigue and inflammatory markers may overlap. Immunosuppression raises infection risk; infection must be actively considered before escalating immunosuppression.

Antiphospholipid syndrome

Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibodies can confer thrombosis or obstetric risk. Persistently positive tests and compatible clinical events are required for APS.

Treatment reasoning

Hydroxychloroquine supports disease control in many patients. Corticosteroids and immunomodulators depend on severity, organ involvement, fertility plans and infection risks; organ-threatening disease is specialist managed.
