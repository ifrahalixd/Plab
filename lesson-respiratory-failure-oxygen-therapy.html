Respiratory Failure & Oxygen Therapy: diagnosis, airway clearance and exacerbations

Emergency reminder: Major haemoptysis, severe breathlessness, confusion or sepsis requires emergency assessment and escalation. This lesson is educational, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

Definition and classification

Respiratory failure occurs when the respiratory system cannot maintain adequate oxygenation and/or ventilation. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis defines the disturbance.

Clinical application: Treat the patient, not an isolated number. Assess ABCDE, work of breathing, consciousness, oxygen saturation and perfusion.

Type 1: hypoxaemic respiratory failure

Type 1 respiratory failure is low arterial oxygen (PaO₂ <8 kPa on room air) with normal or low PaCO₂. Causes include pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, ARDS and pulmonary embolism.

Clinical application: Oxygenation failure commonly reflects ventilation–perfusion mismatch or shunt. Investigate and treat the cause.

Type 2: hypercapnic respiratory failure

Type 2 respiratory failure features raised PaCO₂ (>6 kPa), usually with hypoxaemia. Causes include COPD exacerbation, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular weakness and sedative toxicity.

Clinical application: Acidosis (pH <7.35) indicates acute or acute-on-chronic ventilatory failure. Interpret pH and bicarbonate to assess compensation.

Recognising deterioration

Red flags include severe work of breathing, inability to speak, exhaustion, cyanosis, altered consciousness, shock and a falling respiratory rate in an exhausted patient.

Clinical application: Call for senior and critical-care help early. Monitor closely; pulse oximetry does not measure carbon dioxide.

ABCDE assessment

Check airway patency, respiratory rate and effort, chest movement and breath sounds, oxygen saturation, circulation, consciousness, temperature and glucose as appropriate.

Clinical application: Obtain ABG when indicated without delaying life-saving treatment. Attach monitoring and establish IV access if unwell.

Arterial blood gas interpretation

Read pH, PaCO₂, bicarbonate/base excess, PaO₂, lactate and inspired oxygen concentration. Decide whether the primary problem is oxygenation, ventilation or both.

Clinical application: ABG values depend on oxygen delivery at sampling. Document FiO₂/device, timing and clinical context.

Oxygen is a drug

Prescribe an oxygen target range and delivery device, then titrate to measured saturation. For most acutely ill adults without hypercapnic risk, aim 94–98%.

Clinical application: Patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure commonly start with 88–92% pending blood gases and individual assessment. Do not withhold oxygen in life-threatening hypoxaemia.

Oxygen delivery devices

Nasal cannulae and simple face masks deliver variable inspired oxygen. Venturi masks provide more controlled concentrations. Reservoir masks deliver high-concentration oxygen in emergencies.

Clinical application: Choose device according to severity and target; check mask fit, flow requirements and reassess frequently.

Why oxygen can worsen hypercapnia

In susceptible patients, excess oxygen may increase PaCO₂, particularly via worsening ventilation–perfusion matching and the Haldane effect.

Clinical application: Use controlled oxygen, obtain blood gases and reassess. Oxygen is still essential when the patient is hypoxaemic.

High-flow nasal oxygen

Heated humidified high-flow nasal oxygen can support selected patients with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure by delivering high flow and controlled FiO₂.

Clinical application: Use in monitored settings with clear escalation criteria. It is not a substitute for ventilatory support when ventilation fails.

Non-invasive ventilation

NIV provides positive-pressure ventilatory support through a mask. It is established for selected patients with acute acidotic hypercapnic respiratory failure, especially COPD exacerbation.

Clinical application: Consider after optimal initial therapy when pH remains <7.35 and PaCO₂ is raised, according to local protocol. Assess contraindications and tolerance.

CPAP versus bilevel NIV

CPAP provides a single continuous positive airway pressure, supporting oxygenation and alveolar recruitment. Bilevel NIV adds inspiratory pressure support to improve ventilation.

Clinical application: Different indications apply; discuss with the respiratory/critical-care team and use local pathways.

When invasive ventilation may be needed

Inability to protect the airway, severe exhaustion, refractory hypoxaemia, deteriorating consciousness or failure of non-invasive support can necessitate intubation.

Clinical application: Urgent anaesthetic/critical-care assessment is required; avoid delaying escalation while repeating ineffective treatments.

ARDS

Acute respiratory distress syndrome is an acute inflammatory lung injury with hypoxaemia and bilateral pulmonary opacities not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload.

Clinical application: Manage the underlying cause, support oxygenation and involve critical care early. Lung-protective ventilation is a critical-care intervention.

Cause-directed investigations

Consider chest X-ray, ECG, blood count, inflammatory markers, cultures, renal profile, troponin or PE investigations as clinically indicated.

Clinical application: Differentials include pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, pneumothorax, PE, asthma, COPD and drug-related hypoventilation.

PLAB 2 communication and handover

Explain that oxygen levels or carbon dioxide removal are inadequate and urgent monitoring/treatment is needed. Use SBAR for escalation.

Clinical application: Document target saturation, oxygen device, ABG results, response to therapy and ceiling-of-care decisions made with senior input.

PLAB 2: emergency recognition and explanation

  1. ABCDE and immediate escalation for respiratory exhaustion, confusion or severe hypoxaemia.
  2. Explain oxygenation versus ventilation and why blood gases are needed.
  3. Set a target oxygen range and select appropriate delivery device.
  4. Discuss cause-directed investigations and potential NIV/critical-care assessment.
  5. Handover using SBAR and document reassessment.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Oxygenation versus ventilation

Oxygenation depends on alveolar oxygen, diffusion, shunt and V/Q matching. Ventilation removes CO₂; a patient can have failure of either or both.

V/Q mismatch and shunt

Perfusion of poorly ventilated lung reduces arterial oxygen. True shunt responds less well to increased inspired oxygen than simple V/Q mismatch.

Acute versus chronic hypercapnia

Renal bicarbonate retention compensates over time. A low pH with high PaCO₂ suggests an acute component; a near-normal pH with high bicarbonate can reflect chronic compensation.

Why controlled oxygen matters

Excess oxygen can worsen CO₂ retention in susceptible patients. However, critical hypoxaemia must be corrected immediately.

NIV physiology

Inspiratory pressure support augments tidal volume and CO₂ clearance, while expiratory pressure helps maintain airway patency and alveolar recruitment.

ARDS physiology

Diffuse alveolar-capillary injury produces oedema, low compliance and shunt; critical-care teams use lung-protective strategies.
