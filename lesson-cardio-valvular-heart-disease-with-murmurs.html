1. Overview and anatomy

Four valves maintain one-way blood flow: mitral (LA→LV), aortic (LV→aorta), tricuspid (RA→RV), and pulmonary (RV→pulmonary artery). Stenosis obstructs forward flow; regurgitation permits backward flow. Chronic pressure overload produces concentric hypertrophy; chronic volume overload causes chamber dilation and eccentric remodelling.

Core principle: Describe a murmur by timing, site, radiation, intensity, pitch, and response to manoeuvres—but diagnose severity using echocardiography, not auscultation alone.

2. Symptoms, red flags and bedside assessment

Ask about exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, ankle swelling, exercise tolerance, chest pain, syncope, palpitations, fever, embolic symptoms, past rheumatic fever, congenital disease, prior valve surgery and family history. Examine pulse character, blood pressure, JVP, apex beat, thrills, heart sounds, lung bases and peripheral oedema.

Urgent escalation: acute pulmonary oedema, hypotension/shock, new murmur with fever and suspected endocarditis, syncope with suspected severe aortic stenosis, or sudden severe regurgitation after MI or aortic dissection. Acute severe regurgitation may have a surprisingly soft murmur.

3. Aortic stenosis (AS)

Common causes are age-related calcific disease, congenital bicuspid aortic valve and, less commonly in the UK, rheumatic disease. LV pressure overload causes hypertrophy and eventually failure.

  • Symptoms: exertional breathlessness, angina and syncope/presyncope; severe AS may be asymptomatic.
  • Examination: harsh ejection systolic murmur at the right upper sternal edge radiating to the carotids, slow-rising low-volume pulse, soft/absent A2 in advanced disease.
  • Tests: transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) for peak velocity, mean gradient, valve area, LV function and associated disease. Discordant measurements require specialist interpretation.
  • Management: symptomatic severe AS generally requires specialist assessment for valve intervention (surgical AVR or TAVI based on age, anatomy, operative risk and shared decision-making). No drug reverses calcific AS.

Exam trap: Do not treat symptomatic severe AS with exercise testing without specialist supervision.

4. Aortic regurgitation (AR)

AR results from leaflet disease or aortic-root dilation. Causes include bicuspid valve, aortic-root disease, infective endocarditis and acute aortic dissection. Chronic AR produces LV volume overload; acute AR can cause abrupt pulmonary oedema and shock.

  • Examination: early diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal edge (often best sitting forward in expiration); chronic severe AR may cause a wide pulse pressure and collapsing pulse.
  • Investigations: TTE for mechanism, regurgitation severity, LV dimensions/function and aortic-root size; CT/MRI if aortic pathology needs further assessment.
  • Management: cardiology surveillance for chronic disease; valve surgery when symptomatic severe AR or relevant LV changes meet specialist criteria. Acute severe AR is a surgical emergency.

5. Mitral regurgitation (MR)

Primary MR reflects leaflet/chordal disease (e.g., degenerative prolapse, rheumatic disease, endocarditis). Secondary MR occurs when LV or atrial remodelling prevents leaflet coaptation.

  • Examination: pansystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla; a displaced hyperdynamic apex may indicate chronic severe disease.
  • Complications: AF, pulmonary hypertension, heart failure and LV dysfunction.
  • Management: TTE defines mechanism/severity. Severe primary MR may need valve repair (preferred when durable) or replacement. Secondary MR requires optimal heart-failure treatment first, with selected patients assessed for intervention.
Acute MR: papillary-muscle rupture after MI or chordal rupture may cause shock/pulmonary oedema; urgent echocardiography and cardiothoracic input.

6. Mitral stenosis (MS)

Most commonly rheumatic. Left atrial pressure rises, leading to pulmonary congestion, pulmonary hypertension and AF.

  • Symptoms: exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, haemoptysis, palpitations and embolic events.
  • Examination: loud S1, opening snap and low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at apex, best heard in left lateral position with bell.
  • Management: TTE measures valve area and gradient; diuretics relieve congestion, rate control may help; suitable rheumatic valves can undergo percutaneous mitral commissurotomy. Severe symptomatic disease otherwise requires specialist intervention.
  • Anticoagulation: AF with moderate-to-severe rheumatic MS generally requires a vitamin K antagonist (warfarin), not a DOAC; verify current guidance and individual indications.

7. Right-sided valve disease and prosthetic valves

Tricuspid regurgitation: commonly functional from RV/annular dilation; pansystolic murmur at lower left sternal edge that increases with inspiration (Carvallo sign), prominent JVP v waves, pulsatile liver and oedema. Treat underlying cause and assess for intervention when severe.

Pulmonary stenosis: ejection systolic murmur at upper left sternal edge; congenital disease is common. Pulmonary regurgitation: may follow repaired congenital disease or pulmonary hypertension.

Mechanical prosthetic valves: require lifelong vitamin K antagonist anticoagulation; DOACs are contraindicated. Bioprosthetic valves: antithrombotic plan varies by valve, procedure and comorbidity. New symptoms or altered prosthetic sounds warrant urgent evaluation.

9. UK management and referrals

1. Suspect valve disease. Characterise symptoms and murmur; assess haemodynamic stability and heart-failure signs.
2. Arrange echocardiography. NICE NG208 recommends echo for adults with a murmur and suspected valve disease based on symptoms, signs or abnormal ECG; urgent assessment for concerning presentations.
3. Refer to a heart-valve team. Symptomatic moderate/severe disease, severe disease even if asymptomatic, or LV dysfunction requires specialist decisions about surveillance and intervention.
4. Treat complications safely. Manage AF, congestion and heart failure according to relevant guidelines. Drug therapy does not replace intervention for severe mechanical valve disease.
5. Shared decisions and follow-up. Discuss SAVR versus TAVI or repair versus replacement where relevant, pregnancy plans, anticoagulation, dental health, activity and follow-up imaging.

Endocarditis prevention: NICE CG64 does not recommend antibiotic prophylaxis routinely for dental procedures; emphasise oral health and prompt assessment of unexplained fever. Specific specialist advice may differ in selected circumstances.

10. PLAB 2 and clinical cases

Case 1: exertional syncope and carotid-radiating murmur

Suspect symptomatic aortic stenosis. Assess stability, ECG and urgent echo/cardiology review. Explain that the valve may be narrowed and further tests determine whether intervention is needed.

Case 2: sudden breathlessness 3 days after MI

Acute MR from papillary-muscle rupture is a key concern. ABCDE, senior/cardiology escalation, urgent bedside echo and cardiothoracic discussion.

Case 3: rheumatic MS with new AF

Assess stability, rate and heart-failure symptoms; arrange echo and specialist anticoagulation assessment. Warfarin is generally used for AF with moderate-to-severe rheumatic MS.

OSCE: explain a heart murmur

“I can hear an extra sound as blood moves through your heart. It does not tell us by itself how serious the problem is. An ultrasound scan of the heart can show how the valves open and close. We will discuss the results and the next steps together.” Check understanding and safety-net for chest pain, fainting or worsening breathlessness.

Valvular heart disease · Deep Dive

Cardiac cycle and murmur timing

Systole runs from S1 to S2. AS and pulmonary stenosis cause systolic ejection murmurs; MR and TR cause holosystolic murmurs. AR produces early diastolic flow back into the LV; MS produces mid-diastolic turbulent filling into the LV.

Pressure–volume physiology

Stenosis increases the pressure gradient across a valve. Aortic stenosis raises LV afterload, driving concentric hypertrophy. Regurgitation increases end-diastolic volume and wall stress, driving chamber dilation over time.

Haemodynamics

In severe AS, gradient depends on flow as well as valve area; low-flow states can obscure severity. In acute MR, noncompliant left atrium causes rapid pulmonary venous pressure rise and pulmonary oedema.

Pharmacology and anticoagulation

Diuretics relieve congestion but do not correct valve anatomy. Anticoagulation decisions depend on AF, rheumatic MS and prosthetic valve type. Mechanical valves require vitamin K antagonists; DOACs are not suitable.

Pathology and genetics

Degenerative calcification, rheumatic commissural fusion, endocarditis destruction and myxomatous degeneration have different mechanisms. Bicuspid aortic valve can be familial and is associated with aortopathy.

OSCE technique

Inspect, palpate pulse and apex, auscultate all valve areas, use bell at apex in left lateral position for MS and diaphragm with patient leaning forward for AR; assess radiation, dynamic changes and signs of heart failure.
