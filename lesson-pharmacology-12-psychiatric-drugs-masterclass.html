Psychiatric Pharmacology · Deep Dive

Receptor occupancy and time course

Serotonin transporter occupancy occurs soon after an SSRI dose, but symptomatic improvement generally takes longer. Neuroadaptation, altered stress circuitry and neuroplasticity are implicated. Avoid interpreting lack of immediate improvement as treatment failure.

Dopamine pathways

Mesolimbic dopamine modulation relates to antipsychotic efficacy; nigrostriatal D2 blockade contributes to EPS; tuberoinfundibular blockade increases prolactin. Histamine H1, muscarinic and alpha-1 receptor effects explain many sedation, anticholinergic and postural adverse effects.

Pharmacokinetics and interactions

Fluoxetine and its active metabolite persist for weeks and can complicate switching. CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 interactions affect several psychotropics. Cigarette smoke (not nicotine) induces CYP1A2, influencing clozapine concentrations. Lithium is renally cleared and sensitive to sodium/water balance and interacting medicines.

Why lithium monitoring matters

Lithium distributes into body water and has a narrow safety margin. Levels must be interpreted with dose timing, formulation, kidney function, hydration and symptoms. An apparently modest concentration does not exclude clinically significant toxicity in a symptomatic patient. Follow current monitoring schedules and local laboratory advice.

Antipsychotic monitoring rationale

Metabolic risk is related partly to appetite and insulin signalling; monitor weight, glycaemic markers and lipids. QT risk is greater with some agents and interacting drugs. Movement-disorder assessment identifies EPS and tardive dyskinesia before they cause major harm.

Overdose and risk assessment

Tricyclic overdose can cause sodium-channel blockade with QRS widening, arrhythmias and seizures. Assess suicide risk and limit dispensing quantities where clinically appropriate. A normal early presentation does not reliably exclude serious toxicity; suspected overdose needs urgent assessment.

OSCE decision making

For each psychiatric medicine: explain indication and alternatives, assess capacity and consent, screen for self-harm, discuss adverse effects and monitoring, and give specific safety-net advice. Document shared decisions and follow-up.

Psychiatric Pharmacology · Clinical Masterclass

60-second clinical summary

Match the medicine to the syndrome and patient. SSRIs are common first-line antidepressants but assess bipolar history and suicide risk. Antipsychotics require metabolic and movement monitoring. Lithium requires careful renal, thyroid and serum-level monitoring. Recognise serotonin syndrome, NMS and clozapine bowel toxicity promptly. Benzodiazepines carry dependence and respiratory-depression risks.

1. Clinical prescribing framework

Establish the diagnosis, severity, suicide and self-harm risk, substance use, bipolar history, comorbid illness, current medicines, pregnancy potential and patient preferences. Use psychological and social interventions alongside medicines when indicated. Agree target symptoms, expected benefits, review date, monitoring and discontinuation plan. A medicine is not a substitute for urgent assessment when someone is acutely unsafe.

2. Neurotransmitters and receptor targets

Serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine regulate mood, arousal, motivation and psychosis-related signalling. GABA-A receptors mediate fast inhibitory transmission. Antidepressant benefits often lag behind transporter blockade because downstream receptor, network and neuroplastic changes take time. Antipsychotic D2 antagonism or partial agonism alters dopaminergic signalling; 5-HT2A antagonism is common in several second-generation drugs.

3. Depression: choosing an antidepressant

For depression requiring medication, an SSRI such as sertraline is often considered because of tolerability and safety profile, but selection depends on interactions, previous response, overdose risk and the current NICE pathway. Discuss that improvement can take several weeks, early adverse effects may precede benefit, and close follow-up is needed, particularly for younger people or anyone with suicide risk. Screen for previous mania/hypomania before prescribing.

4. SSRIs: sertraline, fluoxetine, citalopram and escitalopram

SSRIs inhibit the serotonin transporter (SERT). Common adverse effects include nausea, headache, insomnia or somnolence, sexual dysfunction and sometimes hyponatraemia (SIADH), especially in older adults. SSRIs increase bleeding risk with NSAIDs and antithrombotics. Citalopram and escitalopram have dose-related QT concerns. Fluoxetine has a long half-life and important CYP interactions; switching requires drug-specific washout advice. Abrupt discontinuation can cause withdrawal symptoms, particularly with shorter half-life agents.

5. SNRIs, mirtazapine and trazodone

Venlafaxine and duloxetine inhibit serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake; venlafaxine can raise blood pressure and has clinically important withdrawal effects. Duloxetine can also treat some neuropathic pain syndromes. Mirtazapine blocks presynaptic alpha-2 receptors and certain serotonin receptors; sedation, appetite increase and weight gain are common. Trazodone is sedating and can cause postural hypotension. Choice is guided by symptom profile and adverse effects, not a simplistic “best drug” ranking.

6. TCAs, MAOIs and overdose safety

Tricyclic antidepressants such as amitriptyline inhibit monoamine reuptake but also block muscarinic, histamine H1 and alpha-1 receptors. They can cause anticholinergic effects, orthostatic hypotension and cardiac conduction toxicity; overdose is especially dangerous. MAOIs require specialist oversight because of major food/drug interactions and hypertensive or serotonergic toxicity. Avoid combining serotonergic medicines without checking interaction guidance.

7. Serotonin syndrome

Serotonin toxicity may follow initiation, dose increase or interaction of serotonergic drugs. Features include agitation, diaphoresis, diarrhoea, hyperthermia, hyperreflexia and inducible or spontaneous clonus. Clonus and hyperreflexia are particularly helpful clues. Stop implicated drugs, assess ABCDE, seek urgent senior/toxicology input and provide supportive treatment; severe cases need emergency escalation. Distinguish from neuroleptic malignant syndrome, which often evolves more slowly with marked rigidity.

8. Antipsychotics: indications and shared decisions

Antipsychotics are used for schizophrenia and other psychoses, acute mania and selected additional indications. Explain expected benefit, movement effects, sedation, weight/metabolic effects, sexual effects and possible cardiac risks. Baseline assessment usually includes weight/BMI, pulse, blood pressure, metabolic tests, movement assessment and ECG when indicated. Follow the local monitoring schedule and specialist treatment plan.

9. First-generation and second-generation antipsychotics

Haloperidol is a potent D2 antagonist and can cause extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), akathisia, dystonia and QT prolongation. Risperidone can raise prolactin and cause EPS, particularly at higher doses. Olanzapine commonly causes weight gain and metabolic disturbance. Quetiapine may be sedating and cause orthostatic hypotension. Aripiprazole is a D2 partial agonist and can cause akathisia. Individual responses vary.

10. Extrapyramidal adverse effects and tardive dyskinesia

Acute dystonia may cause painful neck, jaw or eye muscle spasms; severe laryngeal dystonia threatens the airway. Akathisia causes distressing inner restlessness and may be mistaken for agitation. Drug-induced parkinsonism causes rigidity, bradykinesia and tremor. Tardive dyskinesia involves persistent involuntary movements after longer exposure. Review the drug and dose, assess urgency, and seek specialist advice; anticholinergic treatment is not appropriate for every movement disorder.

11. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

NMS is a rare life-threatening syndrome associated with dopamine-blocking drugs or abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic treatment. Suspect it with fever, severe rigidity, altered mental state, autonomic instability and raised creatine kinase. Stop the causative drug, arrange emergency admission, monitor for rhabdomyolysis and organ injury, and involve specialists. Do not confuse it with routine antipsychotic sedation.

12. Clozapine: resistant psychosis and major hazards

Clozapine is a specialist option for treatment-resistant schizophrenia after appropriate trials. It requires mandated blood monitoring because of severe neutropenia/agranulocytosis risk, plus attention to myocarditis, cardiomyopathy, seizures, constipation/ileus, pneumonia and metabolic effects. Severe constipation can be fatal: proactively assess bowel function. Smoking changes can alter clozapine levels through CYP1A2 induction; nicotine replacement itself does not reproduce smoke-induced enzyme effects.

13. Bipolar disorder: mood stabilisers

In bipolar disorder, distinguish acute mania, bipolar depression and long-term relapse prevention; treatment differs across phases. Lithium is a major maintenance option when appropriate and requires monitoring. Valproate carries extensive UK reproductive risk restrictions and must follow current MHRA requirements. Lamotrigine has a role in selected bipolar depression/maintenance settings but must be titrated slowly due to serious rash risk. Antidepressant monotherapy can precipitate mood switching in susceptible patients.

14. Lithium: monitoring and toxicity

Lithium has a narrow therapeutic range and is cleared by the kidneys. Monitor serum lithium concentrations at the correct post-dose timing, renal function, thyroid function and calcium as required by UK guidance. Dehydration, acute illness, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs and thiazide diuretics can increase lithium concentrations. Toxicity may cause vomiting, coarse tremor, ataxia, confusion and seizures. With suspected toxicity, withhold lithium and arrange urgent assessment and blood tests; management is specialist-led.

15. Valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine safety

Valproate has significant teratogenic and neurodevelopmental risks and UK prescribing restrictions affecting people of reproductive potential. Confirm current MHRA measures rather than relying on an old checklist. Carbamazepine is an enzyme inducer and may cause hyponatraemia and blood dyscrasias; interactions are substantial. Lamotrigine needs slow titration, especially with valproate co-prescription, and urgent assessment for serious rash.

16. Anxiety: SSRIs and psychological treatment

For generalised anxiety disorder, offer evidence-based psychological treatment and consider an SSRI when medication is appropriate, following NICE recommendations. Review early anxiety activation, sleep changes, sexual effects and withdrawal risk. For panic disorder, antidepressants may be used long term; benzodiazepines are not a preferred routine long-term treatment. Reassess functioning and risk at follow-up.

17. Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs

Diazepam, lorazepam and other benzodiazepines positively modulate GABA-A receptors. They can rapidly reduce anxiety or agitation but cause sedation, falls, impaired driving, tolerance, dependence and withdrawal. Combined use with opioids, alcohol or other sedatives can cause respiratory depression. Use only when indicated, usually for short periods, with a documented review and taper plan. Z-drugs such as zopiclone share several safety concerns.

18. ADHD medicines and prescribing boundaries

Stimulants such as methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine increase catecholaminergic signalling and are initiated/managed within appropriate specialist pathways. Monitor pulse, blood pressure, appetite, weight, sleep and misuse/diversion concerns. Atomoxetine is a selective noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor; guanfacine is an alpha-2A agonist. Follow age-specific NICE and specialist monitoring requirements.

19. Prescribing in pregnancy, older age and comorbidity

Avoid abrupt discontinuation of essential psychiatric medicines solely because pregnancy is discovered. Discuss relapse risk versus fetal/neonatal risks with perinatal mental health specialists and current BNF/MHRA advice. In older adults, consider falls, anticholinergic burden, hyponatraemia, QT risk and polypharmacy. Adjust for renal or hepatic impairment where required and assess overdose toxicity when suicide risk is present.

20. Case 1: depression with possible bipolarity

A 24-year-old requests sertraline after three weeks of low mood. They describe a previous episode of four days with little sleep, unusually high energy and impulsive spending. The correct next step is to assess for hypomania/bipolar disorder and obtain appropriate specialist input before routine antidepressant monotherapy. Ask directly about suicidality and safety.

21. Case 2: lithium toxicity after gastroenteritis

A 58-year-old taking lithium develops diarrhoea, poor oral intake, coarse tremor and unsteady gait. Suspect lithium toxicity precipitated by dehydration and reduced renal clearance. Withhold lithium, urgently assess ABCDE and renal function, obtain a timed lithium concentration and discuss with emergency/toxicology teams. Do not wait for the result if clinically unwell.

22. Case 3: fever and rigidity after antipsychotic change

A patient develops fever, confusion, severe rigidity and tachycardia after increasing haloperidol. Suspect neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Stop dopamine-blocking medication, arrange emergency care and evaluate CK, renal function and complications. Compare with serotonin syndrome, where clonus and hyperreflexia are often prominent.

23. Case 4: clozapine and severe constipation

A patient on clozapine reports five days without a bowel movement, abdominal pain and vomiting. This is an emergency red flag for clozapine-associated gastrointestinal hypomotility and possible obstruction/ileus. Arrange urgent hospital assessment; do not offer routine reassurance or simply increase fibre without assessing obstruction.

24. PLAB 2: antidepressant counselling

Explain the diagnosis and choices, including talking therapy. Discuss the medicine’s purpose, delayed onset, common adverse effects, early worsening or suicidal thoughts, interactions, alcohol and driving, adherence, follow-up and a planned gradual discontinuation when appropriate. Invite questions and use teach-back. If immediate self-harm risk is identified, arrange urgent safeguarding and assessment.

25. High-yield prescribing checklist

Before prescribing: indication and phase of illness; bipolar screening; self-harm/overdose risk; allergy and interactions; pregnancy/reproductive safety; renal and liver function; ECG/metabolic monitoring when indicated; clear counselling; follow-up; and an emergency safety-net. Always verify current BNF, NICE and MHRA restrictions.

    NICE / BNF · Psychiatric prescribing pathways

    Teaching pathways; verify current NICE, BNF, MHRA restrictions and local specialist protocols.

    Starting depression treatment

    Assess suicide risk and possible mania/hypomania → confirm severity and preferences → offer psychological and/or drug treatment as appropriate → check interactions and contraindications → counsel about delayed benefit and early adverse effects → schedule follow-up and safety-net.

    Suspected serious drug reaction

    ABCDE and emergency escalation → identify serotonergic or dopamine-blocking exposures → assess clonus, reflexes, rigidity, temperature and autonomic signs → stop suspected medicine → urgent hospital assessment and supportive treatment.

    Patient on lithium becomes unwell

    Assess dehydration, vomiting/diarrhoea, tremor, ataxia or confusion → withhold lithium if toxicity suspected → urgent lithium level, renal function and electrolytes → review interacting medicines → senior/toxicology advice; resume only with a safe plan.

    Starting or reviewing antipsychotics

    Document indication and consent → baseline weight/BMI, BP, metabolic profile, movement assessment and ECG where indicated → discuss EPS, sedation, metabolic and prolactin risks → regular symptom/adverse-effect monitoring → specialist review if ineffective or unsafe.
