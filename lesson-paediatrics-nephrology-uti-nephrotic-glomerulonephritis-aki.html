Paediatric Nephrology: UTIs, Nephrotic Syndrome, Glomerulonephritis & AKI

Urgent red flags: infant under 3 months with suspected UTI; septic appearance; oliguria/anuria; severe hypertension; nephrotic syndrome with fever or abdominal pain; suspected HUS; hyperkalaemia.

Approach to urinary and renal symptoms

Ask about fever, dysuria, frequency, urgency, abdominal or loin pain, vomiting, urine output, colour, frothy urine, swelling, weight change, recent throat or skin infection, medication and family history. Check hydration, blood pressure, growth, oedema and abdominal findings.

PLAB application: A child with hypertension, oedema or oliguria needs renal assessment, even if urinary symptoms are absent.

UTI presentation by age

Infants may present with unexplained fever, poor feeding, vomiting, irritability or failure to thrive. Older children more often report dysuria, frequency, urgency and suprapubic pain. Fever with loin tenderness suggests upper urinary tract infection (pyelonephritis).

PLAB application: Consider UTI in an infant with unexplained fever; do not rely on dysuria.

Urine sampling and dipstick

Obtain a clean-catch urine sample where possible; catheter or suprapubic aspiration may be needed in selected cases. Follow NICE NG224 age-based dipstick and culture rules. Send culture for infants under 3 months and in suspected upper UTI, recurrent infection, serious illness or discordant findings.

PLAB application: Collect urine before antibiotics when feasible, but never delay urgent treatment of a seriously unwell child.

UTI antibiotics and referral

Babies under 3 months with suspected UTI require immediate paediatric specialist referral and parenteral antibiotics. Older children are treated according to lower versus upper UTI, severity, allergies, local resistance and NICE antimicrobial guidance. Reassess if not improving within 24–48 hours.

PLAB application: A febrile young infant is not managed as simple cystitis in primary care.

Imaging after UTI

Ultrasound, DMSA scan and micturating cystourethrogram are not routine after every UTI. NICE NG224 stratifies imaging by age, atypical features and recurrence. Atypical UTI includes serious illness, poor urine flow, abdominal or bladder mass, raised creatinine, septicaemia, non-E. coli infection or failure to respond within 48 hours.

PLAB application: Know the indications for investigation rather than ordering an ultrasound for every uncomplicated UTI.

Vesicoureteric reflux and recurrent UTI

Vesicoureteric reflux can predispose to recurrent pyelonephritis and renal scarring. Ask about constipation, bladder and bowel dysfunction, fluid intake and toileting habits. Manage modifiable factors and follow specialist advice for recurrent infection and imaging.

PLAB application: Recurrent febrile UTI requires a broader assessment, not repeated empirical antibiotics alone.

Nephrotic syndrome: recognition

Nephrotic syndrome causes heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia and oedema, often beginning around the eyes and becoming generalised. Children may have ascites, weight gain and frothy urine. Minimal change disease is the commonest cause in younger children.

PLAB application: Periorbital swelling with marked proteinuria is a classic PLAB clue.

Nephrotic syndrome: tests and management

Check urine protein, serum albumin, kidney function, electrolytes, blood pressure and volume status. Specialist-directed corticosteroids are usual first-line therapy for typical childhood steroid-sensitive nephrotic syndrome. Monitor weight, urine protein and complications; do not indiscriminately restrict fluids or give diuretics without volume assessment.

PLAB application: Oedema can coexist with intravascular depletion; assess circulation before treatment.

Nephrotic complications and relapse

Important complications include infection, thrombosis, hypovolaemia, acute kidney injury and relapse. Fever, abdominal pain, breathing difficulty, severe headache or reduced urine output warrants urgent assessment. Families may be taught home urine dipstick monitoring and relapse action plans.

PLAB application: A child with nephrotic syndrome and fever or abdominal pain may have serious bacterial infection.

Nephritic syndrome and acute glomerulonephritis

Nephritic syndrome features haematuria, hypertension, reduced GFR and sometimes oedema and proteinuria. Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis can follow throat or skin infection after a latent interval. Investigations may include urinalysis, renal function, complement C3 and antistreptococcal tests.

PLAB application: Cola-coloured urine plus high blood pressure after infection suggests glomerulonephritis.

IgA nephropathy and IgA vasculitis

IgA nephropathy often causes visible haematuria during or shortly after a respiratory infection. IgA vasculitis presents with palpable purpura (typically lower limbs), abdominal pain, arthralgia and possible renal involvement. Monitor urine and blood pressure because nephritis can develop later.

PLAB application: Purpura with abdominal pain in a child requires urine and blood pressure checks.

Haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Typical HUS can follow Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli diarrhoea, sometimes bloody. The triad is microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury. Pallor, bruising, oliguria and hypertension are warning signs. Urgent inpatient supportive management is required.

PLAB application: Bloody diarrhoea followed by pallor and oliguria is an emergency.

Acute kidney injury: recognition

AKI is a sudden fall in kidney function, detected by rising creatinine and/or reduced urine output. Causes are pre-renal (dehydration, shock), intrinsic (glomerulonephritis, HUS, nephrotoxins) or post-renal (obstruction). Creatinine may lag behind injury; compare with baseline and monitor fluid balance.

PLAB application: Review urine output, blood pressure, medicines and volume status in every suspected AKI.

AKI: emergency management

Assess ABCDE, perfusion and volume status. Obtain renal function, electrolytes, glucose, urinalysis and ECG if hyperkalaemia is suspected. Correct shock carefully, stop nephrotoxins where possible, manage fluid overload and seek urgent paediatric renal advice for severe AKI, hyperkalaemia, acidosis or obstruction.

PLAB application: Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes requires immediate emergency treatment according to local protocol.

Haematuria, proteinuria and hypertension

Visible haematuria can arise from infection, glomerular disease, stones or trauma. Persistent proteinuria, especially with haematuria, hypertension or impaired kidney function, needs assessment. Use age- and height-appropriate paediatric blood pressure interpretation and repeat measurements with the correct cuff.

PLAB application: Do not label hypertension as normal using adult blood-pressure cut-offs.

PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain urine collection, culture, antibiotics, hydration and when to return. For nephrotic syndrome discuss monitoring, relapse, infection risk and specialist follow-up without promising a cure. Safety-net for persistent fever, poor intake, vomiting, swelling, blood in urine, reduced urine output, drowsiness or breathlessness.

PLAB application: Use clear language, check parental understanding and give an explicit urgent-care plan.

PLAB 2 — parent consultation

Introduce yourself; confirm child age, symptoms and duration; ask about urine output, fever, pain, drinking, swelling and previous episodes. Explain suspected diagnosis and testing in everyday language, address concerns, discuss follow-up and provide specific safety-netting.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Paediatric Nephrology

Lower versus upper UTI

Lower UTI causes urinary symptoms without systemic features; upper UTI may present with fever, loin pain or systemic illness. Age and severity govern referral and route of antibiotics. NICE NG224 has detailed urine-testing and imaging algorithms.

Nephrotic versus nephritic

Nephrotic: massive protein loss, hypoalbuminaemia and oedema. Nephritic: inflammatory glomerular injury with haematuria, hypertension and impaired filtration. Overlap can occur; investigate atypical findings and refer appropriately.

Fluid assessment pitfall

Visible oedema does not prove adequate circulating volume. Nephrotic children may be intravascularly depleted; indiscriminate diuretics can worsen AKI. In AKI, distinguish shock from overload before giving fluid.

HUS and diarrhoeal illness

Look for anaemia, low platelets and renal dysfunction after diarrhoea. Seek urgent paediatric/nephrology support; management is largely supportive with careful fluid, electrolyte and blood pressure control.

Renal emergencies

Severe hyperkalaemia, pulmonary oedema, hypertensive emergency, acidosis and uraemic complications require immediate escalation. Use local paediatric emergency and renal protocols; renal replacement therapy may be needed.

Long-term renal protection

Track blood pressure, growth, kidney function and proteinuria; support hydration and bowel/bladder health, and avoid unnecessary nephrotoxins. Children with recurrent or atypical UTI, renal scarring or persistent proteinuria need tailored follow-up.
