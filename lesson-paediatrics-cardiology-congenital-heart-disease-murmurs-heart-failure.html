Paediatric Cardiology: Congenital Heart Disease, Murmurs & Heart Failure

Emergency red flags: central cyanosis, shock, weak femoral pulses in a sick neonate, hypercyanotic spell, collapse or exertional syncope, severe feeding difficulty with respiratory distress. Escalate urgently.

Approach to paediatric cardiac presentations

Ask about feeding, sweating with feeds, tachypnoea, weight gain, cyanosis, exercise tolerance, syncope, family history of congenital disease or sudden death, and antenatal screening. Examine general appearance, growth, oxygen saturation, pulses, perfusion, respiratory effort, precordium, heart sounds, liver size and signs of syndromes.

PLAB application: A murmur is not the diagnosis: first decide whether the child is well or critically unwell.

Normal transition and duct-dependent circulation

After birth, pulmonary vascular resistance falls and the ductus arteriosus usually closes over the first days. Some critical defects depend on a patent ductus for systemic or pulmonary blood flow. Duct closure can cause shock, cyanosis or profound acidosis despite an initially normal newborn examination.

PLAB application: An ill neonate with shock or cyanosis needs urgent senior neonatal/cardiology review; prostaglandin infusion is specialist-led.

Cyanotic versus acyanotic defects

Acyanotic shunts include VSD, ASD and PDA; large shunts can cause pulmonary overcirculation and heart failure. Cyanotic lesions include tetralogy of Fallot and transposition of the great arteries; some obstructive lesions also cause cyanosis. Pulse oximetry is important, but a normal reading does not exclude all structural disease.

PLAB application: Do not rely on the presence or loudness of a murmur to exclude critical congenital heart disease.

Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

VSD is an opening between the ventricles. A small VSD often causes a loud pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge without symptoms; a large defect can present weeks after birth with tachypnoea, sweating during feeds, recurrent chest infections and poor growth as pulmonary resistance falls. Echocardiography defines size and haemodynamics.

PLAB application: A louder murmur does not necessarily indicate a more severe VSD.

Atrial septal defect (ASD)

A secundum ASD creates left-to-right atrial shunting. Many children are asymptomatic. A systolic ejection murmur at the upper left sternal edge reflects increased flow through the pulmonary valve, with a classically fixed split second heart sound. Echo confirms diagnosis and guides closure decisions.

PLAB application: Fixed splitting of S2 is a high-yield distinguishing clue.

Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

Persistent connection between the aorta and pulmonary artery produces a continuous machinery-like murmur below the left clavicle, sometimes with bounding pulses and a wide pulse pressure. A large PDA can cause heart failure; management depends on age, prematurity, symptoms and haemodynamics.

PLAB application: A continuous murmur suggests PDA, but not every PDA has an audible murmur.

Tetralogy of Fallot and hypercyanotic spells

Tetralogy comprises VSD, overriding aorta, right ventricular outflow obstruction and right ventricular hypertrophy. Cyanotic spells can follow crying or feeding and present with sudden deepening cyanosis and distress. Seek emergency help, minimise agitation, position knee-to-chest if appropriate, provide oxygen and follow specialist resuscitation protocol.

PLAB application: Hypercyanotic spells are emergencies; definitive management requires specialist care.

Transposition of the great arteries

In d-transposition, the aorta arises from the right ventricle and pulmonary artery from the left, producing parallel circulations. Profound cyanosis may develop early, sometimes with little murmur. Survival requires mixing through ASD, VSD or PDA; urgent neonatal cardiology care, prostaglandin and sometimes atrial septostomy may be required.

PLAB application: A blue newborn with minimal respiratory signs can have critical congenital heart disease.

Coarctation of the aorta

Aortic narrowing may produce weak or delayed femoral pulses, an upper-to-lower limb blood pressure difference and, after duct closure, shock or poor feeding. Older children may have hypertension, headaches or leg fatigue. Compare brachial and femoral pulses; four-limb blood pressure can help but does not replace echocardiography.

PLAB application: Always palpate femoral pulses in a newborn or child with suspected heart disease.

Pulmonary and aortic stenosis

Valvar pulmonary stenosis often causes an ejection systolic murmur at the upper left sternal border; aortic stenosis is heard at the upper right sternal border and may radiate to the neck. Severe obstruction can cause exertional chest pain, syncope or heart failure. Symptomatic children need urgent specialist assessment.

PLAB application: Exertional syncope plus a murmur is a red flag; restrict strenuous exercise pending assessment.

Innocent versus pathological murmurs

Innocent murmurs are usually soft, systolic, variable with position and heard in a thriving child with normal pulses, saturation and no symptoms. Pathological features include diastolic or pansystolic murmurs, harsh quality, abnormal S2, thrills, cyanosis, poor growth, abnormal pulses or a family history of sudden cardiac death.

PLAB application: A diastolic murmur should not be labelled innocent.

Heart failure in infants and children

Infant heart failure often presents as tachypnoea, feeding fatigue, sweating, hepatomegaly and faltering growth rather than ankle oedema. Causes include significant left-to-right shunts, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia or myocarditis. Assess ABCDE, saturation, perfusion, hydration and fluid balance; involve paediatrics and cardiology urgently.

PLAB application: Hepatomegaly with tachypnoea and feeding difficulty is a classic infant heart failure pattern.

Investigations and echocardiography

Obtain pre- and post-ductal saturations where appropriate, ECG for rhythm and hypertrophy, chest radiograph for cardiomegaly or pulmonary vascularity if clinically indicated, and blood tests tailored to illness. Echocardiography is the key structural diagnostic test. Avoid delaying resuscitation while awaiting imaging.

PLAB application: ECG or chest radiograph alone cannot rule out congenital structural disease.

Arrhythmias and supraventricular tachycardia

SVT may cause an infant to be pale, irritable, tachypnoeic or feeding poorly, with a very rapid regular pulse. Obtain ECG, assess perfusion and haemodynamic stability. Unstable tachyarrhythmia requires immediate paediatric resuscitation support; stable cases follow age-appropriate monitored vagal manoeuvres and specialist protocols.

PLAB application: Never use carotid sinus massage or eyeball pressure in a child.

Kawasaki disease and acquired heart disease

Kawasaki disease is a vasculitis that can affect coronary arteries. Consider in a child with persistent fever, conjunctival injection, oral changes, rash, limb changes or cervical lymphadenopathy; incomplete cases occur. Urgent paediatric assessment and echocardiographic follow-up are important. Myocarditis may present with unexplained tachycardia and shock.

PLAB application: Persistent fever with mucocutaneous features requires prompt assessment, even without every classic sign.

PLAB 2 counselling, follow-up and safety-netting

Explain suspected findings in plain language without implying a definitive diagnosis from auscultation. Discuss echocardiography and referral, feeding and growth monitoring, medication adherence if prescribed, and individualised activity advice. Safety-net for cyanosis, collapse, breathing difficulty, poor feeding, marked lethargy or reduced urine output.

PLAB application: In an OSCE, combine an empathetic explanation with specific escalation advice and shared decisions.

PLAB 2 — Explaining a heart murmur to a parent

Introduce yourself, check what the parent understands, acknowledge concern and explain that many murmurs are harmless but some require an ultrasound scan. Ask about feeding, colour, growth, breathing and family history. Explain the referral plan and clear emergency signs without giving false reassurance.

Deep Dive

Why large shunts present later

As pulmonary vascular resistance falls after birth, pulmonary blood flow through a large VSD or PDA rises. This explains why infants may initially appear well but develop tachypnoea and feeding difficulty over subsequent weeks.

Distinguishing murmur clues

VSD: pansystolic at lower left sternal edge. ASD: ejection systolic with fixed split S2. PDA: continuous below left clavicle. Aortic stenosis: ejection systolic radiating to neck. These are patterns, not substitutes for echocardiography.

Duct-dependent emergencies

Critical coarctation or other obstructive lesions may present when the duct closes. Assess airway, breathing, circulation, pre/post-ductal saturations, blood pressure and femoral pulses. Call neonatal/cardiology and resuscitation teams immediately; prostaglandin is delivered under specialist monitoring because apnoea and hypotension can occur.

Hypercyanotic spell mechanism

In tetralogy of Fallot, increased right-to-left shunting during reduced pulmonary blood flow causes acute hypoxaemia. Keep the child calm, request emergency help and use the local specialist algorithm.

Heart failure treatment principles

Stabilise oxygenation and perfusion; assess fluid status carefully. Diuretics, nutritional support and corrective intervention depend on anatomy and specialist advice. Avoid reflexive large fluid boluses in a child with suspected pulmonary oedema.

OSCE examiner checklist

Ask about cyanosis, sweating during feeds, growth, collapse and family history; check saturation and femoral pulses; explain the uncertainty; offer echo/referral; give urgent safety-net advice.
