Surgical nutrition, fluid balance and electrolytes

Urgent escalation: shock, severe dehydration, altered consciousness, seizures, symptomatic hyponatraemia, ECG changes from potassium disorders, oliguria or suspected refeeding syndrome require ABCDE, senior help, monitoring and cause-directed treatment. Avoid rapid unmonitored electrolyte correction.

1 · Assess nutritional risk

Record weight, BMI, recent unintentional weight loss, intake, swallowing, gastrointestinal function, comorbidities and functional status. Use MUST screening in adults and refer to dietetics/nutrition support team. Look for malnutrition in cancer, bowel obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease and major surgery. Treat underlying disease and discuss goals of care.

2 · Nutrition support hierarchy

Use oral food, food fortification and oral nutritional supplements when safe and adequate. If the gastrointestinal tract works but oral intake is insufficient or unsafe, consider enteral feeding (nasogastric, nasojejunal or gastrostomy depending on indication). Consider parenteral nutrition when oral/enteral intake is inadequate or impossible because the gut is non-functional, inaccessible or leaking. Assess aspiration risk, line infection, glucose and biochemical monitoring.

3 · Refeeding syndrome

Prolonged poor intake, low BMI, major weight loss, alcohol dependence or low potassium/phosphate/magnesium increase risk. Refeeding increases insulin, shifting phosphate, potassium and magnesium intracellularly and potentially causing arrhythmias, respiratory failure and fluid overload. Identify risk before feeding; follow NICE CG32 for cautious initiation, thiamine and electrolyte supplementation and close monitoring. Do not withhold all feeding indefinitely while awaiting normalisation of electrolytes.

4 · Fluid assessment

Assess pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, mucous membranes, JVP, oedema, lung examination, weight, urine output, fluid chart and laboratory trends. Distinguish resuscitation, routine maintenance, replacement of ongoing losses, redistribution and reassessment (the 5 Rs, NICE CG174). Enteral/oral fluids are preferred where appropriate. Avoid both under-resuscitation and fluid overload, especially in renal or cardiac failure.

5 · Resuscitation and maintenance

For adult IV fluid resuscitation, NICE CG174 generally recommends a 500 mL crystalloid bolus containing sodium 130–154 mmol/L over less than 15 minutes, with immediate reassessment; tailor to shock type and comorbidity. Routine maintenance often starts at 25–30 mL/kg/day water, ~1 mmol/kg/day each sodium/potassium/chloride and 50–100 g/day glucose; use lower fluid volumes for frail, older or cardiac/renal-impaired patients. These are starting guides, not automatic prescriptions.

6 · Replace gastrointestinal losses

Vomiting and nasogastric suction often cause chloride and potassium depletion and metabolic alkalosis. Diarrhoea or high-output ileostomy may cause sodium/water depletion, potassium and bicarbonate losses with metabolic acidosis. Measure outputs and urine, monitor U&E, magnesium and acid-base status, replace losses according to measured needs and seek specialist stoma/nutrition advice for persistent high output.

7 · Hyponatraemia

Confirm sodium and assess symptoms, onset, serum osmolality, glucose, volume status and medication causes. Severe neurological symptoms such as seizures require emergency hypertonic saline under specialist protocol with frequent sodium checks. Over-rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination, especially in chronic hyponatraemia. Mild/asymptomatic cases need cause-directed treatment rather than indiscriminate saline.

8 · Hypernatraemia

Usually reflects water deficit or excess sodium. Assess volume status and causes (poor access to water, fever, gastrointestinal loss, diabetes insipidus). Restore circulation first if shocked, then replace water deficit carefully and monitor sodium. Chronic or unknown-duration hypernatraemia requires controlled correction to reduce cerebral oedema risk.

9 · Potassium disorders

Hyperkalaemia: confirm true result where appropriate but never delay treatment for dangerous ECG changes. Cardiac monitoring, IV calcium for ECG changes, insulin–glucose and nebulised salbutamol to shift potassium, and potassium removal (e.g. binders or dialysis as indicated) follow local emergency protocols. Hypokalaemia: assess ECG, magnesium, renal function and ongoing losses; replace orally when safe, IV with monitoring for severe/symptomatic cases. Never give undiluted IV potassium.

10 · Calcium, magnesium and phosphate

Hypocalcaemia can cause tetany, paraesthesia, seizures or QT prolongation; symptomatic severe cases require monitored IV calcium. Hypomagnesaemia may sustain refractory hypokalaemia and cause arrhythmias; replace magnesium. Hypophosphataemia can cause weakness, respiratory failure and refeeding complications; check and replace based on severity. Interpret calcium using albumin-adjusted or ionised calcium as appropriate.

11 · Acute kidney injury and fluid safety

Trend creatinine and urine output; identify hypovolaemia, sepsis, obstruction and nephrotoxic medications. Check bladder and consider renal imaging when obstruction suspected. Avoid reflex fluid boluses in pulmonary oedema or anuric renal failure; seek renal/critical care help for refractory hyperkalaemia, acidosis or overload.

12 · Postoperative nutrition and discharge

Encourage early oral/enteral nutrition when clinically appropriate, including enhanced recovery pathways. Document nutrition goals, fluid plan, stoma outputs, electrolytes, medication review and follow-up. Involve dietitians, pharmacists and specialist nurses. Monitor for aspiration, feeding tube problems, catheter sepsis and glycaemic instability.

High-yield clinical frameworks

Nutrition decision pathway

Screen MUST → assess swallowing and functional gut → oral/fortified diet if possible → enteral if gut works but oral intake inadequate → parenteral if gut cannot be used → monitor intake, weight, glucose, phosphate, potassium and magnesium.

NICE IV fluids: 5 Rs

Resuscitation: restore perfusion; Routine maintenance: daily needs; Replacement: measured deficits/losses; Redistribution: account for oedema/third spacing; Reassessment: repeat examination, observations, urine output and bloods.

Electrolyte red flags

Seizure/confusion → sodium and glucose urgently. Weakness/palpitations or ECG changes → potassium, calcium and magnesium. Malnourished patient starting feeds → phosphate, magnesium, potassium and thiamine.

High-output stoma

Measure output → check dehydration, U&E and magnesium → replace salt/water appropriately → investigate infection/obstruction and medication effects → specialist stoma team.

Potassium emergency

ABCDE + ECG → if hyperkalaemic ECG toxicity, IV calcium for membrane stabilisation → insulin–glucose ± salbutamol to shift K → remove K and recheck levels/glucose. Follow local protocol.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Postoperative dehydration

A 72-year-old after bowel surgery is tachycardic, hypotensive and oliguric. Approach: ABCDE, assess for bleeding and sepsis, obtain U&E and lactate, resuscitate with monitored crystalloid when indicated, reassess and escalate.

Case 2 — Refeeding risk

A patient with advanced oesophageal cancer has eaten little for 12 days and is about to start tube feeds. Approach: assess NICE CG32 refeeding risk, give thiamine and appropriate supplements, start feeding cautiously and monitor phosphate, potassium, magnesium and fluid balance.

Case 3 — High-output ileostomy

A patient with a new ileostomy has large watery outputs, dizziness and rising creatinine. Approach: measure intake/output, assess sodium and magnesium losses, replace fluids/electrolytes, seek stoma specialist input and investigate triggers.

Case 4 — Hyperkalaemia

A postoperative patient with AKI has potassium 6.9 mmol/L and broad QRS complexes. Approach: emergency senior call, ECG monitoring, immediate IV calcium under protocol, shift and remove potassium, serial glucose/potassium checks and renal review.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Nutrition and fluid counselling

Station: A 64-year-old recovering from colorectal surgery has poor appetite and asks why the team is monitoring fluid balance and offering nutritional support.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, invite concerns and ask about intake, weight loss, nausea, swallowing and stoma losses.
  2. Explain how surgery increases nutritional needs and why poor intake delays healing.
  3. Discuss dietitian assessment, oral supplements and enteral feeding if needed; parenteral feeding only when the gut cannot be used adequately.
  4. Explain fluid chart, urine output, weight and blood monitoring in plain language.
  5. Explain risks of dehydration, fluid overload and electrolyte imbalance; discuss symptoms to report.
  6. Ask about cultural/dietary preferences and support shared decisions.
  7. Check understanding and safety-net for dizziness, reduced urine, confusion, palpitations or persistent vomiting.

Examiner focus: clear communication, nutritional screening, patient-centred decisions, refeeding awareness and safe escalation.

Rapid revision — 12 key points

  1. Screen surgical patients for malnutrition using MUST.
  2. Use the gut if functional: oral first, enteral when oral inadequate.
  3. Use parenteral nutrition when enteral feeding is inadequate or not possible.
  4. Refeeding syndrome causes phosphate, potassium and magnesium shifts.
  5. High-risk refeeding requires thiamine, cautious feeding and monitoring.
  6. NICE CG174 fluid prescribing uses the 5 Rs.
  7. Adult fluid resuscitation often begins with 500 mL isotonic crystalloid and reassessment.
  8. Maintenance fluid prescriptions must be individualised.
  9. High-output ileostomies cause water, sodium and magnesium depletion.
  10. Severe symptomatic hyponatraemia is an emergency; avoid overcorrection.
  11. Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes needs immediate cardiac protection.
  12. Postoperative oliguria demands assessment for shock, AKI and obstruction.
