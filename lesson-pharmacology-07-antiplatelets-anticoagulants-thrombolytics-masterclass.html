Antithrombotic pharmacology · Deep Dive

Molecular mechanisms, monitoring and clinically important distinctions.

Platelet signalling

Aspirin suppresses thromboxane A2 synthesis by irreversible COX-1 acetylation. ADP activates platelet P2Y12, reducing cAMP and promoting aggregation; P2Y12 antagonists interrupt this feedback. Platelets are anucleate and cannot rapidly replace irreversibly inhibited enzymes/receptors. Antiplatelet efficacy is particularly relevant to high-shear arterial thrombosis.

Factor Xa and thrombin

Factor Xa with factor Va generates thrombin on phospholipid surfaces. Thrombin cleaves fibrinogen and activates XIII to crosslink fibrin. Direct Xa inhibitors reduce thrombin generation; dabigatran inhibits existing thrombin. A normal PT or aPTT does not reliably exclude clinically relevant DOAC activity; specialist drug-calibrated assays may be necessary.

Warfarin initiation

Warfarin decreases vitamin K-dependent gamma-carboxylation. Existing clotting factors must clear before full anticoagulant effect. Protein C can decline early, creating a transient procoagulant imbalance; this helps explain why bridging is required in selected acute thrombosis pathways. CYP2C9 and VKORC1 variants contribute to dose variability. INR is a standardised PT ratio, not a direct measure of every bleeding risk.

Heparin and HIT

UFH binds antithrombin through a pentasaccharide sequence, accelerating inhibition of Xa and IIa. LMWH predominantly enhances anti-Xa activity. HIT antibodies against platelet factor 4–heparin complexes activate platelets and cause thrombosis. A 4Ts score estimates pretest probability; laboratory confirmation uses immunoassays and functional testing where available.

Risk scores and limitations

CHA₂DS₂-VASc estimates thromboembolic risk in AF, while bleeding-risk tools identify modifiable factors and support review rather than automatically withholding treatment. Scores do not replace clinical judgment, especially in valvular disease, pregnancy, recent surgery or active bleeding.

Reversal decision science

The reversal decision depends on site and severity of haemorrhage, time since last dose, renal function and agent. PCC provides clotting factors but does not directly neutralise a Xa inhibitor. Vitamin K restores synthesis of vitamin K-dependent factors but is too slow alone in critical bleeding. Reversal itself can increase thrombosis risk; plan anticoagulant resumption after haemostasis and specialist risk assessment.

Thrombolytic biology

Plasminogen activators generate plasmin, which degrades fibrin. Fibrinolysis has greater systemic haemorrhage risk than routine anticoagulation and is therefore restricted to selected emergencies. In acute ischaemic stroke, imaging must exclude intracranial haemorrhage and treatment eligibility depends on current specialist stroke protocols.

Practical clinical checks

Before prescribing: confirm indication and duration, document bleeding history and medication list, obtain FBC and renal/liver function, check pregnancy and interacting agents, select correct regimen for AF versus VTE, assess follow-up and adherence, provide safety-net advice and document when to review.

Drug class comparison

1 · Clinical framework: choose by indication

Antiplatelets prevent platelet-rich arterial thrombosis; anticoagulants suppress coagulation and fibrin formation; thrombolytics actively convert plasminogen to plasmin to dissolve fibrin. They are not interchangeable. Ask whether the patient has ACS, coronary stent, atrial fibrillation (AF), venous thromboembolism (VTE), mechanical valve, or life-threatening thrombosis. Assess active bleeding, platelet count, renal function, pregnancy, interactions and procedural plans before prescribing.

2 · Primary haemostasis and platelet medicines

Vascular injury exposes collagen and von Willebrand factor, promoting platelet adhesion; ADP acting at P2Y12 and thromboxane A2 amplify activation. Aspirin irreversibly inhibits platelet COX-1 and thromboxane A2 production for the platelet lifespan. Clopidogrel and prasugrel irreversibly inhibit P2Y12 after hepatic activation; ticagrelor directly and reversibly inhibits P2Y12. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) commonly means aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor after ACS or coronary intervention, with regimen and duration tailored to ischaemic and bleeding risks and specialist guidance.

3 · Aspirin, clopidogrel and P2Y12 inhibitors

Low-dose aspirin is used for secondary prevention in selected vascular disease; avoid assuming it is suitable for primary prevention. Adverse effects include gastrointestinal bleeding, dyspepsia and bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive patients. Clopidogrel is a prodrug affected by CYP2C19 function and interacting medicines; ticagrelor can cause dyspnoea and bradyarrhythmias; prasugrel has important restrictions including previous stroke/TIA. A patient with coronary stent should not independently stop DAPT: early interruption may cause stent thrombosis. Gastroprotection with a PPI may be indicated by bleeding risk; check drug-specific interactions and BNF.

4 · Coagulation cascade and anticoagulant targets

Tissue factor initiates coagulation, producing factor Xa and thrombin (IIa). Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin and amplifies coagulation. Apixaban, rivaroxaban and edoxaban inhibit factor Xa; dabigatran inhibits thrombin. Warfarin inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase, lowering synthesis of factors II, VII, IX and X and proteins C and S. Heparins accelerate antithrombin activity. Anticoagulants prevent extension and recurrence of thrombosis but do not instantly lyse established clots.

5 · DOACs: practical prescribing

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are commonly used for non-valvular AF stroke prevention and VTE treatment/prevention in eligible patients. Choice and dose depend on the indication, age, weight, renal function, interacting medicines and bleeding risk. Some VTE regimens require an initial higher-intensity phase, while dabigatran and edoxaban require preceding parenteral anticoagulation for acute VTE. Do not extrapolate AF dosing to VTE. Check BNF and local protocols, including Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance where indicated. DOACs are generally unsuitable for mechanical heart valves and for certain high-risk antiphospholipid syndrome patients.

6 · Warfarin and INR

Warfarin has delayed onset, numerous food/drug interactions and requires INR monitoring. INR targets depend on indication and valve type; 2.0–3.0 is common for AF/VTE but not universal. Vitamin K intake should be consistent rather than eliminated. Antibiotics, amiodarone and many other drugs can alter INR. For acute VTE when warfarin is selected, appropriate initial parenteral anticoagulation is required until therapeutic anticoagulation criteria are met. Mechanical valves usually require a vitamin K antagonist rather than a DOAC, under specialist-directed targets.

7 · Unfractionated and low-molecular-weight heparin

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) acts rapidly and is often used where rapid titration/reversal is desirable, including severe renal dysfunction or high-risk procedures; monitor using local aPTT or anti-Xa protocols. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), such as enoxaparin, is usually administered subcutaneously, has more predictable effects and may require renal dose adjustment. Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an immune prothrombotic complication: a platelet fall typically 5–10 days after exposure, or earlier with recent exposure, warrants urgent assessment and cessation of all heparin if suspected, with specialist alternative anticoagulation.

8 · Thrombolysis: when clot dissolution matters

Alteplase and tenecteplase are fibrinolytic agents used in selected time-critical emergencies under specialist protocols, such as eligible acute ischaemic stroke, selected STEMI when timely primary PCI is unavailable, and haemodynamically unstable pulmonary embolism. They can cause catastrophic intracranial or systemic bleeding. Confirm indication, timing, imaging and absolute/relative contraindications before treatment. Routine thrombolysis is not appropriate for uncomplicated DVT or stable PE.

9 · Bleeding, reversal and emergency response

Major bleeding is a medical emergency: ABCDE, stop the implicated drug, obtain IV access, FBC, coagulation tests, renal function, group and crossmatch; identify and control the source. Seek urgent haematology/specialist advice. Warfarin-associated life-threatening bleeding commonly requires IV vitamin K plus four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate per protocol. Idarucizumab reverses dabigatran; andexanet alfa may be available for certain factor Xa inhibitor-associated life-threatening bleeds subject to local criteria, with PCC-based strategies used in some settings. Protamine reverses UFH more completely than LMWH. Do not delay resuscitation while waiting for laboratory results.

10 · Special populations and interactions

Pregnancy: LMWH is commonly preferred when anticoagulation is indicated; warfarin and DOACs are generally avoided, with complex exceptions such as mechanical valves requiring specialist care. Renal impairment raises exposure to several DOACs and LMWH. Older age, prior bleeding, thrombocytopenia, liver disease and concomitant NSAIDs/antiplatelets increase bleeding risk. Triple therapy (oral anticoagulant plus DAPT) has substantial bleeding risk and is reserved for selected circumstances with specialist-defined duration.

11 · Clinical case: AF and stroke prevention

A 76-year-old with hypertension and newly diagnosed AF asks for aspirin instead of an anticoagulant. Explain that aspirin is not an adequate substitute for anticoagulation to prevent AF-related stroke when anticoagulation is indicated. Assess stroke risk with CHA₂DS₂-VASc and bleeding factors, renal function, preferences and contraindications. Shared decision-making includes adherence, missed-dose instructions and warning signs of bleeding.

12 · Clinical case: black stools on apixaban

A patient on apixaban for VTE develops melaena, dizziness and hypotension. Treat as major gastrointestinal haemorrhage: urgent ABCDE assessment, resuscitation, stop anticoagulant, blood tests and crossmatch, source control and reversal consideration according to local guidance. Do not simply advise withholding one dose and booking routine follow-up.

13 · Clinical case: thrombocytopenia after heparin

A postoperative patient on heparin develops a 55% platelet fall on day 7 and a new DVT. Suspect HIT despite thrombocytopenia because HIT is prothrombotic. Calculate a 4Ts score, stop all heparin (including flushes), request appropriate tests and initiate specialist-directed non-heparin anticoagulation when indicated. Do not transfuse platelets routinely without specific indication.

14 · Clinical case: recent coronary stent

A patient with a recent drug-eluting stent needs an elective dental procedure and wants to stop clopidogrel. Establish stent date, ACS history, planned procedure and bleeding risk; contact cardiology and the procedural team before altering antiplatelets. Unsupervised cessation can precipitate stent thrombosis. Many minor procedures can proceed without stopping therapy, depending on procedure-specific guidance.

15 · PLAB 2 counselling and safety-net

Explain the indication and intended benefit; ask about OTC ibuprofen, aspirin, herbal medicines and alcohol; review missed doses without doubling; advise avoiding contact sports where appropriate and carrying an anticoagulant alert card. Discuss bruising versus red flags: prolonged bleeding, black stools, blood in urine, severe headache, neurological symptoms or head injury require urgent assessment. Check pregnancy plans and procedures. Use teach-back and document shared decisions.

16 · Exam traps and take-home synthesis

Aspirin/clopidogrel act on platelets, not coagulation factors. AF-related stroke prevention usually needs anticoagulation rather than aspirin when indicated. Warfarin requires INR; DOACs do not require routine INR but do require periodic renal function, blood count and adherence review. Mechanical valves are a major DOAC exclusion. Suspected HIT means stop heparin, not simply switch UFH to LMWH. Thrombolysis is not the same as anticoagulation. Always consider bleeding severity and local reversal availability.
