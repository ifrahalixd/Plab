Shoulder Dystocia, Cord Prolapse & Uterine Rupture

Emergency priority: Declare the emergency, call the obstetric team, protect maternal and fetal wellbeing and expedite appropriate delivery. Never apply fundal pressure for shoulder dystocia.

Three time-critical obstetric emergencies

Shoulder dystocia is failure of the shoulders to deliver after the head requiring additional manoeuvres; cord prolapse is descent of the umbilical cord through the cervix alongside or past the presenting part after membrane rupture; uterine rupture is disruption of the uterine wall, usually during labour.

PLAB application: Call the obstetric emergency team immediately and communicate clearly.

First response and team roles

Recognise the emergency, announce it, call senior obstetric, midwifery, anaesthetic and neonatal assistance, note the time and allocate roles. Use closed-loop communication and document events contemporaneously.

PLAB application: Do not leave the patient alone; prioritise maternal and fetal safety.

Shoulder dystocia recognition

After the fetal head delivers, the shoulders fail to deliver with gentle axial traction; the turtle sign (retraction of the head) may be seen. Diagnosis is clinical.

PLAB application: Avoid excessive traction and fundal pressure.

Shoulder dystocia risk factors

Previous shoulder dystocia, fetal macrosomia, diabetes, obesity, prolonged second stage and instrumental delivery increase risk; many cases are unpredictable.

PLAB application: Do not assume absence of risk factors excludes dystocia.

Shoulder dystocia immediate manoeuvres

Call for help, stop pushing temporarily as directed, perform McRoberts manoeuvre (hyperflex maternal hips) and apply suprapubic pressure by a trained clinician. Reassess promptly.

PLAB application: McRoberts plus suprapubic pressure is an early approach; NEVER use fundal pressure.

Further shoulder dystocia manoeuvres

If unresolved, an experienced clinician may use internal rotational manoeuvres (Rubin/Woods), deliver the posterior arm, or change to all-fours position where appropriate. Episiotomy may create room for internal manoeuvres but does not itself release bony impaction.

PLAB application: Escalate rapidly; avoid repeated forceful traction.

After shoulder dystocia

Assess the newborn for brachial plexus injury, clavicular or humeral fracture and hypoxic injury; assess the mother for PPH and severe perineal tears. Debrief and document head-to-body interval and manoeuvres.

PLAB application: Explain what happened empathetically and arrange appropriate follow-up.

Cord prolapse presentation

Suspect cord prolapse after spontaneous or artificial rupture of membranes with sudden prolonged fetal bradycardia or recurrent variable decelerations. A cord may be visible at the vulva or palpable on vaginal examination.

PLAB application: Fetal heart rate changes after membrane rupture warrant immediate assessment.

Cord prolapse risk factors

Breech or transverse lie, high or unengaged presenting part, prematurity, polyhydramnios, multiple pregnancy and artificial rupture with a high presenting part increase risk.

PLAB application: Check presentation and engagement before planned amniotomy.

Cord prolapse immediate management

Call for help and prepare expedited birth. Relieve cord compression by manually elevating the presenting part with a gloved hand during vaginal examination; use knee–chest or left lateral head-down position as feasible. Minimise cord handling.

PLAB application: Maintain decompression until delivery or a specialist takes over.

Cord prolapse delivery planning

If vaginal birth is not imminent, emergency caesarean birth is usually indicated, with urgency based on fetal heart rate and clinical circumstances. If fully dilated with an imminent safe vaginal birth, assisted vaginal delivery may be considered by a senior clinician.

PLAB application: Do not delay birth for unnecessary investigations.

Uterine rupture risk and recognition

Previous caesarean or uterine surgery, induction or augmentation, obstructed labour and uterine overdistension increase risk. Sudden abnormal fetal heart rate, severe pain, vaginal bleeding, maternal shock, loss of station or cessation of contractions may occur; signs can be subtle.

PLAB application: Persistent fetal bradycardia during VBAC may be the earliest clue.

Uterine rupture emergency response

Activate obstetric emergency response; ABCDE, oxygen if indicated, two large-bore IV lines, blood tests and crossmatch, major haemorrhage protocol when needed. Stop oxytocin. Urgent laparotomy and delivery with surgical repair or hysterectomy may be required.

PLAB application: Do not delay surgery when rupture is strongly suspected.

Differentials and pitfalls

For fetal bradycardia consider maternal hypotension, tachysystole, placental abruption and cord compression. Distinguish shoulder dystocia from a difficult head delivery. Avoid fundal pressure in shoulder dystocia and avoid excessive cord handling in prolapse.

PLAB application: Think about the mechanism and act before complete diagnostic certainty in emergencies.

Communication, consent and documentation

Explain urgent actions in short clear language; seek consent when possible without delaying life-saving care. Record timeline, fetal heart pattern, manoeuvres, medications, team members, mode of delivery and neonatal outcome. Offer debriefing and psychological support.

PLAB application: Use SBAR and closed-loop instructions.

PLAB 2 approach and safety net

For emergency OSCE: recognise signs, summon help, ABCDE, positioning or manoeuvres, plan urgent delivery, reassess and hand over. After the event discuss recovery, birth trauma, future pregnancy planning and follow-up with obstetric team.

PLAB application: Never promise that complications are impossible; acknowledge uncertainty.

PLAB 2 — Obstetric emergency OSCE

State the emergency aloud, summon senior obstetric, anaesthetic and neonatal help, begin appropriate immediate manoeuvres and explain urgent delivery. Use SBAR, document timings and offer a post-event debrief.

Deep Dive

Shoulder dystocia mechanism

The anterior shoulder commonly impacts behind the pubic symphysis. McRoberts changes pelvic geometry and suprapubic pressure aims to adduct/dislodge the shoulder. Do not use fundal pressure.

Cord prolapse and fetal hypoxia

Compression interrupts umbilical blood flow. Rapid recognition, manual elevation of the presenting part and expedited birth minimise hypoxic risk. The cord should be handled as little as possible.

Uterine rupture versus dehiscence

Complete rupture involves full-thickness separation and can cause haemorrhage and fetal compromise. Dehiscence is often incomplete and may be asymptomatic. Suspected rupture in labour requires emergency response.

Fetal heart monitoring

A sudden prolonged deceleration or bradycardia after membrane rupture suggests cord compression; fetal bradycardia during VBAC may signal rupture. Escalate according to current intrapartum monitoring guidance.

Complications and aftercare

Maternal injury includes PPH, perineal trauma and surgical morbidity. Neonatal complications include hypoxia, brachial plexus injury and fracture. Document accurately and offer a sensitive debrief.

PLAB 2 clinical priorities

In an emergency station, name the diagnosis, summon help, state immediate safe measures, explain the definitive delivery plan and use structured handover. Do not attempt specialist manoeuvres beyond your competence.
