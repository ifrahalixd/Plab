Diabetes pharmacology · Deep Dive

Beta-cell electrophysiology

Glucose metabolism raises the beta-cell ATP/ADP ratio, closing KATP channels. Depolarisation opens voltage-gated calcium channels and triggers insulin granule exocytosis. Sulfonylureas close SUR1-associated KATP channels independent of glucose concentration, explaining hypoglycaemia. Incretins potentiate glucose-dependent secretion through cAMP pathways, lowering hypoglycaemia risk when not combined with insulin secretagogues.

Insulin signalling and insulin resistance

Insulin receptor autophosphorylation recruits IRS proteins and activates PI3K–Akt signalling, driving GLUT4 translocation in muscle and adipose tissue. In the liver, insulin restrains gluconeogenesis. Visceral adiposity, inflammatory signalling, ectopic lipids and genetics contribute to insulin resistance; beta-cell exhaustion can follow. Metformin predominantly lowers hepatic glucose output, with mechanisms including mitochondrial and gut-related effects.

Kidney transport and SGLT2

The proximal tubule reabsorbs most filtered glucose via SGLT2, with SGLT1 acting downstream. SGLT2 inhibition increases glucosuria and natriuresis, influences tubuloglomerular feedback and can reduce intraglomerular pressure. Lower insulin-to-glucagon signalling and stress physiology may increase ketogenesis, so euglycaemic DKA remains possible.

HbA1c versus real-time glucose

HbA1c reflects average glycaemia over roughly 2–3 months but may be misleading with haemolysis, anaemia, haemoglobin variants or altered red-cell survival. CGM adds time in range and time below range; capillary testing confirms suspected symptomatic lows where appropriate. Clinical targets should be individualised rather than treating a number in isolation.

Insulin kinetics and practical calculations

Insulin action depends on formulation, injection site, temperature, physical activity and lipohypertrophy. Units are not millilitres: concentration must be checked before calculating volume. For example, 10 units of U-100 insulin corresponds to 0.1 mL, but concentrated insulin differs and dedicated devices should be used. Never transfer this example into a prescription without checking the exact product and delivery device.

DKA biochemical sequence

Insulin deficiency and counter-regulatory hormones increase lipolysis; free fatty acids enter hepatic beta-oxidation, generating ketones (especially beta-hydroxybutyrate). The resulting anion-gap metabolic acidosis drives compensatory deep breathing. Osmotic diuresis causes fluid and electrolyte loss. Serum potassium may initially appear normal or high despite total-body depletion; insulin and correction of acidosis shift potassium intracellularly.

PLAB safety framework

Identify immediate danger first: unconscious hypoglycaemia, suspected DKA/HHS, severe dehydration or acute kidney injury. Then identify diabetes type, current treatment, meal intake, renal function and medication changes. Treat emergencies using local protocols and involve senior/diabetes teams. Explain the plan clearly and document safety-net advice.

Diabetes medicines: mechanisms, selection and safety

1 · Clinical approach to diabetes pharmacology

Type 1 diabetes requires insulin because autoimmune beta-cell destruction produces absolute insulin deficiency. Type 2 diabetes involves insulin resistance and progressive beta-cell dysfunction. Before choosing therapy, confirm diabetes type, symptoms, HbA1c, renal function, cardiovascular disease, heart failure, weight, pregnancy plans, hypoglycaemia risk, affordability and the patient’s priorities. A person with suspected diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) needs emergency assessment rather than routine outpatient medication adjustment.

2 · Insulin physiology and preparations

Insulin binds a receptor tyrosine kinase, increasing glucose uptake in muscle and fat via GLUT4, suppressing hepatic glucose production, promoting glycogen and lipid synthesis, and shifting potassium intracellularly. Rapid-acting analogues are used with meals; basal analogues provide background coverage. Human soluble insulin and isophane insulin have different onset and duration profiles. Insulin is prescribed in UNITS, with a clear device, concentration and regimen. Never use “U” as an abbreviation. Insulin omission in type 1 diabetes can precipitate DKA.

3 · Basal–bolus, mixed insulin and safe administration

Basal–bolus treatment combines long-acting background insulin with mealtime rapid-acting insulin; individual doses are adjusted using glucose patterns, carbohydrate intake, activity, illness and specialist guidance. Premixed regimens can suit selected patients but reduce flexibility. Check insulin name, strength (including concentrated products), pen compatibility, injection technique, site rotation and storage. Lipohypertrophy can impair absorption. Do not interchange insulin products or concentrations without an appropriate prescribing and education plan.

4 · Metformin: first-line principles and cautions

Metformin reduces hepatic glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity; it does not ordinarily cause hypoglycaemia when used alone. Gastrointestinal adverse effects are common; gradual titration and modified-release formulations may help. Check renal function and current BNF thresholds for dose reduction or discontinuation. Temporarily withhold metformin during significant dehydration, hypoxia or acute kidney injury and follow local guidance around iodinated contrast when relevant. Rare lactic acidosis is more likely with severe acute illness or impaired clearance. Long-term therapy may reduce vitamin B12.

5 · SGLT2 inhibitors: glucose, heart and kidney

Empagliflozin and dapagliflozin inhibit renal SGLT2 in the proximal tubule, increasing urinary glucose and sodium excretion. Benefits can extend to heart failure and chronic kidney disease in eligible patients, even when glucose lowering is modest. Risks include genital fungal infection, volume depletion and rare euglycaemic DKA. Teach sick-day withholding during acute illness or fasting, and urgent ketone assessment for nausea, abdominal pain, vomiting or breathlessness even with near-normal glucose. Check indication-specific eGFR criteria and perioperative policies.

6 · GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual incretin agents

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and liraglutide increase glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress inappropriate glucagon, delay gastric emptying and promote satiety. They may support weight reduction and cardiovascular benefit for selected people. Gastrointestinal effects are common; dehydration, gallbladder disease and pancreatitis symptoms require assessment. Tirzepatide is a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist. Eligibility and sequencing depend on the current NICE guidance, indication and local formulary. Counsel about administration, titration, pregnancy planning and contraceptive considerations where applicable.

7 · Sulfonylureas, DPP-4 inhibitors and pioglitazone

Gliclazide stimulates pancreatic insulin release by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels and can cause hypoglycaemia and weight gain, especially with missed meals or renal impairment. DPP-4 inhibitors (e.g. sitagliptin) prolong endogenous incretin activity, are generally weight neutral and have low hypoglycaemia risk when used alone; adjust selected agents for kidney function. Pioglitazone improves insulin sensitivity through PPAR-gamma activation but can cause oedema, weight gain and worsen heart failure; review fracture and bladder-cancer cautions and BNF restrictions.

8 · Hypoglycaemia: recognition and immediate treatment

Hypoglycaemia often produces sweating, tremor, hunger, palpitations, confusion, behavioural change and eventually seizures or coma. In an awake adult who can swallow safely, give 15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck capillary glucose after about 10–15 minutes, repeat if still low, then provide longer-acting carbohydrate if appropriate. A drowsy, unconscious or fitting patient must not receive oral intake: call for help, protect airway, give IV glucose or IM glucagon according to local protocol, and monitor for recurrence. Sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycaemia may be prolonged and require admission.

9 · DKA and HHS: recognise the emergency

DKA is characterised by ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis, often with hyperglycaemia but sometimes with only mildly elevated glucose during SGLT2 treatment. Features include vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration and Kussmaul breathing. HHS usually features profound hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality and dehydration with little significant ketosis. Both require urgent hospital management with fluids, electrolyte monitoring and protocol-driven insulin where indicated; potassium can fall rapidly with treatment. Never treat suspected DKA as simple hyperglycaemia.

10 · Renal disease, cardiovascular disease and obesity

Drug choice should consider CKD stage, albuminuria, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure and weight. SGLT2 inhibitors and selected GLP-1 agents offer cardiorenal benefits in appropriate patients. Insulin and sulfonylurea doses may need adjustment as renal function declines because hypoglycaemia risk rises. Individualise HbA1c targets in frail patients and those with recurrent hypoglycaemia. Review the current NICE type 2 diabetes guideline and BNF rather than memorising a single universal drug sequence.

11 · Pregnancy, steroids, hospitalisation and sick days

Diabetes in pregnancy requires specialist management; insulin is commonly central and metformin may be used in selected circumstances under guidance. Other agents are not routinely interchangeable with pregnancy-approved treatments. Glucocorticoids can produce marked daytime hyperglycaemia requiring tailored monitoring and treatment. In acute illness, ensure hydration and glucose/ketone monitoring; do not stop basal insulin in type 1 diabetes. Sick-day instructions differ by drug, diabetes type and severity, so provide written, individualised advice.

12 · Monitoring and safe prescribing checklist

Review HbA1c trends, self-monitoring or CGM data, time below range, kidney function, electrolytes, blood pressure, weight, albuminuria, foot and retinal screening and treatment burden. At every insulin review check injection technique and access to supplies. Confirm allergies, drug interactions and dose adjustment; discuss driving rules for hypoglycaemia and occupational safety. Document shared decisions, follow-up timing and escalation thresholds.

13 · Clinical case 1: insulin and missed meals

A 23-year-old with type 1 diabetes uses basal–bolus insulin and becomes shaky after taking rapid-acting insulin but skipping lunch. Capillary glucose is 3.1 mmol/L and they are alert. Give fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck in 10–15 minutes, repeat if needed, then address the mismatch between mealtime insulin and carbohydrate. Explain that basal insulin should generally continue even during illness; tailor adjustments with the diabetes team.

14 · Clinical case 2: SGLT2-associated euglycaemic DKA

A 56-year-old taking empagliflozin has vomiting, abdominal pain and deep breathing after an infection; capillary glucose is 10 mmol/L. Normal-ish glucose does not exclude DKA. Stop the SGLT2 inhibitor, obtain blood ketones and venous gas urgently, and refer for emergency hospital treatment. Reinforce sick-day rules after recovery.

15 · Clinical case 3: recurrent hypoglycaemia in an older adult

An 81-year-old with CKD taking gliclazide has repeated morning confusion and a fall. Check capillary glucose promptly, treat hypoglycaemia, assess injuries and consider prolonged recurrence. Review the sulfonylurea, renal function, meal pattern and individualised HbA1c goal; consider safer therapy and involve the patient or carers.

16 · Clinical case 4: choosing therapy with heart failure

A 62-year-old with type 2 diabetes, heart failure and CKD needs medication review. Consider an SGLT2 inhibitor if appropriate for heart and kidney indications after checking renal thresholds, volume status and contraindications. Avoid pioglitazone in heart failure. Explain expected benefits, genital infection risk, hydration and DKA warning signs.

17 · PLAB 2 counselling: new insulin prescription

Introduce yourself and confirm the patient’s understanding. Explain why insulin is needed, demonstrate the exact pen and dose in units, timing with meals, injection sites and sharps disposal. Teach glucose monitoring, symptoms and treatment of hypoglycaemia, driving precautions, sick-day and ketone rules, and when to seek urgent help. Use teach-back and arrange diabetes specialist nurse follow-up.

18 · Exam synthesis and pitfalls

Type 1 diabetes always needs insulin. Metformin alone rarely causes hypoglycaemia. Sulfonylureas and insulin commonly do. SGLT2-associated DKA may occur without marked hyperglycaemia. Do not give oral sugar to an unconscious patient. Pioglitazone can worsen heart failure. Review renal function and patient-specific indications before prescribing; follow current NICE/BNF and local emergency protocols.
