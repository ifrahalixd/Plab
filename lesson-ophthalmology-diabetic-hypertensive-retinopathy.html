Diabetic & Hypertensive Retinopathy

Emergency red flags: sudden visual loss, flashes and a curtain over vision, or markedly raised blood pressure with acute organ injury or disc swelling.

Overview

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is retinal microvascular disease associated with diabetes. Hypertensive retinopathy is retinal vascular injury associated with raised blood pressure.

Retinal anatomy

The retina converts light into neural signals; the macula provides central detailed vision. Retinal capillary injury can cause leakage, ischaemia and new vessel growth.

Diabetes risk factors

Longer diabetes duration, raised HbA1c, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, renal disease and pregnancy can increase risk of diabetic retinal disease.

Early non-proliferative DR

Microaneurysms are characteristic early findings; dot-blot haemorrhages and hard exudates can develop.

More severe non-proliferative DR

Cotton-wool spots reflect nerve fibre layer ischaemia; venous beading and intraretinal microvascular abnormalities indicate more advanced disease.

Proliferative DR

Retinal ischaemia stimulates VEGF-mediated neovascularisation. Fragile vessels may cause vitreous haemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment.

Diabetic macular oedema

Leakage and retinal thickening at the macula can reduce central vision at any DR stage. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) helps assess oedema.

Symptoms and examination

DR may be asymptomatic until advanced. Ask about blurred vision, floaters, sudden visual loss and diabetes control; check visual acuity and perform fundus assessment.

UK diabetic eye screening

Eligible people with diabetes are invited to NHS diabetic eye screening; screening intervals and pathways depend on risk and local programme rules. Screening is distinct from a routine sight test.

DR prevention

Optimise glycaemic control, blood pressure and lipids, support smoking cessation and ensure attendance at screening. Avoid abrupt unsupervised medication changes.

DR treatment

Specialist management may involve intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment, retinal laser photocoagulation or vitrectomy, depending on stage, macular involvement and complications.

Pregnancy and diabetes

Pre-existing diabetes warrants retinal assessment before pregnancy where possible and during pregnancy under specialist guidance; pregnancy can accelerate retinopathy.

Hypertensive retinal changes

Arteriolar narrowing and arteriovenous nicking reflect chronic hypertension; flame haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots and hard exudates can occur with more severe injury.

Papilloedema and hypertensive emergency

Optic disc swelling with markedly elevated blood pressure and acute target-organ injury raises concern for hypertensive emergency. Arrange emergency assessment; do not manage as routine outpatient hypertension.

Differentials and emergencies

Sudden painless visual loss may be vitreous haemorrhage, retinal detachment or vascular occlusion. Flashes, floaters or a curtain over vision require urgent eye assessment.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that retinal disease may have no early symptoms. Discuss screening, glucose/BP control, smoking cessation, visual symptoms requiring urgent help and multidisciplinary follow-up.

PLAB 2: retinopathy consultation

Introduce yourself and obtain consent. Ask about visual changes, diabetes duration and control, blood pressure, medications, pregnancy, screening attendance and functional impact. Check acuity and perform appropriate eye examination. Explain asymptomatic retinal damage, the importance of screening, specialist referral when indicated, and urgent visual red flags.

Deep Dive

Microvascular disease

Hyperglycaemia causes capillary dysfunction and loss of pericytes, producing microaneurysms and leakage.

Ischaemia and neovascularisation

Capillary closure produces retinal hypoxia and VEGF signalling; new vessels are fragile and can bleed.

Why macular oedema matters

Fluid at the macula affects high-resolution central vision; OCT helps assess anatomy.

Hypertension

Chronic high pressure causes arteriolar narrowing and AV nicking; severe vascular injury may produce haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots.

Emergency distinction

Disc swelling with acute hypertensive organ injury warrants emergency management, not routine follow-up.
