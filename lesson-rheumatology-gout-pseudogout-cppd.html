Gout & Pseudogout (CPPD)

PLAB priority: A hot swollen joint can be septic arthritis. Exclude infection urgently when suspected, even if crystals are identified.

01. Overview

Gout is an inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals. Calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) disease causes acute CPP crystal arthritis, historically called pseudogout. Both can mimic infection.

PLAB application: An acutely painful swollen joint requires septic arthritis consideration, even when crystals are found.

02. Why gout occurs

Sustained hyperuricaemia promotes MSU crystal deposition; renal underexcretion is common. Hyperuricaemia alone does not establish gout and many people with high urate never develop attacks.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose gout from a urate result alone.

03. Risk factors

Gout risks include chronic kidney disease, obesity, alcohol, diuretics, metabolic syndrome, genetics and high purine intake. CPPD is associated with age, previous joint damage and sometimes haemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism or hypomagnesaemia.

PLAB application: Review medications and comorbidities without blaming patients.

04. Classic acute gout

Rapid onset severe pain, erythema, warmth and swelling, often overnight. First metatarsophalangeal joint involvement (podagra) is characteristic; ankles, knees and wrists also occur.

PLAB application: Recognise podagra, but remember gout may affect any joint.

05. CPPD presentation

Acute CPP crystal arthritis often involves the knee or wrist in older adults. CPPD may also cause chronic inflammatory or osteoarthritis-like symptoms.

PLAB application: A swollen knee in an older patient is not automatically pseudogout.

06. Septic arthritis: essential exclusion

Septic arthritis may present with a hot, swollen, exquisitely painful joint and reduced movement; fever can be absent. It is an emergency requiring urgent hospital assessment, aspiration and specialist-led antibiotics. Crystals and infection can coexist.

PLAB application: Never allow crystal identification to rule out infection.

07. Focused history

Ask onset, site, prior episodes, fever, trauma, skin breaks, prosthetic joint, immunosuppression, medications, kidney disease, alcohol and functional impact. Check for recent illness and sexual health risks where appropriate.

PLAB application: Establish red flags before planning outpatient treatment.

08. Examination

Assess vital signs and sepsis features; inspect swelling, erythema and skin, compare joints, assess active/passive movement, neurovascular status and potential tophi. Avoid forceful painful manoeuvres.

PLAB application: Acutely unwell patients need urgent escalation.

09. Joint aspiration and microscopy

Synovial fluid should be examined for crystals, white cells, Gram stain and culture when septic arthritis is possible. MSU crystals are needle-shaped and strongly negatively birefringent; CPP crystals are often rhomboid and weakly positively birefringent.

PLAB application: Joint fluid culture remains important even if crystals are seen.

10. Serum urate and imaging

Serum urate can be normal during an acute gout flare; repeat at least 2 weeks after flare resolution if clinical suspicion persists. Ultrasound may show double-contour sign or tophi in gout; radiographs may show chondrocalcinosis in CPPD, but absence does not exclude it.

PLAB application: A normal urate during a flare does not rule out gout.

11. Treating an acute gout flare

NICE NG219 recommends an NSAID, colchicine or a short course of oral corticosteroid as first-line options, taking comorbidities, interactions and preferences into account. Consider gastroprotection with NSAIDs. Intra-articular or intramuscular steroid may be appropriate when alternatives are unsuitable and infection excluded.

PLAB application: Choose therapy based on renal function, gastrointestinal risks and interactions.

12. Treating acute CPP crystal arthritis

Rest, ice and suitable anti-inflammatory treatment can help. NSAIDs, colchicine or corticosteroids may be used based on patient factors; aspiration and intra-articular corticosteroid may help once infection is excluded.

PLAB application: There is no urate-lowering role for allopurinol in CPPD.

13. Long-term gout prevention

Discuss urate-lowering therapy (ULT) particularly with recurrent or troublesome flares, tophi, CKD stages 3–5, diuretic use or chronic gouty arthritis. NICE recommends treat-to-target urate below 360 micromol/L (6 mg/dL); consider below 300 micromol/L for tophi or ongoing frequent flares.

PLAB application: Distinguish acute anti-inflammatory treatment from chronic urate reduction.

14. Allopurinol and febuxostat

Allopurinol or febuxostat can be used as first-line ULT under NICE NG219, considering cardiovascular risk and local prescribing advice. Start low and titrate using serial urate measurements. Explain rash warning signs and the need for adherence. Do not routinely stop established ULT during a flare.

PLAB application: Review renal function, drug interactions and adverse effects.

15. Starting ULT and flare prophylaxis

Offer prophylaxis, usually colchicine when suitable, during ULT initiation or dose titration because early flares can occur. NICE generally suggests starting ULT 2–4 weeks after a flare settles, but consider starting during a flare if attacks are frequent. Shared decisions matter.

PLAB application: Explain that an early flare does not mean ULT is failing.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety net

Explain the diagnosis without stigma, use shared decisions on medication, gradual lifestyle changes and hydration, and discuss cardiovascular/renal risk. Seek urgent care for fever, rapidly worsening pain, inability to bear weight, systemic illness or a suspected infected joint.

PLAB application: Use clear safety-netting and check understanding.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Crystal-triggered innate immunity

MSU crystals activate innate immune pathways, including NLRP3 inflammasome signalling and IL-1β release. Neutrophil recruitment produces sudden severe synovitis.

Crystal microscopy and limitations

MSU crystals are typically long and needle-shaped with strong negative birefringence. CPP crystals are often shorter, rhomboid and weakly positively birefringent. Operator skill matters, and finding crystals does not exclude infection.

Why urate can mislead during attacks

Serum urate can transiently decrease during acute inflammation. A single result during a flare is not a reliable rule-out test; repeat at least two weeks after resolution if indicated.

Treat-to-target ULT rationale

Sustained urate lowering below saturation allows gradual dissolution of MSU deposits and reduces future flares. Early mobilisation of deposits can transiently increase attacks, hence prophylaxis.

CPPD versus gout

CPPD involves calcium pyrophosphate crystals and may cause acute synovitis, chondrocalcinosis or chronic joint symptoms. Unlike gout, no established medication removes CPP crystal burden.

The septic arthritis trap

Bacterial joint infection can destroy cartilage rapidly. Fever may be absent, and simultaneous crystal disease is possible. A concerning hot joint needs urgent specialist evaluation and appropriate synovial sampling.
