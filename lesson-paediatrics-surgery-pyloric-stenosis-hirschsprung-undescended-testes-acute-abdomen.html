Paediatric Surgery: Pyloric Stenosis, Hirschsprung Disease, Undescended Testes & Acute Abdomen

Emergency red flags: Green bilious vomiting, sudden testicular pain, peritonitis, shock, painful irreducible hernia, or a toxic infant with abdominal distension. Escalate immediately.

Surgical assessment of the child

Start with ABCDE, age, onset and progression of pain, vomiting colour, bowel motions, flatus, urine output, fever, trauma and previous operations. Assess hydration, perfusion, distension, tenderness, guarding, hernias and testicular position. Give appropriate analgesia early.

PLAB application: A well-appearing child may still have an evolving surgical emergency; repeat observations and examination.

Bilious vomiting in a neonate

Green vomit suggests obstruction distal to the ampulla of Vater. Malrotation with midgut volvulus can rapidly compromise bowel perfusion. Keep nil by mouth, obtain IV access, start resuscitation as indicated, insert a nasogastric tube on specialist advice and contact neonatal surgery immediately.

PLAB application: Do not attribute green vomiting to simple reflux; urgent surgical assessment is essential.

Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis

Usually presents at approximately 2–8 weeks with progressive forceful non-bilious vomiting after feeds, persistent hunger, dehydration and weight faltering. An olive-shaped mass may be palpable but is often absent. Ultrasound confirms diagnosis; prolonged vomiting can cause hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis.

PLAB application: Correct fluid and electrolyte disturbances before pyloromyotomy; this is not an immediate operation before stabilisation.

Hirschsprung disease

Aganglionosis of distal bowel causes functional obstruction. Features include failure to pass meconium within 48 hours, distension, bilious vomiting and later severe constipation or growth problems. Rectal suction biopsy establishes diagnosis; contrast enema may support assessment.

PLAB application: Fever, explosive diarrhoea, distension and toxicity suggest Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis: an emergency.

Intussusception

Typically affects infants and toddlers, with episodic severe colicky pain, drawing up legs, pallor and vomiting; the child may appear normal between episodes. Lethargy may dominate. Ultrasound may show a target sign. Air/contrast enema reduction is often possible when stable and without perforation.

PLAB application: Redcurrant jelly stool is a late sign; do not wait for the classic triad.

Appendicitis

Pain may begin centrally and migrate to the right iliac fossa with anorexia, fever, nausea and localised tenderness. Younger children can present atypically and perforate early. Pregnancy and gynaecological causes matter in adolescents. Ultrasound is often first-line imaging when indicated.

PLAB application: A normal inflammatory marker or absent fever cannot safely rule out appendicitis.

Testicular torsion

Sudden unilateral testicular or groin pain, nausea and a high-riding or abnormal lie testis may occur; abdominal pain alone is possible. Immediate urological/surgical review is required. Imaging must not delay exploration when torsion is suspected.

PLAB application: Time-critical: testicular viability falls with prolonged ischaemia.

Undescended testes

Examine both testes in a warm room and distinguish palpable undescended testes from retractile testes. Persistent undescended testes after 6 months corrected age warrant specialist referral; orchidopexy is generally planned in infancy, often by 12 months. Bilateral non-palpable testes require urgent specialist endocrine/urological assessment.

PLAB application: Do not order routine ultrasound before referral for an otherwise uncomplicated undescended testis.

Inguinal hernia and incarceration

An intermittent groin or scrotal swelling, particularly in infants or former premature babies, suggests an inguinal hernia. A painful irreducible swelling with vomiting or distress suggests incarceration/strangulation. Urgent surgical assessment is needed; avoid repeated forceful attempts at reduction.

PLAB application: Differentiate an inguinal hernia from a hydrocele; an irreducible painful lump is an emergency.

Acute scrotum differential

Torsion, torsion of an appendix testis, epididymo-orchitis, trauma and incarcerated hernia may mimic one another. Examine abdomen, groin and scrotum sensitively with a chaperone as appropriate. A normal urinalysis does not exclude torsion.

PLAB application: If torsion cannot be confidently excluded, escalate immediately.

Bowel obstruction and volvulus

Obstruction may cause bilious vomiting, abdominal distension, pain, failure to pass stool and dehydration. Malrotation/volvulus, atresia, meconium ileus and adhesions are important causes depending on age. Bowel ischaemia may progress to shock, acidosis and peritonitis.

PLAB application: Do not delay surgical referral for imaging in an unstable child.

Necrotising enterocolitis

Primarily affects premature or medically vulnerable neonates. Abdominal distension, feed intolerance, bloody stools, apnoea, temperature instability and systemic deterioration are concerning. Management requires urgent neonatal team review, bowel rest, gastric decompression, antibiotics and surgical consultation where indicated.

PLAB application: Pneumatosis intestinalis on imaging is a characteristic finding, but management begins on clinical suspicion.

Abdominal wall and umbilical conditions

Gastroschisis exposes bowel without a covering sac, usually to the right of the umbilicus; omphalocele has a membranous sac and may coexist with other anomalies. Protect exposed bowel, prevent heat/fluid loss and involve neonatal surgery. Uncomplicated umbilical hernias often resolve spontaneously.

PLAB application: Never apply pressure or attempt to push exposed bowel back into the abdomen.

Paediatric trauma and abdominal pain

Consider blunt abdominal injury, splenic or hepatic injury, and non-accidental injury when the history is concerning. Check haemodynamic status, mechanism and associated injuries. A child may compensate despite significant blood loss. Involve senior emergency, surgical and safeguarding teams as appropriate.

PLAB application: Unexplained injury or inconsistent history requires objective documentation and safeguarding escalation.

Initial management of surgical emergencies

Call for senior help, ABCDE assessment, monitoring, bedside glucose, IV/IO access when needed, appropriate fluid resuscitation, analgesia, nil by mouth and targeted blood tests. Antibiotics are indicated for suspected perforation, peritonitis or serious infection under local guidance. Reassess frequently and arrange safe transfer.

PLAB application: Do not let investigations delay treatment of shock, volvulus or torsion.

PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain what is suspected and why urgent assessment may be needed; avoid false reassurance. Obtain a focused history from caregiver and child, address distress and explain consent, chaperones and examination sensitively. Safety-net for green vomiting, worsening pain, distension, persistent vomiting, blood in stool, lethargy and scrotal pain.

PLAB application: Use clear language: “Green vomit can mean a blockage and needs emergency assessment.”

PLAB 2 OSCE — vomiting infant

Introduce yourself; assess immediate stability and hydration; ask specifically whether vomit is green or blood-stained, about passage of meconium and stools, feeding, urine output and weight. Explain that green vomiting requires emergency surgical assessment. Do not offer a routine discharge plan when red flags are present.

Deep Dive — Clinical reasoning

Why green vomit matters

Bile enters the intestine beyond the stomach. True green emesis can indicate intestinal obstruction and must prompt urgent assessment, particularly in neonates. Yellow milk-stained vomit is not equivalent, but colour descriptions should be clarified without delaying care.

Pyloric stenosis: resuscitate before surgery

Repeated loss of gastric hydrochloric acid produces chloride depletion and alkalosis; dehydration and potassium depletion may coexist. Ultrasound confirms pyloric muscle thickening. Replace fluids and correct electrolytes before definitive pyloromyotomy.

Hirschsprung: pathology and enterocolitis

Absent enteric ganglion cells prevent normal distal bowel relaxation. Definitive diagnosis is by rectal biopsy. Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis can occur before or after surgery and presents with systemic illness, distension and diarrhoea; urgent antibiotics, fluids and specialist bowel decompression may be needed.

The painful scrotum is time critical

Torsion can be intermittent, can present with abdominal pain and may have an atypical examination. Clinical suspicion mandates immediate surgical discussion. Doppler ultrasound may assist selected uncertain cases but must never postpone exploration when torsion is likely.

Intussusception: intermittent does not mean benign

Venous congestion and bowel oedema can progress to ischaemia. A child may be pale or lethargic between painful episodes. Ultrasound is preferred for diagnosis; enema reduction requires appropriate specialist supervision and is contraindicated in perforation or peritonitis.

Age helps structure the differential

Newborn: malrotation, atresia, Hirschsprung, NEC. Young infant: pyloric stenosis and incarcerated hernia. Toddler: intussusception. School-age: appendicitis. Adolescent: appendicitis, torsion and pregnancy-related or gynaecological emergencies. These are patterns, not exclusion rules.
