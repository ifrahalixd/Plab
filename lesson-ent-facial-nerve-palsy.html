Facial Nerve Palsy

Red flags: sudden facial weakness with other neurological symptoms is a stroke emergency. Inability to close the eye can threaten vision.

Facial nerve anatomy

Cranial nerve VII supplies muscles of facial expression, stapedius, taste to the anterior two-thirds of tongue and parasympathetic fibres. It traverses the temporal bone and parotid gland.

PLAB application: Check forehead, eye closure, smile and cheek puffing.

Upper versus lower motor neurone

A typical supranuclear lesion causes contralateral lower facial weakness with relative forehead sparing. Peripheral lesions usually weaken the ipsilateral upper and lower face.

PLAB application: Forehead sparing is not perfectly reliable: assess for stroke symptoms in every acute presentation.

Bell’s palsy

Acute idiopathic unilateral lower motor neurone facial weakness develops over hours to days, often reaching maximum severity within 72 hours. It is a diagnosis of exclusion.

PLAB application: Ask about gradual onset, recurrent palsy, ear symptoms, rash, trauma and other neurological deficits.

Initial assessment

Establish exact onset, progression, weakness distribution, headache, limb symptoms, speech, swallowing, hearing, vertigo, ear pain, vesicles and eye symptoms.

PLAB application: Check vital signs, full cranial nerves, limb neurology and gait when appropriate.

Stroke exclusion

Sudden facial weakness with limb weakness, dysarthria, visual symptoms, ataxia or other focal neurological signs requires emergency stroke assessment.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose Bell’s palsy solely because the forehead moves or fails to move.

Bell’s palsy corticosteroids

For adults presenting within 72 hours, offer oral prednisolone according to current local/BNF guidance after considering contraindications and shared decision-making. Benefit is greatest when started early.

PLAB application: Confirm diabetes, infection risks, pregnancy, interactions and suitability; do not delay emergency stroke care.

Eye protection

Incomplete eyelid closure risks exposure keratopathy and corneal ulceration. Use lubricating eye drops during the day and ointment at night as appropriate; protect or tape the eye closed for sleep safely.

PLAB application: Eye pain, redness or visual change requires urgent ophthalmic assessment.

Antivirals

Antivirals alone are not recommended for typical Bell’s palsy. Combination with steroids may be considered in selected severe cases or when herpes zoster is suspected under local guidance.

PLAB application: Ramsay Hunt syndrome requires prompt specialist-guided treatment.

Ramsay Hunt syndrome

Herpes zoster oticus can cause severe ear pain, vesicles around the ear or mouth and ipsilateral peripheral facial palsy; hearing loss or vertigo may coexist.

PLAB application: Look carefully for vesicles; early antivirals and steroids are often considered with specialist input.

Otitis and mastoiditis

Acute otitis media, cholesteatoma or mastoiditis may rarely produce facial palsy. Fever, otorrhoea, severe otalgia or mastoid swelling are warning signs.

PLAB application: Urgent ENT evaluation is needed for facial palsy with ear infection.

Trauma and parotid disease

Temporal bone fracture can injure CN VII. A slowly progressive or recurrent facial palsy, especially with parotid mass, may indicate tumour or another structural cause.

PLAB application: Progressive weakness is not typical of uncomplicated Bell’s palsy.

Other differentials

Consider Lyme disease with exposure risk, Guillain–Barré syndrome (especially bilateral weakness), sarcoidosis, diabetes-associated neuropathy and other neurological disease.

PLAB application: Bilateral palsy or additional neurological signs warrants specialist assessment.

Investigations

Typical isolated Bell’s palsy is usually diagnosed clinically without routine imaging. Atypical, progressive, recurrent or non-resolving cases may need targeted blood tests, imaging or specialist referral.

PLAB application: Test according to findings, not indiscriminately.

Recovery and prognosis

Many patients improve over weeks to months, though some have persistent weakness, synkinesis or eye problems. Arrange follow-up and consider facial therapy or specialist referral for incomplete recovery.

PLAB application: Set realistic expectations and document baseline facial function.

Referral thresholds

Emergency referral for suspected stroke; urgent ophthalmology for corneal risk or visual symptoms; urgent ENT for Ramsay Hunt, ear infection complications, trauma or progressive weakness.

PLAB application: If no improvement after about 3 weeks or incomplete recovery after 3 months, reassess and consider referral.

PLAB 2 consultation

Explain the diagnosis and uncertainty, offer treatment within the therapeutic window, demonstrate eye care, discuss recovery and agree safety-netting.

PLAB application: Check understanding, concerns, occupation and practical ability to use eye protection.

PLAB 2 OSCE: new facial weakness

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, assess onset and screen urgently for stroke.
  2. Ask about ear pain, rash, hearing symptoms, trauma, recurrence, eye closure and other neurological symptoms.
  3. Examine facial movements, both ears, other cranial nerves and relevant limb neurology.
  4. Explain likely diagnosis and uncertainty; discuss early steroids when suitable.
  5. Demonstrate eye lubrication/protection and provide urgent return precautions.

Deep Dive

Why forehead weakness matters

Bilateral cortical input often preserves upper-face movement after a supranuclear lesion, whereas a peripheral CN VII lesion affects both upper and lower facial muscles. This is a clue, not a definitive stroke exclusion test.

Why steroids are time-sensitive

Early corticosteroid therapy reduces inflammation and improves chances of complete recovery in eligible patients with Bell’s palsy.

Why eye care is urgent

Weak orbicularis oculi and impaired blinking expose the cornea, increasing risk of dryness, ulceration and visual impairment.

Ramsay Hunt versus Bell’s palsy

Vesicular rash, severe otalgia, hearing loss or vertigo points toward zoster-associated facial palsy rather than uncomplicated Bell’s palsy.

When the course is atypical

Gradual progression, recurrence, bilateral involvement or additional neurological signs require investigation for another cause.
