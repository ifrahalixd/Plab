Bronchiectasis: diagnosis, airway clearance and exacerbations

Emergency reminder: Major haemoptysis, severe breathlessness, confusion or sepsis requires emergency assessment and escalation. This lesson is educational, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

Definition and pathophysiology

Bronchiectasis is permanent abnormal dilatation of bronchi associated with chronic airway inflammation, impaired mucus clearance and recurrent infection.

Clinical application: A vicious cycle of infection, inflammation and airway injury causes daily sputum and exacerbations.

Clinical features

Chronic productive cough, recurrent chest infections, breathlessness, fatigue and sometimes haemoptysis are typical. Crackles and wheeze may be heard.

Clinical application: Ask about sputum volume and colour, exacerbation frequency, weight loss, sinus disease and quality of life.

Causes and associations

Previous severe infection, immune deficiency, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA), aspiration, autoimmune disease, ciliary dysfunction and cystic fibrosis may contribute.

Clinical application: Investigate underlying causes; some are treatable and should not be missed.

Diagnosis with CT

Thin-section chest CT confirms bronchiectasis; chest radiography may be normal or nonspecific. CT may show bronchial dilatation and lack of normal tapering.

Clinical application: Obtain sputum cultures and assess lung function, symptoms and exacerbations alongside imaging.

Microbiology

Sputum culture helps identify organisms and guide antibiotics. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is associated with more severe disease and may require specialist eradication or long-term strategies.

Clinical application: Collect sputum before antibiotics when feasible without delaying urgent treatment; review previous culture results.

Baseline investigations

Consider full blood count, immunoglobulins, ABPA testing and other investigations guided by history; perform spirometry and assess oxygenation where indicated.

Clinical application: British Thoracic Society guidance recommends a structured aetiological assessment and specialist input.

Airway clearance

Airway clearance is a cornerstone of treatment. Respiratory physiotherapists teach techniques such as active cycle of breathing, huff coughing and positive expiratory pressure devices.

Clinical application: Develop a daily individualised plan and increase clearance during exacerbations.

Exercise and prevention

Physical activity, pulmonary rehabilitation for exercise limitation, vaccinations and smoking cessation support long-term health.

Clinical application: Review nutrition, reflux, sinus symptoms and comorbidities.

Stable medication

Routine inhaled corticosteroids are not indicated for bronchiectasis alone unless another condition such as asthma or COPD justifies them. Bronchodilators may help coexisting obstruction.

Clinical application: Avoid automatic ICS prescribing; tailor to physiology and comorbid disease.

Exacerbation recognition

An exacerbation involves acute worsening of cough, sputum, breathlessness or systemic symptoms beyond usual variation.

Clinical application: Assess severity, oxygenation, sepsis features and differential diagnoses such as pneumonia or PE.

Exacerbation treatment

Antibiotics are often required for bacterial exacerbations, selected using prior sputum microbiology, allergies, severity and local guidance. NICE NG117 addresses antimicrobial prescribing.

Clinical application: Send sputum for culture where possible; optimise airway clearance and reassess response. Course length depends on severity and organism.

When to admit

Severe breathlessness, hypoxaemia, sepsis, haemodynamic instability, inability to take oral treatment or failure of outpatient treatment may require hospital care.

Clinical application: Use ABCDE and prompt escalation for respiratory failure or sepsis.

Haemoptysis

Blood-streaked sputum can occur, but major haemoptysis is an emergency. Airway compromise and blood loss take priority.

Clinical application: Urgent specialist assessment is needed for significant or recurrent bleeding; bronchial artery embolisation may be considered.

Long-term antibiotics

Frequent exacerbations may warrant specialist consideration of long-term macrolides or inhaled antibiotics in selected patients.

Clinical application: Before macrolides, evaluate non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection, ECG/QT risk, hearing and drug interactions; monitor for resistance.

Pseudomonas and NTM

Chronic Pseudomonas infection may influence treatment. Non-tuberculous mycobacteria require careful microbiological diagnosis; not every positive culture means disease.

Clinical application: Avoid unplanned macrolide monotherapy if NTM disease is possible because of resistance risk.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that damaged airways trap mucus, causing infections. Discuss sputum collection, airway clearance, exercise, vaccinations and an exacerbation action plan.

Clinical application: Safety-net for haemoptysis, worsening breathlessness, chest pain, high fever or confusion.

PLAB 2: Bronchiectasis counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, obtain consent and ask about cough, sputum, infections and haemoptysis.
  2. Explain CT confirmation and sputum testing.
  3. Discuss daily airway clearance, exercise, vaccinations and smoking cessation.
  4. Explain when antibiotics or specialist care are needed.
  5. Safety-net for major haemoptysis, worsening breathlessness or sepsis.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Vicious cycle

Infection triggers neutrophilic inflammation; airway damage impairs mucus clearance, increasing infection risk.

Why CT is required

Plain films may be nonspecific. CT can show bronchial dilatation and loss of tapering.

Pseudomonas

Persistent infection is associated with more exacerbations and may require specialist eradication or suppressive treatment.

Why physiotherapy works

Airway clearance techniques mobilise retained secretions and support cough clearance.

NTM caution

Long-term macrolide monotherapy can cause resistance if unrecognised NTM infection is present.

Haemoptysis physiology

Inflamed, enlarged bronchial arteries can bleed; significant haemoptysis is an airway emergency.
