Acute Pancreatitis, Chronic Pancreatitis & Pancreatic Cancer

Surgery · Lesson 12 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Pancreatic disease: diagnosis, severity and urgent action

Emergency: Acute pancreatitis may cause shock, hypoxia, acute kidney injury and organ failure. Start ABCDE, adequate analgesia, monitored IV crystalloid resuscitation when indicated, and urgent senior review. Persistent organ failure or clinical deterioration requires critical-care assessment.

1 · Acute pancreatitis: causes and diagnosis

Inflammation from premature pancreatic enzyme activation. Common causes are gallstones and alcohol; consider hypertriglyceridaemia, hypercalcaemia, drugs, ERCP and malignancy. Typical presentation: sudden severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, nausea and vomiting. Diagnose when two of three are present: characteristic pain, serum lipase/amylase ≥3× upper limit of normal, or characteristic imaging. Enzymes may be normal late or in selected circumstances; interpret clinically.

2 · Initial investigations

Observations, fluid status, urine output, oxygenation and mental status. Request FBC, U&E/creatinine, LFTs, calcium, glucose, CRP, lipase (or amylase), triglycerides if cause unclear, VBG/ABG and lactate if unwell. ECG if cardiac differential. Ultrasound to look for gallstones and duct dilatation. Contrast CT is not routinely required immediately for a clear uncomplicated diagnosis; use for diagnostic uncertainty, deterioration or complications, often after the first 48–72 hours when necrosis is more apparent.

3 · Severity and complications

CategoryDefinition / implications
MildNo organ failure or local/systemic complications
Moderately severeTransient organ failure (<48 h) and/or local/systemic complications
SeverePersistent organ failure (>48 h); high-risk and often critical-care needs

Watch for hypovolaemia, respiratory failure/ARDS, AKI, hypocalcaemia, pancreatic or peripancreatic fluid collections, pseudocyst, necrosis and infected necrosis. Cullen and Grey Turner signs are rare late signs of haemorrhagic disease, not reliable screening tools. Severity requires repeated clinical reassessment, not an isolated enzyme level.

4 · Management of acute pancreatitis

Admit, give effective analgesia and antiemetics; provide goal-directed IV crystalloid if needed, reassessing perfusion, urine output and risk of fluid overload. Give oxygen if hypoxic, VTE prevention as appropriate and monitor organ function. Encourage early oral feeding as tolerated in mild disease; use enteral nutrition in severe disease when oral intake is inadequate. Do not keep everyone nil by mouth for prolonged periods. Do not prescribe prophylactic antibiotics for sterile pancreatitis or sterile necrosis. Give antibiotics for proven or strongly suspected infection and source control as indicated. Urgent ERCP is for coexisting cholangitis or ongoing biliary obstruction, not for every case of gallstone pancreatitis. Discuss same-admission cholecystectomy after mild gallstone pancreatitis when suitable; severe cases may need delayed surgery.

5 · Chronic pancreatitis

Progressive inflammation and fibrosis cause recurrent/chronic epigastric pain radiating to the back, pancreatic calcification, duct abnormalities, steatorrhoea, malabsorption, weight loss and type 3c diabetes. Risks include alcohol, smoking, genetic predisposition and recurrent acute pancreatitis. CT/MRCP/EUS may support diagnosis. Management: stop alcohol and smoking, dietetic input, pain management, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy for exocrine insufficiency, fat-soluble vitamin/nutritional assessment and diabetes care. Endoscopic or surgical intervention may be considered for selected duct obstruction or refractory pain.

6 · Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma may present with painless progressive jaundice (head of pancreas), dark urine, pale stools, pruritus, weight loss, anorexia, epigastric/back pain, new-onset diabetes or thromboembolism. Urgent suspected cancer assessment is needed for concerning features; follow current NICE NG12 age/symptom referral criteria. Investigation may include pancreatic-protocol contrast CT for diagnosis/staging, EUS-guided biopsy when tissue is required, and specialist MDT review. CA19-9 is not a screening or standalone diagnostic test and may rise with benign biliary obstruction. Treatment depends on resectability and fitness: surgery (e.g. Whipple for selected head lesions), systemic therapy, biliary stenting and palliative support.

7 · PLAB differentiators

Acute pancreatitis: abrupt severe pain + enzyme rise. Chronic pancreatitis: longstanding pain, steatorrhoea, diabetes and calcification. Pancreatic cancer: progressive jaundice, weight loss and new diabetes; painless jaundice is a major red flag. Persistent jaundice with fever/rigors also raises concern for ascending cholangitis and needs emergency assessment.

Clinical pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Severe epigastric pain

ABCDE → analgesia, observations and IV access → lipase/amylase, FBC, U&E, LFTs, calcium, glucose → diagnosis using 2/3 criteria → gallstone ultrasound → reassess for organ failure → early feeding when tolerated, monitored fluids, treat cause.

Pathway B — Deteriorating pancreatitis

Hypotension, hypoxia, oliguria or confusion → urgent senior/critical-care review → repeated labs, perfusion and urine-output assessment → CT if indicated to assess complications → multidisciplinary intervention for infected necrosis or other complications.

Pathway C — Painless jaundice and weight loss

Assess for cholangitis and other urgent causes → LFTs and appropriate imaging → urgent suspected pancreatic cancer pathway → pancreatic-protocol CT and specialist MDT → stage and plan treatment/palliation.

Slide 1 — Diagnostic criteria

Acute pancreatitis: 2 of characteristic pain, enzymes ≥3× ULN, imaging.

Slide 2 — Antibiotic trap

No routine prophylactic antibiotics for sterile pancreatitis/necrosis.

Slide 3 — Nutrition trap

Early oral feeding when tolerated; enteral nutrition if needed.

Slide 4 — Cancer trap

CA19-9 does not replace imaging or biopsy where indicated.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Sudden epigastric pain

A 43-year-old develops severe epigastric pain radiating to the back with vomiting; lipase is 6× ULN. Answer: acute pancreatitis. Assess severity, treat pain, monitor fluids/organ function, check gallstones and other causes.

Case 2 — Gallstones with fever and jaundice

A 67-year-old has pancreatitis plus fever, jaundice, rigors and hypotension. Answer: possible ascending cholangitis with gallstone pancreatitis. Immediate sepsis care, IV antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage assessment, usually ERCP.

Case 3 — Steatorrhoea and diabetes

A 52-year-old with long alcohol exposure has chronic epigastric pain, oily stools, weight loss and newly diagnosed diabetes. CT shows pancreatic calcifications. Answer: chronic pancreatitis with exocrine and endocrine insufficiency; address alcohol/smoking, enzyme replacement, nutrition and diabetes.

Case 4 — Painless jaundice

A 71-year-old reports dark urine, pale stools, itch and unintentional weight loss without pain. Answer: pancreatic head cancer is a key differential. Urgent cancer pathway and pancreatic imaging; assess for other obstructive causes and cholangitis.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute pancreatic symptoms station

Brief: A 69-year-old reports jaundice, itching and weight loss. Take a focused history, explain concerns and outline next steps.

Station checklist

  1. Introduce, confirm identity and consent; assess distress and observations.
  2. Ask jaundice onset/progression, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus and abdominal/back pain.
  3. Explore weight loss, appetite, vomiting, altered bowel habit, steatorrhoea and new diabetes.
  4. Screen fever/rigors, confusion and hypotension symptoms for cholangitis.
  5. Ask smoking, alcohol, previous pancreatitis, gallstones, family history and medication.
  6. Examine general appearance, jaundice, abdomen and hydration; assess urgent red flags.
  7. Explain LFTs, blood counts, glucose and imaging; arrange urgent suspected cancer assessment.
  8. Discuss specialist pancreatic CT, possible EUS/biopsy and MDT decisions without prematurely confirming cancer.
  9. Communicate uncertainty empathetically; invite questions and assess support needs.
  10. Safety-net for fever, worsening jaundice, severe pain, vomiting, confusion or collapse.

Patient-friendly explanation: “Yellowing of the skin can happen when bile cannot drain properly. There are several possible reasons, including a blockage. Because you have also lost weight, we need to investigate urgently to rule out a serious cause.”

Rapid revision and UK references

  1. Acute pancreatitis diagnosis: any 2 of typical pain, lipase/amylase ≥3× ULN, or characteristic imaging.
  2. Gallstones and alcohol are common causes; investigate aetiology.
  3. Severe disease is defined by persistent organ failure over 48 hours.
  4. Resuscitate carefully and reassess; avoid indiscriminate aggressive fluid loading.
  5. Early oral/enteral nutrition is preferred when tolerated.
  6. No prophylactic antibiotics for sterile pancreatic inflammation or necrosis.
  7. Urgent ERCP when cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction warrants drainage.
  8. Chronic pancreatitis causes pain, steatorrhoea and diabetes; treat exocrine insufficiency with enzymes.
  9. Painless progressive jaundice and weight loss require urgent pancreatic cancer assessment.
  10. CA19-9 is not a stand-alone cancer test.
