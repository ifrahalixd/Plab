Deep Dive · Renal physiology and prescribing reasoning

Glomerular filtration and autoregulation

GFR reflects net filtration pressure and filtration coefficient. Afferent arteriolar dilation raises glomerular capillary pressure; efferent constriction can initially preserve filtration. NSAIDs inhibit prostaglandin-mediated afferent dilation, while ACE inhibitors/ARBs reduce angiotensin II-mediated efferent constriction. In volume depletion, the combination can cause a marked decline in GFR.

Transporter map

Proximal tubule: carbonic anhydrase and SGLT2. Thick ascending limb: NKCC2. Distal convoluted tubule: NCC. Collecting duct: ENaC and mineralocorticoid receptor. Blocking sodium reabsorption upstream increases sodium delivery downstream, influencing potassium and hydrogen secretion.

Why loop diuretics cause metabolic alkalosis

Increased distal sodium delivery and aldosterone activity favour potassium and hydrogen ion secretion. Volume contraction also sustains bicarbonate retention. Assess chloride, potassium and volume status when interpreting alkalosis.

Diuretic resistance

Reduced renal perfusion, impaired gut absorption, high dietary sodium, nephron adaptation and drug interactions can blunt diuresis. Escalation is not automatically safe; reassess diagnosis, adherence, congestion and renal function, and involve senior teams for combination strategies.

Fractional excretion pitfalls

Urinary sodium and fractional excretion indices can be misleading after diuretics, in CKD, sepsis and mixed injury. They do not replace examination, trend analysis, urinalysis and imaging where indicated.

Renal dose calculation

Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance estimates depend on age, weight, sex and serum creatinine. The correct weight descriptor and applicability vary by body habitus and drug. eGFR is indexed to 1.73 m² and may need adjustment in unusual body size. In evolving AKI, estimates lag true function; seek pharmacy or renal advice for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.

Hyperkalaemia mechanisms

Reduced renal excretion, acidosis, tissue breakdown and drugs that impair aldosterone signalling may increase potassium. ECG severity does not correlate perfectly with serum concentration. Calcium raises the threshold potential of cardiomyocytes, temporarily reducing excitability; insulin activates Na+/K+-ATPase, shifting potassium intracellularly. Neither replaces potassium elimination.

Hyponatraemia and brain adaptation

Chronic hypotonic hyponatraemia leads brain cells to shed osmolytes. Rapid correction then risks cellular dehydration and osmotic demyelination. Acute severe symptomatic cases require urgent treatment, but correction limits and monitoring depend on chronicity and individual risk.

CKD-mineral bone disease

Declining nephron mass reduces phosphate excretion and calcitriol synthesis. FGF23 and PTH rise, altering calcium-phosphate balance and bone turnover. Treatment depends on serial biochemical findings and specialist recommendations, not a single calcium result.

PLAB prescribing synthesis

Never prescribe a diuretic on the basis of ankle swelling alone. Distinguish cardiac, renal, hepatic and venous causes; check BP and perfusion; choose the class for the indication; consider interactions and pregnancy; document electrolyte and renal monitoring; and arrange review of clinical response.

1. Renal pharmacology: the clinical framework

Before prescribing, determine whether the problem is fluid overload, hypertension, electrolyte imbalance, acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease. Assess perfusion, blood pressure, fluid balance, urine output, weight, serum creatinine trend and potassium. A normal-looking creatinine does not rule out impaired renal function in a person with low muscle mass. Consider obstruction, sepsis, nephrotoxins and concurrent medicines.

2. Nephron physiology and drug targets

The proximal tubule reabsorbs most filtered sodium and water. The thick ascending limb uses NKCC2 to reabsorb sodium, potassium and chloride and generates the medullary gradient. The distal convoluted tubule uses NCC; the collecting duct uses ENaC and aldosterone-regulated potassium secretion. ADH increases collecting-duct water permeability. Each diuretic class blocks a different transporter and creates a characteristic electrolyte pattern.

3. Loop diuretics: furosemide and bumetanide

Loop diuretics inhibit NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb, causing powerful natriuresis and increased urinary calcium and magnesium losses. They relieve congestion in heart failure and oedematous states; they do not directly treat intrinsic AKI or improve kidney recovery. Monitor volume status, weight, BP, creatinine, sodium, potassium and magnesium. Risks include dehydration, hypotension, hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis and ototoxicity, particularly with high exposure or interacting ototoxic drugs.

4. Thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics

Bendroflumethiazide, indapamide and chlortalidone inhibit NCC in the distal tubule. They are used in selected hypertension and fluid-management settings. Thiazides reduce urinary calcium excretion but can cause hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia, hyperuricaemia and glucose intolerance. Severe hyponatraemia may develop after starting treatment, especially in older adults; assess symptoms and check electrolytes after initiation and dose changes.

5. Potassium-sparing diuretics

Amiloride blocks ENaC in the collecting duct; spironolactone and eplerenone antagonise mineralocorticoid receptors. They reduce potassium excretion and may be indicated in selected heart failure or hyperaldosteronism. Hyperkalaemia risk increases with CKD, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium supplements and dehydration. Check baseline and follow-up potassium and kidney function. Spironolactone can cause gynaecomastia; eplerenone has fewer antiandrogenic effects.

6. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors and osmotic diuretics

Acetazolamide inhibits proximal-tubule carbonic anhydrase, increasing bicarbonate loss and causing metabolic acidosis; selected uses include glaucoma, altitude illness and some specialist indications. Mannitol increases tubular osmotic load and is reserved for specific monitored settings. Neither is a routine replacement for loop diuretics in uncomplicated peripheral oedema. Consider contraindications, volume status and electrolyte effects.

7. AKI: recognition and initial management

Acute kidney injury is recognised through a rise in creatinine and/or reduced urine output, with interpretation using current KDIGO/NICE criteria. Look for prerenal hypoperfusion, intrinsic disease and postrenal obstruction. Treat sepsis, haemorrhage or dehydration promptly, review nephrotoxins, check urinalysis, monitor urine output and request imaging when obstruction is suspected. Diuretics are not routinely used to treat AKI itself, but may be used for fluid overload under appropriate supervision.

8. CKD: kidney protection and cardiovascular risk

Chronic kidney disease is classified using eGFR category and albuminuria, confirmed over time. Kidney-protective therapy may include BP control, ACE inhibitor or ARB for indicated albuminuric disease, and SGLT2 inhibitors for eligible patients according to current guidance. Check renal function and potassium after renin–angiotensin system treatment changes. A small creatinine rise can be expected; significant changes require review for volume depletion, renovascular disease and other causes.

9. Renal drug dosing: eGFR versus creatinine clearance

Many drug doses depend on kidney function, but the appropriate metric is drug-specific. Some medicines use Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance rather than laboratory eGFR, particularly certain anticoagulants and medicines with narrow therapeutic indices. In AKI, neither estimate is reliable because creatinine is not at steady state. Use current BNF, product information, specialist pharmacy advice, actual weight where appropriate, and clinical monitoring rather than a universal cut-off.

10. ACE inhibitors and ARBs in kidney disease

ACE inhibitors reduce angiotensin II formation and ARBs block AT1 receptors, lowering intraglomerular pressure and albuminuria in selected patients. They can cause hyperkalaemia and an initial fall in eGFR. Check BP, renal function and potassium before and after starting or increasing doses. Avoid combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB. Pregnancy is a contraindication. Assess for bilateral renal artery stenosis or severe volume depletion if renal function deteriorates markedly.

11. SGLT2 inhibitors and renal haemodynamics

SGLT2 inhibitors reduce proximal sodium–glucose reabsorption, increasing distal sodium delivery and restoring tubuloglomerular feedback. Benefits may include slowing CKD progression and reducing heart failure events in eligible groups, even without diabetes. A small initial eGFR dip can occur. Counsel on genital infection risk, dehydration, and temporary withholding during significant acute illness or prolonged fasting according to local sick-day guidance; consider euglycaemic ketoacidosis in symptomatic patients.

12. Hyperkalaemia: ECG, stabilisation and potassium removal

Hyperkalaemia can cause fatal arrhythmias. Repeat an unexpected result promptly if haemolysis is possible, but never delay emergency management when severe hyperkalaemia or ECG changes are present. Obtain ECG and urgent senior help. IV calcium stabilises myocardium when indicated but does not lower potassium. Insulin with glucose shifts potassium into cells; nebulised salbutamol may be an adjunct. Definitive removal involves renal excretion, binders in selected circumstances or dialysis. Monitor glucose and rebound potassium.

Emergency distinction

Calcium protects the myocardium; it does not reduce serum potassium. Insulin–glucose moves potassium into cells temporarily. Potassium must ultimately be removed and monitored for rebound. Follow the current local hyperkalaemia protocol.

13. Hypokalaemia: causes, risks and replacement

Causes include loop or thiazide diuretics, gastrointestinal loss, hyperaldosteronism and intracellular shifts. Symptoms include weakness, cramps and arrhythmias; check ECG when severe or symptomatic. Assess magnesium because deficiency can make potassium replacement ineffective. Oral potassium is preferred when safe and appropriate; IV replacement requires controlled infusion and monitoring, with rates and concentrations determined by local protocol. Identify and treat the underlying cause.

14. Hyponatraemia: avoid reflex treatment

First establish symptoms, acuity, serum osmolality, glucose, volume status and possible causes including diuretics, SIADH and adrenal insufficiency. Severe neurological symptoms require emergency specialist-led hypertonic saline according to protocol. Overly rapid correction can cause osmotic demyelination, particularly in chronic hyponatraemia and high-risk patients. Fluid restriction may be appropriate for some euvolaemic cases, but is not a universal treatment. Check serial sodium closely.

15. Hypernatraemia and water deficit

Hypernatraemia usually reflects a water deficit relative to sodium. Causes include impaired thirst, limited water access, gastrointestinal or renal water loss, and diabetes insipidus. Assess volume status, neurological features, urine concentration and ongoing losses. Restore circulation first if shocked, then replace free water carefully using a monitored plan. The correction rate depends on acuity and context; avoid unmonitored rapid shifts.

16. Calcium, phosphate and magnesium disorders

Loop diuretics increase urinary calcium loss, while thiazides decrease it. CKD can impair phosphate excretion and vitamin D activation, contributing to CKD-mineral bone disorder. Severe symptomatic hypocalcaemia may require IV calcium and ECG monitoring. Hypomagnesaemia can cause arrhythmias and refractory hypokalaemia. Magnesium replacement must account for kidney function to avoid accumulation.

17. Contrast, NSAIDs and other nephrotoxins

NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated afferent arteriolar dilation and can precipitate AKI during hypovolaemia or with other haemodynamic stressors. ACE inhibitors/ARBs affect efferent arteriolar tone; adding a diuretic and NSAID increases AKI risk. Aminoglycosides and some other drugs can cause direct tubular toxicity. Review medication combinations, indication, hydration, kidney function and alternatives rather than labelling every medicine permanently contraindicated.

18. Fluid overload and pulmonary oedema

Acute pulmonary oedema requires ABCDE assessment, oxygen if hypoxaemic, assessment for acute heart failure or other causes, and urgent escalation. IV loop diuretics are often used for congestion when clinically appropriate; consider blood pressure, renal function and prior diuretic exposure. Monitor response through symptoms, urine output, weight and biochemistry. In anuric severe renal failure or refractory overload, urgent dialysis assessment may be necessary.

19. Renal replacement therapy: emergency indications

Urgent dialysis may be needed for refractory hyperkalaemia, severe metabolic acidosis, pulmonary oedema resistant to medical treatment or symptomatic uraemic complications. The decision depends on clinical context rather than creatinine alone. Consult renal and critical care teams early. Medication dosing, anticoagulation and fluid prescriptions change during dialysis and require specialist guidance.

20. PLAB synthesis: safe prescribing and communication

For any renal prescription: identify the clinical goal; establish baseline renal function and trend; assess fluid status; check sodium and potassium; identify interactions; choose drug and dose using current renal guidance; plan monitoring and review; explain warning symptoms and sick-day advice. In OSCEs, communicate the reason for monitoring and what to do if vomiting, diarrhoea, dizziness, palpitations or reduced urine output develop.

Clinical case 1 · Diuretic-associated hyponatraemia

A 79-year-old starts indapamide and develops confusion and falls. Sodium is 119 mmol/L. Stop the suspected causative medicine, assess neurological severity and volume status, and obtain urgent senior evaluation. Severe symptoms may require monitored hypertonic saline. Avoid rapid unmonitored correction.

Clinical case 2 · Hyperkalaemia in CKD

A patient with CKD taking ramipril and spironolactone develops weakness and potassium 6.9 mmol/L with broad QRS complexes. Treat as an emergency: ECG monitoring, urgent senior help, IV calcium as indicated, temporising potassium shift, and definitive removal plan. Review medicines after stabilisation.

Clinical case 3 · Oedema and rising creatinine

A patient with heart failure has crackles, elevated JVP and leg oedema, with worsening creatinine. Do not assume all creatinine rises mean diuretics must be stopped: assess congestion, perfusion and response, involve senior clinicians and monitor electrolytes and renal function while treating decompensated heart failure.

Clinical case 4 · AKI after vomiting

A patient with diarrhoea continues ibuprofen, an ACE inhibitor and a diuretic. They are hypotensive and oliguric. Prioritise resuscitation, assess sepsis and obstruction, review potentially contributory medicines, monitor urine output and repeat blood tests. Explain the rationale for a personalised sick-day plan after recovery.

PLAB 2 communication station

Explain to a patient with CKD why a new diuretic requires blood tests. Cover symptom goals, daily weights if relevant, dizziness and dehydration warnings, potassium symptoms, when to seek urgent care, and who will review the results. Use teach-back and avoid telling patients to stop all medicines indiscriminately during illness.

    NICE / BNF · Renal prescribing pathways

    Educational decision support only; follow current local emergency protocols and BNF for actual prescriptions.

    Starting or changing a diuretic

    Confirm indication → assess fluid status and BP → baseline creatinine, Na, K and sometimes Mg → review interacting medicines → choose class/dose → agree symptoms, weight and lab follow-up → adjust for clinical response and adverse effects.

    Severe hyperkalaemia

    Urgent ECG and senior help → cardiac monitoring → IV calcium if indicated → insulin–glucose and other temporising measures per protocol → potassium removal strategy, including dialysis if needed → repeat potassium and glucose, monitor rebound.

    AKI assessment

    ABCDE and volume status → compare creatinine trend/urine output → treat sepsis, shock or obstruction → review NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs, diuretics and renally cleared medicines individually → repeat tests and escalate if refractory complications.

    CKD prescription review

    Check eGFR and albuminuria → assess indication for renal-protective medicines → use drug-specific CrCl/eGFR dosing instructions → check potassium and BP → counsel sick-day precautions → plan monitoring and specialist referral when indicated.

    UK clinical references

    BNF · NICE NG203: CKD · NICE NG148: AKI · NICE NG100: IV fluids · UK Kidney Association guidelines. Check current versions and local protocols.
