Urological Emergencies: Urinary Retention, Renal Colic & Obstructive Uropathy

Surgery · Lesson 30 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Urological emergencies: a safe PLAB approach

Emergency rule: an obstructed urinary tract with infection (fever, rigors, sepsis), anuria, solitary kidney obstruction or acute kidney injury requires urgent urology assessment. Antibiotics alone do not adequately treat an infected obstructed collecting system: urgent drainage is usually required.

1 · First assessment

Use ABCDE for an unwell patient. Ask about pain onset/location/radiation, inability to pass urine, haematuria, dysuria, fever, vomiting, previous stones, prostate symptoms, medications, pregnancy possibility, and renal history. Record observations, fluid status, abdominal examination (palpable bladder), renal angle tenderness and genital examination when indicated. Check for neurological red flags such as saddle anaesthesia, new leg weakness and bowel dysfunction suggesting cauda equina syndrome.

2 · Acute urinary retention (AUR)

Typically sudden inability to void with painful suprapubic distension. Causes include benign prostatic enlargement, urethral stricture, constipation, infection/prostatitis, anticholinergic or opioid drugs, postoperative retention, and neurological pathology. Assess bladder volume by scan when available, examine and check urinalysis, renal function and electrolytes. Prompt urethral catheterisation is first-line when appropriate, with aseptic technique, urine output recording and monitoring for haematuria, hypotension or post-obstructive diuresis. Seek experienced help for difficult catheterisation; do not repeatedly force a catheter. Suspected urethral injury (e.g. blood at meatus after pelvic trauma) needs urology/trauma advice before urethral instrumentation.

3 · Retention: next steps

Investigate reversible causes, treat constipation/infection as appropriate, review culprit drugs and consider an alpha-blocker such as tamsulosin in men with likely prostatic obstruction before a trial without catheter, following local practice. Arrange a safe trial without catheter and follow-up; renal impairment, recurrent retention, haematuria or suspected malignancy may require urgent specialist review. Chronic retention may be painless and present with overflow incontinence or renal impairment.

4 · Renal and ureteric colic

Severe intermittent flank pain radiating to groin, often with restlessness, nausea/vomiting and microscopic or visible haematuria. Absence of haematuria does not exclude stones. Consider differentials: abdominal aortic aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, pyelonephritis, appendicitis and testicular torsion. Urinalysis and urine culture if infection suspected, FBC, U&E/eGFR, pregnancy test when appropriate and imaging are important.

5 · Imaging and analgesia

In adults with suspected renal colic, NICE NG118 recommends urgent low-dose non-contrast CT within 24 hours; pregnant patients and children should generally have ultrasound first. Offer an NSAID by any route as first-line analgesia if not contraindicated; IV paracetamol if NSAIDs are contraindicated or inadequate; opioids if both are contraindicated or insufficient. Do not use antispasmodics routinely. Tailor analgesia to renal function, dehydration, bleeding risk and pregnancy.

6 · Stones: management

Small uncomplicated stones may pass spontaneously with analgesia, hydration to normal requirements, safety-netting and follow-up. NICE advises considering an alpha-blocker for distal ureteric stones less than 10 mm, recognising patient-specific contraindications and local policy. Persistent uncontrolled pain, persistent vomiting, significant obstruction, infection or renal impairment require urology review. Stone size and location guide ureteroscopy, shockwave lithotripsy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy; stone analysis and prevention are part of follow-up.

7 · Infected obstructed kidney / pyonephrosis

Fever, tachycardia, hypotension, raised inflammatory markers or sepsis in a patient with ureteric obstruction is a time-critical emergency. Obtain cultures where feasible without delaying therapy, give prompt IV antibiotics according to local sepsis guidance, IV fluids according to haemodynamics and urgent urology referral for decompression by ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy. Definitive stone treatment is generally deferred until infection is controlled.

8 · Obstructive uropathy and post-renal AKI

Obstruction may be lower tract (prostate, urethral stricture, clot retention) or upper tract (stones, malignancy, retroperitoneal disease). Check creatinine, potassium, urine output and ultrasound when appropriate for hydronephrosis. Bladder outlet obstruction may respond to catheterisation; upper tract obstruction may require stent or nephrostomy. Bilateral obstruction or obstruction of a solitary functioning kidney can rapidly cause anuria and life-threatening hyperkalaemia.

9 · Post-obstructive diuresis

After relief of prolonged obstruction, brisk urine output can cause hypovolaemia and sodium/potassium abnormalities. Measure hourly urine output where indicated, monitor blood pressure, weight and electrolytes, and replace fluid thoughtfully according to specialist/local protocols rather than automatically matching all output.

10 · Haematuria and clot retention

Visible haematuria can arise from infection, stones, malignancy or trauma. Assess haemodynamic stability, blood count, coagulation and medication history. Painful retention with clots may require specialist placement of a large-bore three-way catheter, bladder washout and irrigation. Do not assume haematuria is due to anticoagulants alone; arrange appropriate cancer assessment according to NICE referral guidance.

11 · Additional urological emergencies

Testicular torsion: sudden unilateral testicular pain, high-riding testis or absent cremasteric reflex; immediate surgical assessment, do not delay for ultrasound when suspicion is high. Fournier gangrene: rapidly progressive perineal pain, swelling or systemic toxicity; immediate resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics and surgical debridement. Acute bacterial prostatitis: fever, perineal pain and urinary symptoms; avoid vigorous prostatic massage and manage sepsis promptly.

12 · Discharge and prevention

Safety-net for fever, rigors, inability to void, anuria, worsening pain, persistent vomiting or haematuria. Encourage adequate usual hydration for stone prevention (not forced fluids during acute colic), reduce excess salt, discuss stone composition and metabolic risk, and arrange urology follow-up when indicated.

PLAB decision pathways

Sudden inability to void

ABCDE if unwell → history and examination → bladder scan → prompt appropriate catheterisation → record residual and output → U&E, urinalysis and cause assessment → follow-up / trial without catheter; escalate difficult catheter or suspected urethral trauma.

Flank pain suggestive of stone

Observations and sepsis screen → pregnancy considerations, urinalysis, U&E → NSAID first line if safe → low-dose non-contrast CT in adults within 24 h (ultrasound first in pregnancy/children) → assess stone size, obstruction and complications.

Obstruction plus infection = emergency

ABCDE + cultures if no delay → IV antibiotics and resuscitation → immediate urology discussion → urgent drainage (stent or nephrostomy) → delay definitive stone procedure until sepsis controlled.

Anuria / post-renal AKI

Check bladder, renal function and potassium → assess bilateral/solitary-kidney obstruction → catheterise lower tract obstruction; urgent upper tract drainage when indicated → monitor for post-obstructive diuresis.

Red flags

Sepsis, fever with obstructing stone, anuria, solitary kidney, uncontrolled pain/vomiting, rising creatinine, hyperkalaemia, haemodynamic compromise, suspected cauda equina or urethral injury.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Painful retention

A 74-year-old man has not passed urine for 12 hours and has a tender suprapubic mass. Answer: assess observations, scan bladder, promptly catheterise when safe, document drainage and monitor urine output; investigate prostatic obstruction and arrange trial without catheter.

Case 2 — Uncomplicated ureteric stone

A 32-year-old has colicky loin-to-groin pain, no fever, normal renal function and a small distal stone on CT. Answer: NSAID if safe, antiemetic, consider alpha-blocker for distal stone <10 mm, provide safety-net and follow-up.

Case 3 — Septic obstructed kidney

A 60-year-old with a ureteric stone is febrile, hypotensive and confused. Answer: sepsis management, prompt IV antibiotics and fluids as indicated, immediate urology call for urgent stent or nephrostomy; antibiotics alone are inadequate.

Case 4 — Anuria and bilateral hydronephrosis

A 68-year-old with pelvic malignancy has no urine output, rising creatinine and potassium 6.5 mmol/L. Answer: ECG and emergency hyperkalaemia management, urgent urology and renal assessment for decompression, close fluid/electrolyte monitoring.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Acute urinary retention

Station: A 71-year-old man presents with severe lower abdominal discomfort and inability to pass urine since last night.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and assess pain and immediate stability.
  2. Ask about urinary stream, hesitancy, nocturia, dysuria, haematuria, fever, constipation, medications and previous episodes.
  3. Ask neurological red flags: saddle numbness, new weakness and bowel changes.
  4. Explain examination and bladder scan, obtain consent and preserve privacy.
  5. Explain that urgent bladder drainage with a catheter usually relieves pain; discuss discomfort, infection and bleeding risks.
  6. Escalate if catheter cannot pass, urethral trauma suspected or patient is septic.
  7. Discuss blood/urine tests, possible prostate-related cause, medication review, trial without catheter and follow-up.
  8. Safety-net for fever, blocked catheter, worsening pain, blood clots or reduced urine output; check understanding.

Examiner focus: empathy, prompt decompression, recognition of red flags, consent, explanation and safe follow-up.

Rapid review — 12 essentials

  1. Acute urinary retention is usually painful with a palpable bladder.
  2. Catheterisation is first-line for suitable acute retention; never force a difficult catheter.
  3. Suspected urethral injury requires specialist assessment before urethral catheterisation.
  4. Check neurological red flags in new urinary retention.
  5. Adult renal colic: urgent low-dose non-contrast CT within 24 hours (NICE NG118).
  6. Use ultrasound first in pregnancy and children with suspected stones.
  7. NSAIDs are first-line renal colic analgesia if safe.
  8. Consider alpha-blockers for distal ureteric stones under 10 mm.
  9. Fever/sepsis plus urinary obstruction needs urgent drainage and antibiotics.
  10. Anuria, bilateral obstruction or solitary-kidney obstruction threatens renal function.
  11. Monitor for post-obstructive diuresis after decompression.
  12. Testicular torsion needs immediate surgical assessment.
