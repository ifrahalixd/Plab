Eczema & Atopic Dermatitis

PLAB priority: Treat eczema with consistent emollients and appropriate topical anti-inflammatory treatment; recognise eczema herpeticum as an emergency.

01. Definition and epidemiology

Atopic eczema is a chronic, relapsing, itchy inflammatory skin disease associated with epidermal barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation. It often begins in childhood but can persist or start in adulthood.

PLAB application: Ask about itch, sleep, school/work, quality of life and previous treatments.

02. Typical clinical presentation

Intense pruritus, dry skin, poorly demarcated erythema, excoriations and lichenification are typical. Acute flares may ooze or crust. Erythema may appear less obvious on darker skin; look for papules, altered pigmentation and texture.

PLAB application: Itch is a hallmark; absence of itch should prompt consideration of another diagnosis.

03. Distribution by age

Infants often have face, scalp and extensor involvement; older children and adults commonly have flexural eczema, hands and neck. Distribution varies with ethnicity, occupation and exposures.

PLAB application: Describe location and morphology rather than relying on one stereotypical pattern.

04. Pathophysiology

Impaired skin barrier increases water loss and irritant penetration; immune inflammation perpetuates itch and scratching. Filaggrin-related barrier defects contribute in some patients.

PLAB application: Explain the itch–scratch cycle in accessible terms.

05. History and assessment

Explore onset, flare triggers, family or personal atopy, sleep, infections, contact exposures, occupational wet work, topical treatments and adherence. Assess extent, severity and psychosocial impact.

PLAB application: Ask specifically about treatment quantity and whether steroid fears have reduced use.

06. Differential diagnoses

Consider contact dermatitis, psoriasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, scabies, tinea, ichthyosis and immunodeficiency in atypical or severe cases. Scabies commonly causes nocturnal itch and affects close contacts.

PLAB application: Unilateral annular scaly lesions suggest fungal infection; consider skin scrapings.

07. Emollients and everyday care

Use liberal, frequent leave-on emollients as the foundation of treatment, including when eczema appears clear. Offer suitable soap substitutes, avoid fragranced irritants and choose products acceptable to the patient.

PLAB application: Explain regular application and fire safety: emollient residue on fabrics can ignite.

08. Topical corticosteroids

Choose potency according to severity and site; mild preparations for face and sensitive areas, stronger preparations for suitable short courses on thicker skin when indicated. Apply to active inflamed eczema and review response.

PLAB application: Demonstrate fingertip units and safe, effective use; avoid prolonged unsupervised potent steroid use.

09. Calcineurin inhibitors and escalation

Tacrolimus or pimecrolimus may be used in selected patients, particularly sensitive sites, under relevant guidance. Persistent moderate-to-severe disease may require dermatology referral for phototherapy or systemic therapies including biologics/JAK inhibitors.

PLAB application: Treatment choice depends on age, severity, licensing, safety monitoring and specialist advice.

10. Infected eczema

Suspect bacterial infection with rapidly worsening eczema, weeping, pustules, crusts, fever or malaise, but colonisation alone is common. Not every flare requires antibiotics; manage inflammation and assess for systemic illness.

PLAB application: Follow NICE NG190; antibiotics are not routinely needed in patients who are not systemically unwell.

11. Eczema herpeticum

This is a potentially serious disseminated HSV infection: painful, rapidly spreading monomorphic vesicles or punched-out erosions, often with fever. Eye involvement threatens vision.

PLAB application: Arrange same-day emergency specialist assessment and systemic aciclovir; urgent ophthalmology if periocular.

12. Contact dermatitis and patch testing

Irritant contact dermatitis results from cumulative damage (e.g., detergents, wet work); allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction. Patch testing helps identify suspected allergic triggers.

PLAB application: Persistent hand or treatment-resistant eczema merits occupational and contact allergy history.

13. Children and safeguarding

Assess growth, sleep, family burden and treatment technique. Consider food allergy assessment only when clinically indicated, such as immediate reactions or difficult-to-control eczema in young children; do not recommend broad exclusion diets routinely.

PLAB application: Discuss treatment with caregivers and avoid unnecessary restrictive diets.

14. Complications and comorbidity

Secondary infection, post-inflammatory pigment change, sleep disturbance, anxiety and reduced quality of life are common. Atopic eczema may coexist with asthma and allergic rhinitis.

PLAB application: Ask about mental wellbeing, bullying and sleep disruption.

15. PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that eczema is not contagious and usually fluctuates. Show emollient and topical steroid technique, set expectations for flare treatment and give written safety-net advice.

PLAB application: Use teach-back and agree a practical regimen that fits the patient.

16. Red flags and referral

Urgently assess suspected eczema herpeticum, severe systemic infection or rapidly worsening skin with systemic symptoms. Refer for diagnostic uncertainty, frequent severe flares, significant impact or failure of appropriate treatment.

PLAB application: Painful punched-out lesions plus fever = emergency, not routine eczema review.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Barrier dysfunction and filaggrin

Epidermal barrier defects increase transepidermal water loss and allergen penetration. Not all patients have a filaggrin mutation; eczema reflects interacting genetic, immune and environmental factors.

The itch–scratch cycle

Inflammation triggers itch, scratching damages the barrier and further inflammation develops. Emollients and anti-inflammatory therapy break this cycle.

Topical steroid pharmacology

Potency is distinct from the amount applied. Appropriate potency and duration matter, especially on face, flexures and in children. Undertreatment can prolong severe inflammation.

Infection versus colonisation

Staphylococcus aureus often colonises eczema; crusting alone does not prove antibiotic-responsive infection. Assess systemic illness, worsening severity and alternative causes.

HSV dissemination

Eczema herpeticum can resemble infected eczema but typically causes painful, uniform vesicles or erosions and requires urgent systemic antivirals.

Beyond topical treatment

Moderate-to-severe refractory disease may benefit from phototherapy, biologics or other systemic medicines under specialist supervision with safety monitoring.
