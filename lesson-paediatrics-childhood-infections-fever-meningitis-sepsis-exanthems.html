Childhood Infections: Fever, Meningitis, Sepsis & Exanthems

Emergency red flags: Infant under 3 months with fever ≥38°C, non-blanching rash, reduced consciousness, meningism, shock, seizures or respiratory compromise require urgent assessment.

Fever in children: first approach

Fever is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure and hydration; record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, capillary refill and oxygen saturation. Ask about duration, contacts, immunisation, travel, medicines and focal symptoms.

PLAB application: A child who looks toxic needs immediate assessment regardless of measured temperature.

NICE fever traffic-light system

NICE NG143 categorises features into green, amber and red risk groups for serious illness in under-5s. Consider colour, activity, breathing, circulation, hydration and other features together; safety-net when no clear diagnosis.

PLAB application: A non-blanching rash, reduced responsiveness or grunting is concerning.

Age-specific fever thresholds

Temperature 38°C or higher in an infant under 3 months is a high-risk feature; 39°C or higher in a 3–6-month-old is at least intermediate risk under NICE fever guidance. In very young infants consider serious bacterial infection.

PLAB application: Do not dismiss a young febrile infant because they appear relatively well.

Fever management and antipyretics

Treat the child rather than the number. Offer fluids, monitor urine output and provide clear safety-netting. Consider paracetamol or ibuprofen when distressed; do not use solely to lower temperature, do not give both simultaneously, and do not use response to antipyretics to distinguish serious from minor illness.

PLAB application: Avoid tepid sponging; check age, hydration and contraindications before ibuprofen.

Meningitis: presentation

Bacterial meningitis may cause fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting and altered mental state; infants may have poor feeding, irritability, abnormal cry or a bulging fontanelle. Classical signs may be absent.

PLAB application: Suspected bacterial meningitis is an emergency; do not wait for the classic triad.

Meningococcal disease

Meningococcal infection can rapidly cause sepsis, meningitis and purpura. A non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash is important, but a rash may be absent early. Limb pain, cold extremities and deteriorating perfusion can precede late shock signs.

PLAB application: Immediate emergency transfer and antibiotics under local protocol; do not delay for a rash.

Sepsis recognition

Suspect sepsis with suspected infection and abnormal behaviour, perfusion, breathing, temperature, heart rate or urine output. Fever may be absent; hypothermia is possible. Use age-appropriate NICE sepsis risk criteria and clinical judgement.

PLAB application: Escalate promptly; normal blood pressure does not exclude compensated shock.

Sepsis initial management

Call senior paediatric help, undertake ABCDE, provide oxygen if indicated, obtain IV/IO access, assess glucose and lactate when appropriate, take blood cultures if feasible without delaying antibiotics, and give timely IV antimicrobials according to local pathways. Reassess after fluids, considering risk of overload.

PLAB application: Time-critical treatment and frequent reassessment are more important than completing every test first.

Investigating meningitis

Blood cultures and appropriate blood tests support diagnosis. Lumbar puncture may be indicated once stable and safe; defer when shock, compromised airway, raised intracranial pressure concerns, uncontrolled seizures or other contraindications exist. Never delay urgent antibiotics for LP.

PLAB application: Consider neuroimaging only when clinically indicated; it is not a routine prerequisite for LP.

Measles

Measles usually causes fever, cough, coryza and conjunctivitis followed by a spreading maculopapular rash; Koplik spots may appear. It is highly infectious and can cause pneumonia or encephalitis. Follow isolation, notification and public-health advice.

PLAB application: Ask vaccination and exposure history; telephone ahead before attendance to reduce transmission.

Chickenpox and complications

Varicella causes itchy vesicles in different stages of development. Most healthy children need supportive care; complications include bacterial skin infection, pneumonia and neurological disease. Vulnerable contacts, newborns, pregnant people and immunocompromised patients require specialist advice.

PLAB application: Watch for rapidly worsening skin redness, respiratory distress or altered consciousness.

Scarlet fever

Group A streptococcal infection may cause fever, sore throat, sandpaper-like rash, strawberry tongue and circumoral pallor. Assess for complications and treat according to current UK antimicrobial guidance; advise school exclusion according to public-health recommendations.

PLAB application: Recognise scarlet fever but differentiate from invasive GAS and sepsis.

Roseola and slapped-cheek disease

Roseola (HHV-6/7) often features several days of high fever followed by rash as fever resolves. Parvovirus B19 causes slapped-cheek erythema and a lacy rash; exposure matters in pregnancy and haematological disease.

PLAB application: Rash timing and associated symptoms often distinguish viral exanthems.

Kawasaki disease and MIS-C/PIMS-TS

Consider Kawasaki disease in persistent fever (typically 5 days or more) with conjunctival injection, oral changes, rash, limb changes or cervical lymphadenopathy; incomplete cases occur. PIMS-TS/MIS-C may follow SARS-CoV-2 and present with fever, inflammation, shock and gastrointestinal symptoms.

PLAB application: Persistent unexplained fever with mucocutaneous changes warrants urgent paediatric assessment.

Febrile seizures

A febrile seizure occurs with fever in a young child without CNS infection or another acute cause. During a seizure protect from injury, time it, assess ABC and give emergency treatment for prolonged seizure under local guidance. Evaluate for meningitis or sepsis where indicated.

PLAB application: A first seizure or prolonged, focal or recurrent seizure requires appropriate clinical evaluation.

PLAB 2: parent communication and safety-netting

Explain findings without premature reassurance, acknowledge concern, describe why tests or admission are needed, and discuss infection control. Give explicit return precautions: difficulty breathing, reduced responsiveness, non-blanching rash, seizures, dehydration, or deterioration. Check understanding and arrange review.

PLAB application: Use teach-back and document the agreed plan and safety-net advice.

PLAB 2 — Febrile child consultation

Introduce yourself, assess immediate danger, take a focused fever and exposure history, check immunisations, explain the examination and working diagnosis, obtain consent for tests or admission, and give specific safety-net advice. In a deteriorating child, prioritise urgent help over prolonged counselling.

Deep Dive

Recognising serious bacterial illness

Combine age, overall appearance, perfusion, respiratory effort and mental state. A normal temperature after antipyretics does not rule out serious infection. NICE NG143 is designed for children under 5; use age-appropriate guidance for older children.

Meningitis versus meningococcal sepsis

They can coexist. Meningitis may feature headache, photophobia or neck stiffness; meningococcal sepsis may initially feature limb pain, cold hands and feet, poor perfusion and rapid deterioration. Neither requires a rash to be present.

Antibiotics and lumbar puncture

Obtain cultures before antibiotics if this does not delay treatment. A lumbar puncture is not appropriate in an unstable child. Seek senior support and follow local paediatric antimicrobial guidance; specific antibiotic selection depends on age and clinical context.

Exanthem differentiation

Measles: cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, then rash. Scarlet fever: sore throat and sandpaper rash. Varicella: vesicles in different stages. Roseola: rash after fever subsides. Parvovirus B19: slapped cheeks and lacy rash. Kawasaki disease: persistent fever and mucocutaneous inflammation.

Infection control and notification

Suspected measles and invasive meningococcal disease require prompt infection-control and public-health action. Notify the relevant team and follow UK guidance for isolation, contact tracing and prophylaxis where indicated.

Exam pitfalls

Do not use rash absence to exclude meningococcal disease, antipyretic response to exclude sepsis, or normal blood pressure to exclude shock. Do not postpone antibiotics to perform lumbar puncture in an unstable child.
