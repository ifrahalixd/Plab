Medical Records, Documentation & Data Protection

PLAB core: Good clinical records support continuity, patient safety, accountability and patient trust. Record relevant decisions and information accurately, promptly, clearly and securely.

1 · What belongs in the clinical record?

Record the history, examination, relevant findings, working diagnosis, investigations, treatment, medications, allergies, safety-netting, referrals and follow-up. Include the patient's concerns, information given, options and material risks discussed, consent or refusal, and the rationale for significant decisions. Attribute entries to the author and record when they were made.

2 · Contemporaneous, objective and respectful

Write as soon as practicable, distinguish fact from interpretation, and use professional, non-stigmatising language. Document relevant negative findings and escalation. Do not include speculative personal remarks.

3 · Correcting mistakes

Never secretly erase or backdate an entry. Correct the record transparently so the original entry and audit trail remain accessible; explain the correction and date it. For electronic records, follow the organisation's amendment procedure.

4 · Patient access

Patients can usually request copies of their personal health information via a subject access request (SAR). Identity must be verified as appropriate; requests are handled under UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018. The usual response period is one month, with lawful extensions in qualifying complex cases. Access may be restricted where a legal exemption applies, including serious harm and third-party information in specified circumstances. Do not promise unrestricted access to every entry.

5 · Confidentiality and lawful processing

UK GDPR provides a legal framework for processing personal data; a lawful basis and, for health data, an Article 9 condition are required. This is not the same as obtaining clinical consent to treatment or meeting the separate professional duty of confidentiality. Direct-care sharing should be relevant, necessary and proportionate, with appropriate safeguards.

6 · Data minimisation and security

Use approved systems, strong authentication and role-based access; lock screens and verify recipients. Avoid personal messaging apps, unsecured devices and accessing records without a legitimate work reason. Share the minimum necessary information.

7 · Breaches and openness

If information is sent to the wrong recipient, act promptly to contain the breach, report to the information governance/data protection team, document facts and follow local policy. The organisation assesses whether notification to the ICO is required (normally within 72 hours of awareness for reportable personal data breaches) and whether affected individuals must be informed. Do not conceal the incident.

UK legal scope: UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018 are UK-wide, but NHS records policies and some access-related procedures vary by nation. The GMC documents provided support professional record-keeping and confidentiality principles; statutory time limits and exemptions come from additional UK data-protection law.

Clinical decision pathways

A · Patient asks for their records

  1. Listen and clarify the scope of the request; explain the SAR route.
  2. Verify identity and any representative's authority.
  3. Pass the request promptly to the responsible records/information governance team.
  4. Review relevant exemptions and third-party information with appropriate expertise; do not alter records.
  5. Provide a timely response through a secure method and explain any lawful withholding.

B · You notice a documentation error

  1. Check the clinical facts and any immediate patient-safety implications.
  2. Correct using a dated, attributable amendment that preserves the original entry/audit trail.
  3. Inform the team and patient where clinically relevant; escalate safety incidents.

C · Wrong-patient email

  1. Contain: notify recipient and ask them not to use/share it; seek secure deletion.
  2. Report immediately to information governance and senior staff.
  3. Record what was disclosed, to whom, when and what mitigation occurred.
  4. Follow the organisation's risk assessment and notification process.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Incorrect medication entry

A colleague documents 50 mg rather than 5 mg. Best response: assess whether the patient has received an incorrect dose; correct the record transparently, preserve the audit trail, and communicate any safety concern.

Case 2 · Patient requests notes

A patient asks for their consultation notes because they disagree with the diagnosis. Best response: explain their access rights and the SAR process, facilitate prompt handling, and address concerns without changing the clinical account to avoid scrutiny.

Case 3 · Friend's results

A doctor accesses a friend's laboratory results out of curiosity. Best response: this is inappropriate access even without onward disclosure; stop, follow local incident procedures and reflect on professional duties.

Case 4 · Email sent to wrong address

A referral letter containing identifiable details is misdirected. Best response: contain, report and document the breach immediately; the organisation assesses notification obligations.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Patient challenges their records

Station: A patient says an inaccurate consultation note has damaged their care and wants it deleted.

Candidate checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, verify identity and ensure privacy.
  2. Listen without defensiveness; acknowledge the concern and impact.
  3. Clarify the exact entry and what the patient believes is incorrect.
  4. Explain that factual inaccuracies can be corrected transparently; clinical opinions may require an explanatory statement rather than deletion.
  5. Do not promise to erase the original or alter a record retrospectively without an audit trail.
  6. Explain how to request records or raise a complaint; involve records/information governance team.
  7. Check whether an error has caused clinical harm and arrange review if needed.
  8. Summarise agreed actions, time frame and follow-up; document the conversation.

Useful phrase: “I can see why this is concerning. We'll review the entry and correct any factual errors using the proper process, while keeping an accurate record of what was originally documented.”

Common exam traps

  • A patient requesting records is not automatically entitled to every third-party detail.
  • “Patient consent” is not always the lawful basis for routine healthcare data processing.
  • A doctor's personal curiosity is not a legitimate reason to open a record.
  • Do not promise the patient a complete erasure of an accurate clinical record.
  • The 72-hour ICO clock applies to reportable personal data breaches, not every minor error.
