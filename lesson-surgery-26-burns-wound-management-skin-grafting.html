Burns, wounds and reconstructive principles

Emergency priorities: major burns, inhalation injury, circumferential burns compromising circulation or ventilation, chemical/electrical injury and suspected non-accidental injury need prompt senior/burns-service input. Assess ABCDE first.

1 · Initial first aid

Stop the burning process and remove from the source without endangering rescuers. Cool thermal burns with cool running tap water for 20 minutes, ideally immediately and still beneficial up to 3 hours after injury; avoid ice and prevent hypothermia, especially in children or extensive burns. Remove jewellery and loose clothing, but do not pull off adherent material. Cover with clean non-adherent material or cling film laid lengthwise (not wrapped tightly around a limb); do not use creams or butter. Give analgesia and assess tetanus status.

2 · Primary survey and inhalation injury

Use ABCDE, protect cervical spine if trauma. Facial burns, soot, singed nasal hair, hoarseness, stridor, enclosed-space fire or altered consciousness raise concern for inhalation injury. Give high-flow oxygen when smoke inhalation or carbon monoxide exposure is suspected; pulse oximetry may be falsely reassuring in carbon monoxide poisoning. Early anaesthetic/critical care input for threatened airway; do not wait for stridor before escalation. Consider cyanide toxicity in severe enclosed-space smoke exposure with cardiovascular collapse or marked lactic acidosis and follow specialist toxicology advice.

3 · Depth classification

Superficial epidermal: red, painful, dry, blanching, no blisters (e.g. sunburn), usually heals without scarring. Superficial dermal: moist, pink, blistered, very painful, brisk capillary refill; usually heals within approximately two weeks. Deep dermal: paler/mottled, reduced sensation and slower capillary refill; often requires specialist evaluation and may scar. Full thickness: dry/leathery, white/brown/black, insensate centrally; needs burns assessment and often excision/grafting. Mixed-depth burns are common.

4 · Estimating burn size

Estimate total body surface area (TBSA) for partial-thickness and full-thickness burns only; exclude simple erythema. Adult rule of nines: head/neck 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, perineum 1%. Use Lund–Browder chart for children because proportions differ. Patient's palm including fingers is approximately 1% TBSA for scattered burns, though this is approximate.

5 · Fluid resuscitation

Major burns cause capillary leak and hypovolaemia. Obtain IV access, blood tests, monitor urine output and follow local major-burns protocol. A common starting estimate for adult major thermal burns is the Parkland formula: 4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA of crystalloid in the first 24 hours from time of injury, half during the first 8 hours and remainder over the next 16 hours. This is an initial estimate, not a fixed prescription; modern protocols may use lower starting volumes, and all fluids are titrated to perfusion, urine output and specialist advice. Avoid fluid overload. Children also need age-appropriate maintenance fluids with glucose where indicated.

6 · Referral and admission

Discuss with regional burns service for deep dermal/full-thickness burns, burns of face/hands/feet/genitals/perineum/major joints, significant TBSA, circumferential burns, chemical or electrical injury, suspected inhalation injury, children with concerning burns, and patients with major comorbidities or safeguarding concerns. Thresholds vary by UK network; use current local referral criteria. Do not delay emergency stabilisation while arranging transfer.

7 · Wound assessment and dressings

Document site, size, depth, contamination, neurovascular status and photographs with consent/local policy. Clean gently, remove loose debris, assess blisters using local burns protocol and apply suitable non-adherent dressings. Avoid routine prophylactic systemic antibiotics for uncomplicated clean burns; give antibiotics when infection is clinically suspected. Review for spreading erythema, increasing pain, purulence, fever or systemic deterioration. Maintain nutrition, pain control, mobilisation and scar prevention.

8 · Special injuries

Chemical: use PPE, remove contaminated clothing, brush off dry powders before copious irrigation where appropriate; identify chemical and seek toxicology advice. Electrical: consider deep tissue injury, arrhythmia, rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome even with small skin marks; ECG and monitoring according to exposure and clinical status. Circumferential: monitor distal perfusion and respiratory excursion; urgent burns-surgeon assessment for escharotomy if compromised.

9 · Skin grafts and reconstruction

Split-thickness skin graft: epidermis and part of dermis; covers large defects, donor site heals by re-epithelialisation, more secondary contraction. Full-thickness graft: epidermis and full dermis; better colour/texture and less contraction but requires suitable vascular bed and smaller donor site. Grafts need a clean, well-vascularised recipient bed; infection, haematoma, shear and poor perfusion cause failure. Flaps bring their own blood supply and may cover exposed bone/tendon where grafts cannot reliably take.

10 · Chronic wounds and healing

Healing proceeds through haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Delays arise from infection, ischaemia, diabetes, malnutrition, smoking, pressure and immunosuppression. Assess venous ulcers, arterial ulcers and pressure injuries by cause; use compression for venous disease only after suitable vascular assessment. Surgical wounds may heal by primary intention, secondary intention or delayed primary closure. Provide safety-net advice and appropriate follow-up.

Clinical algorithms and quick comparisons

Burns emergency algorithm

Scene safety → stop burn → cool running water 20 min while preventing hypothermia → ABCDE and airway risk → analgesia, remove jewellery, cover → assess depth/TBSA → fluids if major burn → burns service referral.

Depth versus healing

Superficial epidermal: red, dry, no blister. Superficial dermal: pink, wet, blistered, painful. Deep dermal: mottled/pale, reduced sensation. Full thickness: leathery/insensate. Depth can evolve; reassess.

Rule of nines (adult)

Head 9%; each arm 9%; each leg 18%; front torso 18%; back torso 18%; perineum 1%. Exclude erythema-only burns from TBSA.

Fluid formula example

70 kg × 20% TBSA × 4 mL = 5600 mL estimated first 24 hours; 2800 mL in first 8 hours from injury, remainder over 16 hours. This is a teaching example; individualise and titrate under a major-burns protocol.

Inhalation injury

Enclosed-space smoke + hoarse voice/soot/facial burns → oxygen, urgent senior airway review; normal pulse oximetry does not exclude CO exposure.

Skin coverage choice

Clean vascular bed and large defect → split-thickness graft often appropriate; smaller cosmetic site → full-thickness graft in selected cases; exposed poorly vascularised structures → consider flap.

Wound infection

New spreading erythema, purulence, fever or deterioration → examine wound, consider cultures if indicated, assess sepsis, treat based on severity and local antimicrobial guidance.

Four applied clinical cases

Case 1 — Hot tea scald

A toddler has a blistered forearm after a scald 15 minutes ago. Decision: cool under running tap water for 20 minutes, prevent hypothermia, give analgesia, assess extent/depth and safeguarding context; seek burns advice using paediatric criteria.

Case 2 — House fire

A 45-year-old rescued from an enclosed-space fire is hoarse with soot around the mouth; pulse oximeter reads 99%. Decision: suspect inhalation/CO injury despite normal SpO₂; high-flow oxygen, ABCDE, urgent anaesthetic and burns review.

Case 3 — Circumferential burn

A patient has a deep circumferential forearm burn, increasing pain and reduced distal perfusion. Decision: urgent burns-surgeon assessment for vascular compromise and possible escharotomy; monitor neurovascular findings.

Case 4 — Failed graft

A split-thickness graft becomes dusky and lifts over a collection after surgery. Decision: suspect haematoma/seroma, infection or shear compromising graft take; prompt surgical review and wound assessment.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Scald burn assessment and counselling

Station: A 30-year-old spilled boiling water over the forearm 30 minutes ago and asks whether they need hospital treatment.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm timing, mechanism, first aid and tetanus status; assess pain and other injuries.
  2. Ask about airway symptoms, chemical/electrical exposure, comorbidities and safeguarding when appropriate.
  3. Explain cooling with cool running water for 20 minutes, removal of jewellery and preventing hypothermia; advise against ice, butter and home remedies.
  4. Assess burn site, blistering, sensation, capillary refill, depth and estimated TBSA; check distal neurovascular status.
  5. Offer analgesia and a clean non-adherent covering; explain specialist referral for deep, extensive, circumferential or high-risk-site burns.
  6. Discuss wound care, dressing review, infection signs, healing/scarring and follow-up.
  7. Safety-net urgently for increasing pain, spreading redness, fever, numbness, reduced finger circulation or any breathing problem.
  8. Check understanding, invite questions and document findings and plan.

Rapid revision — 12 essentials

  1. Stop the burn and cool thermal burns with running tap water for 20 minutes.
  2. Prevent hypothermia; never apply ice directly.
  3. Assess ABCDE and identify inhalation injury early.
  4. Normal pulse oximetry cannot exclude carbon monoxide poisoning.
  5. Superficial epidermal burns do not count towards resuscitation TBSA.
  6. Adult rule of nines: each arm 9%, each leg 18%, front and back trunk 18% each.
  7. Use Lund–Browder for more accurate paediatric burn size estimates.
  8. Parkland is an initial estimate; titrate fluids to perfusion and specialist protocol.
  9. Deep, high-risk-site, circumferential, chemical and electrical burns need burns-service assessment.
  10. Do not routinely prescribe systemic antibiotics for uncomplicated clean burns.
  11. Split-thickness grafts cover larger defects; flaps carry their own blood supply.
  12. Infection, haematoma, shear and poor perfusion impair graft survival.
