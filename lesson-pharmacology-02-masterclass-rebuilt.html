Safe prescribing · Clinical deep dive

The pharmacology and human factors behind common prescribing errors.

PHARMACOKINETICS

1 · Why renal function matters

Clearance determines drug exposure over time. When clearance falls, a maintenance regimen that was previously appropriate may cause accumulation. Half-life may increase, particularly for renally eliminated drugs and active metabolites. Serum creatinine alone can be misleading in people with low muscle mass or rapidly changing kidney function.

Clinical link: check the drug-specific kidney-function method and recommended monitoring; a loading dose and maintenance dose may require different adjustments.

PHARMACODYNAMICS

2 · Additive adverse effects

Two drugs can cause harm without directly altering each other's concentrations. Opioids plus sedatives can depress respiration; multiple blood-pressure-lowering drugs can contribute to syncope; antithrombotic combinations can increase bleeding. This is pharmacodynamic interaction.

DRUG HANDLING

3 · Formulation is not a minor detail

Immediate-release and modified-release products may have different dosing intervals and risks. Transdermal patches continue releasing drug after application; a patch left on during a new application can cause duplication. Never crush a formulation unless suitability has been checked.

THERAPEUTIC INDEX

4 · Narrow margins of safety

For medicines such as lithium and some immunosuppressants, small changes in concentration can matter clinically. Changes in renal function, fluid balance, interacting drugs or adherence may require reassessment and drug-level monitoring where recommended.

HUMAN FACTORS

5 · How systems create error

Look-alike/sound-alike names, interruptions, autopopulated orders, inconsistent units and poor transitions of care all increase risk. Independent checks, standardised charts, medicines reconciliation and a culture of reporting reduce harm.

PRESCRIBING LAW & ETHICS

6 · Accountability and consent

Prescribers are responsible for working within competence, obtaining adequate information, explaining material risks and documenting decisions. Controlled-drug prescriptions have additional legal requirements. When errors occur, be open and follow local statutory and professional duty-of-candour processes.

MONITORING

7 · Closed-loop prescribing

A test ordered but not reviewed is a safety gap. Record what to monitor, when, who reviews it and what changes should trigger action. Examples include renal function and potassium after selected cardiovascular medicines, blood counts with marrow-suppressing drugs and glucose with insulin.

CALCULATION

8 · Units, concentrations and dimensional analysis

Write units beside every value. For a solution containing 20 mg/mL, a 60 mg prescribed dose requires 60 mg ÷ 20 mg/mL = 3 mL. The mg units cancel, leaving mL. If the final units do not match the required output, recheck the calculation.

Clinical reasoning checkpoints

Case A · Insulin and missed meal

A patient is prescribed rapid-acting insulin but is not eating. Confirm glucose and insulin regimen, assess risk and seek immediate local diabetes-team guidance rather than administering automatically.

Case B · Deterioration after new medicine

New hypotension and rash after an antibiotic may signal anaphylaxis. Treat as an emergency using the local resuscitation pathway; incident documentation comes after stabilisation.

1. What makes prescribing safe?

Safe prescribing is an iterative process: establish a diagnosis or therapeutic goal, consider non-drug options, select an evidence-based medicine, individualise its regimen, communicate the order, and monitor both benefit and harm. The safest drug is not necessarily the most potent: it is the option whose expected benefit outweighs risk for the particular patient.

Prescribing errors may involve selection (wrong drug), patient (wrong person), dose, formulation, route, timing, omission, duplication, monitoring or documentation. An adverse drug reaction can occur despite correct use; an error is a preventable failure in the medication-use process. An error may cause no harm, near-miss harm or actual harm.

2. Before prescribing: the clinical safety screen

Confirm the patient and the indication

Use at least two identifiers according to local policy. Check what is being treated, whether a medicine is needed, how urgent treatment is and what outcome you expect. A diagnosis matters: an antibiotic cannot compensate for a missed abscess requiring drainage, and a sedative can obscure a deteriorating neurological examination.

Medication history and reconciliation

Ask what the patient actually takes, including prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, inhalers, patches, eye drops, herbal products and recently stopped drugs. Compare the patient's account with reliable records, packaging, pharmacy or carers when appropriate. Record the name, dose, route, frequency, indication and last dose for relevant drugs. Clarify discrepancies rather than copying a historical list.

Allergy versus intolerance

Document the suspected drug, reaction, severity, timing and whether treatment was needed. Anaphylaxis, urticaria and severe delayed skin reactions require different risk assessment from nausea or an expected pharmacological effect. Do not relabel an uncertain reaction as 'no allergy' without assessment. Escalate complex allergy histories and use local antibiotic-allergy guidance.

Red flag: A previous severe cutaneous adverse reaction (for example Stevens–Johnson syndrome) or anaphylaxis warrants careful avoidance and specialist advice. Do not casually re-challenge.

3. Writing a complete, unambiguous prescription

Requirements differ by setting, medicine and whether the prescription is electronic, inpatient, outpatient or for a controlled drug. Always follow local legal and prescribing-system rules. A typical safe order includes the correct patient, medicine name, strength and formulation, dose with units, route, frequency and timing, indication where useful, start/stop or review date, and prescriber details.

Example — illustrative, not a treatment recommendation

“Paracetamol 500 mg tablets — take TWO tablets (total 1 g) by mouth when required for pain, up to four times in 24 hours; minimum 4-hour interval; review duration and maximum daily dose against current BNF and individual risk.” This demonstrates why formulation, number of units, total dose, route, frequency and limits must be clear. Lower limits or alternatives may be needed for some patients.

Units and abbreviations

Write micrograms and units in full when confusion is possible. Use a leading zero before a decimal (0.5 mg), avoid unnecessary trailing zeros (5 mg, not 5.0 mg), and avoid ambiguous abbreviations such as “U” for units. Confirm concentrations: 1 mL of one formulation may contain a very different amount from 1 mL of another. State the route explicitly where ambiguity could cause harm.

As-required medicines and antimicrobial prescriptions

For PRN orders, state the indication, dose, minimum interval and maximum frequency/daily amount as appropriate. For antibiotics, record the indication, agent, route, planned duration or review date, relevant microbiology, allergy and renal checks. Use local antimicrobial guidelines and review IV-to-oral switch when appropriate.

4. Dose calculations without shortcuts

Tablets: number of tablets = prescribed dose ÷ strength per tablet. Liquid: volume (mL) = required dose (mg) ÷ concentration (mg/mL). Weight-based: required dose = prescribed mg/kg × weight in kg, subject to maximum dose and formulation checks. Infusion: rate (mL/hour) = prescribed amount per hour ÷ concentration per mL.

Worked example A — oral liquid

Prescription: 250 mg. Bottle: 125 mg in 5 mL. Concentration = 25 mg/mL. Volume = 250 ÷ 25 = 10 mL. Check the label and measuring device; do not assume all bottles have the same concentration.

Worked example B — infusion arithmetic

Solution contains 400 mg in 200 mL (2 mg/mL). An illustrative order requests 12 mg/hour. Rate = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 mL/hour. This is a maths example only, not a validated drug regimen; real infusions require drug-specific protocol and pump checks.

Common calculation traps: mg versus micrograms (1000-fold), mg versus mL, using pounds instead of kilograms, incorrect decimal placement, incorrect concentration and forgetting a maximum dose. For high-risk calculations use an independent check under local policy.

6. Individualising prescriptions

Kidney impairment and acute illness

Check whether the medicine is renally cleared, nephrotoxic or requires monitoring. In stable renal function, estimated kidney function may guide adjustment, but some drugs use Cockcroft–Gault creatinine clearance rather than eGFR. In acute kidney injury, estimates are less reliable because creatinine is changing. Seek pharmacy or senior advice for narrow-therapeutic-index medicines.

Older adults and frailty

Polypharmacy increases cumulative risks such as falls, delirium, hypotension, anticholinergic burden and bleeding. Review whether each medicine still has a clear indication; consider a lower starting dose where clinically appropriate, but do not undertreat serious illness simply because of age.

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and children

Confirm pregnancy possibility sensitively where relevant; use current UK pregnancy resources and specialist guidance rather than blanket assumptions that all medicines are unsafe. Paediatric prescribing often needs a verified weight, age-appropriate formulation, maximum dose and independent calculation. For breastfeeding, consider infant exposure and available safety evidence.

7. When a medication error occurs

1 · Recognise and protect

Assess ABCDE and observations. Identify the medicine, amount, route and time. If there is suspected serious toxicity or deterioration, summon urgent senior/resuscitation help and follow emergency protocols.

↓
2 · Prevent further harm

Withhold further doses when appropriate, secure the medicine/chart, check relevant investigations and seek pharmacy, toxicology or specialist advice. Do not reverse or stop essential therapy blindly without considering risks.

↓
3 · Escalate, disclose and document

Tell the responsible senior and clinical team promptly. Record objective facts, actions, observations and the plan. Explain openly to the patient, apologise when appropriate and follow the organisation's duty-of-candour process.

↓
4 · Report and learn

Use the local incident reporting system and appropriate national reporting routes (for example, Yellow Card for suspected adverse drug reactions). Participate in a systems-focused review rather than hiding the mistake or blaming an individual.

Errors often have multiple contributors: confusing packaging, similar drug names, poor handover, interruptions, missing weight, inaccessible lab results or poorly designed electronic defaults. A good response addresses both the patient and the underlying system.

8. Monitoring, review and deprescribing

Every prescription needs a follow-up question: how will we know whether it works and whether it is causing harm? Monitoring may include symptoms, blood pressure, glucose, drug concentrations, FBC, electrolytes, renal function, liver tests or ECG, depending on the medicine. Define when to reassess and who will act on results.

At discharge, reconcile the final medicine list, explain starts/stops/dose changes, provide written information and communicate monitoring responsibilities to the GP and patient. For long-term medicines, review ongoing indication, benefit, adverse effects, adherence and withdrawal risks. Some drugs require tapering rather than abrupt discontinuation.

9. Worked clinical cases

Case A · Methotrexate ordered every day

A patient with rheumatoid arthritis has methotrexate documented as “10 mg daily” on admission. Reasoning: low-dose methotrexate for inflammatory disease is generally prescribed weekly; daily dosing can be life-threatening. Do not administer until the schedule and indication are verified with the patient, records and specialist/pharmacist. Assess any doses already taken, symptoms and relevant blood tests; escalate promptly if exposure occurred.

Case B · New confusion after opioid escalation

An older inpatient becomes drowsy after an opioid dose increase; respiratory rate is low. Reasoning: suspect opioid toxicity while checking glucose and other causes. Perform ABCDE, call for urgent help, support airway and breathing, withhold further opioid and follow the local naloxone protocol if indicated. Monitor for recurrent toxicity because opioid duration may exceed naloxone's effect.

Case C · Anticoagulant on discharge

A patient with atrial fibrillation has two anticoagulants listed on discharge. Reasoning: clarify whether this represents an intentional short overlap or duplication; review indication, procedure history, renal function, bleeding risk and specialist plan. Do not send an ambiguous prescription. Communicate the corrected regimen and monitoring clearly.

Case D · Antibiotic allergy history

A patient reports a “penicillin allergy” but recalls only mild nausea many years ago. Reasoning: take a structured allergy history, distinguish intolerance from immune-mediated reaction, and use local risk assessment and prescribing guidance. Do not erase the label or challenge the patient without an appropriate process.

10. PLAB 2 OSCE: explaining a prescribing error

Station: You are the junior doctor. A patient received an unintended extra dose of a medicine and is currently stable. Explain what happened and the next steps.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, ensure privacy and ask what the patient understands.
  2. State the known facts plainly without speculation: “An additional dose was given when it should not have been.”
  3. Apologise and acknowledge the impact: “I'm sorry this happened.” Avoid defensive language or blaming colleagues.
  4. Explain immediate assessment, observations, tests and monitoring in language the patient understands.
  5. Describe senior review, incident reporting, investigation and how updates will be provided.
  6. Invite questions, check understanding, document the discussion and safety-net symptoms.
OSCE examiner looks for: patient safety first, truthful disclosure, empathy, clear explanation, appropriate escalation, follow-up and documentation. Do not promise there is zero risk or speculate about fault.

11. PLAB exam traps and take-home checklist

  • “Allergy” on the chart is a starting point for clarification, not permission to ignore it.
  • A normal creatinine result does not always mean normal renal clearance, especially in frail or acutely ill patients.
  • Never convert mg to mL without the formulation's concentration.
  • “Weekly” and “daily” are not interchangeable for methotrexate.
  • In an error, treat the patient before completing paperwork.
  • Prescribing is not finished when the order is signed: monitoring and handover are essential.
