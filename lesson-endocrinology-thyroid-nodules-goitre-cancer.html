Thyroid Nodules, Goitre & Thyroid Cancer

Emergency: Stridor, airway compromise or expanding post-thyroidectomy neck swelling requires immediate escalation.

Overview and priorities

Thyroid nodules are common and most are benign. Assessment aims to identify malignancy, thyroid dysfunction and compressive symptoms.

PLAB application: Ask about rapid growth, hoarseness, dysphagia and breathing difficulty.

History of a thyroid lump

Ask onset, growth, pain, radiation exposure, family thyroid cancer or MEN2, thyroid symptoms and previous neck surgery.

PLAB application: Persistent unexplained hoarseness or progressive compressive symptoms warrant urgent assessment.

Examination

Inspect and palpate thyroid while swallowing; note size, consistency, fixation, asymmetry, tenderness and cervical lymph nodes. Assess voice and airway.

PLAB application: A hard fixed mass or suspicious nodes increase concern but examination cannot rule out cancer.

Thyroid function testing

Check TSH as an initial functional assessment; add FT4 and sometimes FT3 when indicated. Most thyroid cancers are euthyroid.

PLAB application: Normal TSH does not exclude thyroid cancer.

Ultrasound risk stratification

Ultrasound evaluates solid versus cystic structure, echogenicity, irregular margins, microcalcifications, shape and suspicious lymph nodes.

PLAB application: Ultrasound findings guide need for ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration.

Fine needle aspiration cytology

FNA samples selected nodules based on ultrasound features, size and clinical context. UK cytology may use Thy categories (Thy1–Thy5).

PLAB application: Thy1 is non-diagnostic; Thy2 benign; Thy3 indeterminate; Thy4 suspicious; Thy5 malignant.

When to refer urgently

Rapidly enlarging mass, persistent unexplained hoarseness, suspicious lymphadenopathy or airway compromise needs prompt escalation; suspected cancer follows local urgent referral criteria.

PLAB application: Stridor or acute airway compromise is an emergency, not a routine clinic referral.

Multinodular goitre

May be euthyroid, hypothyroid or hyperthyroid. Large goitres may cause swallowing, breathing or positional symptoms.

PLAB application: Assess function, compressive symptoms and suspicious dominant nodules.

Benign nodules and cysts

Many benign asymptomatic nodules need observation rather than immediate surgery. Recurrent symptomatic cysts may need specialist treatment.

PLAB application: Avoid promising that every nodule requires removal.

Papillary thyroid cancer

Most common differentiated thyroid cancer; often spreads to cervical lymph nodes and generally has a favourable prognosis.

PLAB application: Suspicious cervical nodes may need ultrasound and sampling.

Follicular thyroid cancer

Differentiated cancer with potential haematogenous spread; cytology alone may not distinguish follicular adenoma from carcinoma because capsular or vascular invasion needs histology.

PLAB application: An indeterminate follicular lesion may require diagnostic surgery.

Medullary thyroid cancer

Arises from parafollicular C cells, may secrete calcitonin and can be associated with germline RET variants and MEN2.

PLAB application: Specialist evaluation includes hereditary risk assessment and genetic counselling where indicated.

Anaplastic thyroid cancer

Rare aggressive malignancy, often presenting with a rapidly growing neck mass, dysphagia, hoarseness or airway compromise.

PLAB application: Urgent multidisciplinary assessment and airway planning are essential.

Cancer staging and treatment

Management is multidisciplinary and depends on histology, tumour size, nodes, metastases and patient preferences. Options include lobectomy or total thyroidectomy, selected radioactive iodine and surveillance.

PLAB application: Not all cancers need identical surgery or radioiodine; treatment is risk-adapted.

Post-operative issues

Monitor for neck haematoma, voice change from recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and hypocalcaemia from parathyroid injury; thyroid hormone replacement may be needed.

PLAB application: Expanding neck swelling after thyroid surgery is an airway emergency.

Follow-up and counselling

Explain ultrasound and cytology uncertainty, shared decisions, potential surgery, lifelong levothyroxine after total thyroidectomy and specialist follow-up.

PLAB application: Safety-net increasing neck size, new hoarseness, dysphagia or breathing difficulty.

PLAB 2: Neck lump consultation OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent and explore patient concerns.
  2. Ask about onset, growth, voice, swallowing, breathing, pain, radiation exposure and family history.
  3. Examine thyroid, cervical nodes and voice while respecting dignity.
  4. Explain thyroid blood tests, ultrasound and selective FNA in plain language.
  5. Safety-net rapid growth, persistent hoarseness, dysphagia and breathing difficulty; escalate stridor immediately.

Deep Dive

Why TSH matters

Suppressed TSH may indicate a functioning nodule and change the diagnostic pathway, but TSH does not determine cancer risk alone.

Ultrasound and FNA

Sampling decisions balance sonographic suspicion, size and clinical context; not every nodule needs a needle biopsy.

Cytology limitations

Follicular-patterned lesions may require surgical histology to determine invasion.

Lymphatic versus blood spread

Papillary cancers often involve neck nodes; follicular cancers may spread haematogenously.

Hereditary medullary cancer

RET-associated disease can have implications for relatives and evaluation of associated endocrine tumours.

Postoperative neck haematoma

Even a small-looking expanding neck swelling may threaten the airway and needs immediate escalation.
