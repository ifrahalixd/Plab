Learning objectives

Inflammation, effusions and myocardial disease

Recognise DVT and PE; apply two-level Wells scoring; choose D-dimer, ultrasound and CTPA appropriately; start safe interim and definitive anticoagulation; recognise haemodynamically unstable PE and pregnancy-specific pathways.

Emergency rule: Suspected PE with shock, persistent hypotension, severe hypoxaemia or collapse requires immediate emergency assessment and senior-led treatment. Do not delay resuscitation for scoring tools.

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) overview

DVT is clot formation in a deep vein, most commonly in the lower limb. PE occurs when thrombus embolises into the pulmonary arterial circulation. Virchow triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability.

Assessment and management: Risk factors include recent surgery or immobility, cancer, prior VTE, pregnancy/postpartum state, oestrogen exposure and inherited thrombophilia. Absence of a risk factor does not exclude VTE.

Suspected lower-limb DVT

Unilateral swelling, pain, calf tenderness, pitting oedema and increased warmth raise suspicion. Cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst and musculoskeletal injury can mimic DVT.

Assessment and management: Use the two-level DVT Wells score. Score ≥2 means DVT likely; ≤1 means unlikely. Follow NICE NG158 testing pathways rather than treating a score as a diagnosis.

DVT likely: Wells ≥2

Arrange proximal leg vein ultrasound with result within 4 hours if possible. A negative ultrasound does not always finish the pathway.

Assessment and management: If ultrasound cannot be obtained within 4 hours, offer D-dimer, interim therapeutic anticoagulation (after assessing bleeding risk and contraindications), and ultrasound with result within 24 hours. After a negative proximal scan with positive D-dimer, NICE recommends repeat ultrasound 6–8 days later.

DVT unlikely: Wells ≤1

A D-dimer is the initial test. Negative D-dimer generally makes DVT unlikely when the pathway is correctly applied.

Assessment and management: If D-dimer is positive, arrange proximal ultrasound within 4 hours if possible, or interim therapeutic anticoagulation and ultrasound within 24 hours. Consider an age-adjusted D-dimer threshold for people over 50 where the assay/pathway supports it.

Pulmonary embolism: presentation

Typical symptoms include sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, haemoptysis or syncope. Signs may include hypoxaemia, tachypnoea and evidence of DVT. Normal oxygen saturation does not rule out PE.

Assessment and management: Assess ABCDE, vital signs, ECG and competing emergencies (ACS, pneumothorax, pneumonia, dissection). Apply PERC only in appropriately selected low-suspicion patients; otherwise use the two-level PE Wells score.

PE Wells and diagnostic testing

Two-level PE Wells: >4 points = PE likely; ≤4 points = PE unlikely. The score helps select imaging versus D-dimer, not diagnose PE.

Assessment and management: If PE likely, offer CTPA immediately (or V/Q SPECT/scan when appropriate). If imaging cannot be done immediately, consider interim therapeutic anticoagulation. If PE unlikely, D-dimer first; a positive result leads to imaging. NICE recommends considering age-adjusted D-dimer thresholds over age 50.

Imaging and tests

CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is the usual first-line imaging test for suspected PE. V/Q imaging is considered for contrast allergy, severe renal impairment or other circumstances. Compression ultrasound confirms proximal DVT.

Assessment and management: ECG may show sinus tachycardia or right heart strain, but S1Q3T3 is neither sensitive nor specific. Chest X-ray helps consider alternatives; a normal result does not exclude PE. Troponin and echocardiography can assist risk assessment.

Anticoagulation: first steps

Before anticoagulation assess bleeding risk, renal function, full blood count, liver function and coagulation tests. Baseline results should not cause avoidable delay in indicated interim anticoagulation; review results promptly.

Assessment and management: For confirmed proximal DVT or PE, NICE recommends anticoagulation for at least 3 months. Apixaban or rivaroxaban are commonly offered when suitable; other options include LMWH followed by dabigatran/edoxaban or LMWH with a VKA. Dose, lead-in, renal adjustment and interactions must be checked in current BNF/local protocols.

High-risk (haemodynamically unstable) PE

Persistent hypotension or shock suggests high-risk PE, which can cause acute right ventricular failure and obstructive shock.

Assessment and management: Resuscitate and involve emergency/critical care and specialist teams urgently. NICE advises continuous unfractionated heparin infusion and consideration of systemic thrombolysis for PE with haemodynamic instability; decisions require expert assessment of bleeding risk and contraindications.

Long-term and special circumstances

Review at 3 months: whether VTE was provoked or unprovoked, recurrence and bleeding risks, patient preferences and persistent risk factors. Active cancer, antiphospholipid syndrome and severe renal disease may change anticoagulant choice.

Assessment and management: Do not routinely investigate all unprovoked VTE with extensive occult-cancer imaging without signs/symptoms. Offer history, examination and age-appropriate screening; consider further investigations when clinically indicated.

Pregnancy and postpartum VTE

Pregnancy and puerperium increase VTE risk. Standard DVT/PE Wells scores and routine D-dimer pathways are not validated for pregnancy in the same way as nonpregnant adults.

Assessment and management: Suspected pregnancy-related VTE needs urgent obstetric/medical assessment and pregnancy-specific imaging pathways. LMWH is usually the anticoagulant of choice; DOACs are avoided during pregnancy. Imaging should not be withheld solely because of pregnancy.

DVT complications and prevention

Untreated DVT can lead to PE and post-thrombotic syndrome; recurrent VTE is possible. Long-term symptoms may include chronic swelling and discomfort.

Assessment and management: Encourage mobility and prevention tailored to surgical/medical risk. NICE NG89 covers hospital-associated VTE prevention. Compression stockings are not routinely offered solely to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome after DVT, though they may help symptoms in selected patients.

PLAB 2: focused VTE OSCE

  1. ABCDE and observations; recognise shock, syncope, severe breathlessness and chest pain.
  2. Ask about onset, leg symptoms, immobility, surgery, cancer, oestrogen, pregnancy, prior VTE and bleeding risks.
  3. Examine legs, cardiorespiratory status and oxygenation; do not rely on Homan sign.
  4. Explain Wells assessment, D-dimer, ultrasound/CTPA and why interim anticoagulation may be necessary.
  5. Explain bleeding precautions, medication adherence, red flags and follow-up.

Mechanisms and higher-order reasoning

Deep Dive

Virchow triad and thrombosis

Stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability interact. Immobilisation and surgery contribute to more than one pathway.

Why D-dimer is not diagnostic

It is sensitive but nonspecific: inflammation, pregnancy, malignancy and recent surgery can raise it. It is most useful to exclude VTE in appropriately selected low-probability patients.

Why Wells comes before testing

Pre-test probability changes the meaning of a negative or positive result. High-probability patients need imaging rather than relying on D-dimer.

PE haemodynamics

Large emboli raise pulmonary vascular resistance, strain the right ventricle and can reduce left ventricular filling, causing obstructive shock.

Anticoagulants versus thrombolysis

Anticoagulants prevent clot propagation while endogenous fibrinolysis resolves thrombus. Thrombolysis actively breaks down clot but substantially increases bleeding risk; it is not routine for stable PE.

Why renal function matters

Drug selection and dosing depend on renal clearance; DOACs and LMWH have agent-specific restrictions. Check current BNF and local policy.

Provoked versus unprovoked VTE

A transient major risk factor influences recurrence risk and the decision to stop or extend treatment after the initial 3 months. Shared decision-making weighs recurrent VTE against bleeding.

Pregnancy and VTE

Physiological hypercoagulability and venous stasis increase risk. Pregnancy-specific investigation and anticoagulant selection differ from the standard adult pathway.
