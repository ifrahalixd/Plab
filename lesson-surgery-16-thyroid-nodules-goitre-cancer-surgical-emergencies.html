Thyroid disease: PLAB surgical approach

Emergency red flags: stridor, rapidly enlarging neck swelling, respiratory distress, severe dysphagia, or expanding swelling after thyroidectomy require immediate airway and senior surgical/anaesthetic help. Never delay emergency action for routine imaging.

1 · Clinical assessment

Ask duration, rapid growth, pain, voice change, dysphagia, dyspnoea, positional choking, radiation exposure, family history of thyroid cancer or MEN2, and symptoms of hypo-/hyperthyroidism. Examine neck from front and behind: location, size, movement on swallowing, cervical nodes, tracheal deviation, retrosternal extension and signs of thyroid dysfunction. Thyroid swellings usually move with swallowing; a thyroglossal duct cyst may move with tongue protrusion. Document voice; consider laryngeal examination if dysphonia.

2 · Initial investigations and nodule risk stratification

Check TSH and thyroid function (free T4 ± T3 according to results). Ultrasound assesses nodule composition, echogenicity, margins, calcifications, shape and cervical nodes. Suspicious sonographic features and nodule size guide ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) under local thyroid nodule pathways (e.g. British Thyroid Association ultrasound U classification). Not every nodule requires biopsy. If TSH is suppressed, consider radionuclide scanning for an autonomous nodule with specialist advice; functioning 'hot' nodules are rarely malignant. Cytology may be reported using Thy categories and guide repeat biopsy, surveillance or diagnostic surgery. FNAC cannot reliably distinguish follicular adenoma from carcinoma, which requires histological capsular/vascular invasion.

4 · Thyroid cancers

Papillary: most common differentiated thyroid cancer; often lymphatic spread to cervical nodes. Follicular: haematogenous spread to bone/lung; histological invasion distinguishes carcinoma. Medullary: parafollicular C-cell tumour producing calcitonin; can be associated with RET/MEN2; specialist genetic assessment and exclusion of phaeochromocytoma before thyroid surgery when MEN2 suspected. Anaplastic: rapidly growing aggressive mass, often with airway symptoms; urgent MDT and airway assessment. Thyroid lymphoma: rapidly enlarging thyroid, sometimes on background Hashimoto disease; needs tissue diagnosis and specialist care.

5 · Management and follow-up

Suspected malignancy needs urgent specialist assessment, ultrasound/cytology, appropriate staging and thyroid MDT. Treatment depends on subtype and risk: lobectomy or total thyroidectomy for selected differentiated cancers, lymph-node surgery when indicated, radioactive iodine for selected differentiated cases, thyroid hormone replacement and tailored surveillance (thyroglobulin for appropriate differentiated cancer follow-up). Medullary cancer management differs and does not respond to radioactive iodine. Shared decision-making includes nerve injury, hypocalcaemia, bleeding, lifelong hormone therapy and possible voice changes.

6 · Thyroidectomy complications

Neck haematoma: early tense neck swelling, distress or stridor is an immediate airway emergency; activate emergency protocol and senior help. Where trained and per local protocol, emergency wound opening at bedside may be lifesaving while airway support arrives. Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury: hoarseness (bilateral injury can obstruct airway). Hypocalcaemia from hypoparathyroidism: perioral tingling, carpopedal spasm, tetany, prolonged QT; check adjusted/ionised calcium and treat urgently when symptomatic. Thyroid storm: life-threatening severe thyrotoxicosis with fever, tachyarrhythmia and delirium; resuscitation and urgent endocrine/critical care management. Hypothyroidism: requires replacement following total thyroidectomy.

7 · Referral and communication

Rapid growth, unexplained hoarseness, hard fixed nodule, suspicious nodes or compression merit urgent specialist evaluation. Explain that most nodules are benign but tests are needed to assess risk. Safety-net new breathing difficulty or rapidly increasing swelling as emergencies.

Clinical frameworks

A · New thyroid nodule

History and airway red flags → examine gland, voice and nodes → TSH/free T4 as indicated → thyroid/neck ultrasound → risk-based FNAC or specialist functional imaging if TSH suppressed → MDT/referral or surveillance based on findings.

B · Compressive goitre

Stridor or respiratory compromise? Emergency airway escalation. Otherwise assess dysphagia, orthopnoea, voice, tracheal position → thyroid tests, ultrasound ± cross-sectional imaging if stable and indicated → endocrine/ENT/thyroid surgical assessment.

C · Post-thyroidectomy deterioration

ABCDE and immediate help → expanding neck swelling/stridor: treat as haematoma and airway emergency → hoarseness: assess vocal cord function → tingling/spasm: check calcium and ECG → fever, tachycardia, delirium: consider thyroid storm.

Exam traps

Follicular adenoma versus carcinoma cannot be separated by FNAC alone; medullary cancer arises from C cells; MEN2 requires phaeochromocytoma assessment; a normal TSH does not exclude cancer; an unstable airway takes priority over scans.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Painless thyroid nodule

A 43-year-old has a solitary thyroid nodule and normal TSH. Ultrasound shows irregular margins and microcalcifications. Approach: urgent thyroid specialist assessment and ultrasound-guided FNAC according to size and risk criteria; normal thyroid function does not rule out malignancy.

Case 2 — Retrosternal goitre

A 66-year-old has a large goitre, difficulty swallowing and breathlessness when lying flat. Approach: assess airway immediately, evaluate compression and refer for thyroid surgical review; urgent escalation if stridor or respiratory compromise.

Case 3 — Postoperative neck swelling

One hour after thyroidectomy, a patient becomes distressed with a tense swelling under the wound and noisy breathing. Approach: activate airway and postoperative haematoma emergency response; call senior anaesthetics and surgery immediately, prepare for emergency wound decompression according to protocol.

Case 4 — Tingling after surgery

A patient develops perioral tingling and carpopedal spasm the day after total thyroidectomy. Approach: suspect symptomatic hypocalcaemia, obtain calcium and ECG, give urgent calcium replacement per local protocol and seek senior review.

PLAB 2 — Thyroid lump consultation

Station: A 38-year-old presents with a neck lump that moves on swallowing and asks if it is cancer.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, gain consent and ask the patient's concerns.
  2. Clarify onset, growth, tenderness, recent infection, previous radiation and family thyroid cancer/MEN2.
  3. Screen urgently for breathlessness, stridor, swallowing difficulty and voice change.
  4. Ask weight change, palpitations, heat/cold intolerance, tremor, bowel change and fatigue.
  5. Offer thyroid, neck-node and systemic thyroid examination; arrange a chaperone if appropriate.
  6. Explain most thyroid nodules are not cancer, but appearance and symptoms determine testing.
  7. Discuss blood thyroid tests and ultrasound; biopsy may be recommended based on scan findings.
  8. Explain specialist referral for suspicious findings or compressive symptoms, with an agreed follow-up plan.
  9. Safety-net: emergency help for breathing difficulty/stridor or rapidly expanding swelling; prompt review for new hoarseness or dysphagia.
  10. Check understanding, address anxiety and document the plan.

Plain language: “The thyroid is a gland at the front of the neck. Most lumps are harmless, but an ultrasound and blood tests help us decide whether a small needle sample is needed.”

Rapid revision

  1. Thyroid nodules require TSH, examination and ultrasound risk assessment.
  2. Normal thyroid function does not exclude thyroid cancer.
  3. Ultrasound findings and size determine whether FNAC is indicated.
  4. Suppressed TSH may prompt radionuclide scanning for autonomous nodules.
  5. FNAC cannot establish follicular capsular or vascular invasion.
  6. Papillary thyroid cancer commonly spreads to cervical nodes.
  7. Follicular thyroid carcinoma may spread via blood to bone and lung.
  8. Medullary cancer produces calcitonin and may be linked to RET/MEN2.
  9. Exclude phaeochromocytoma before thyroid surgery in suspected MEN2.
  10. Anaplastic cancer is rapidly aggressive and may threaten the airway.
  11. Expanding neck haematoma after thyroidectomy is an airway emergency.
  12. Post-thyroidectomy tingling/tetany suggests hypocalcaemia; hoarseness suggests laryngeal nerve injury.
