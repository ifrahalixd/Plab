Abnormal cervical screening result: interpretation, triage and patient-centred management

The traditional phrase “abnormal smear” is often used by patients, but the NHS Cervical Screening Programme in England uses primary high-risk HPV (hrHPV) testing. Cytology is then used to examine cells when indicated. An abnormal test is not a diagnosis of cervical cancer. It is a risk signal requiring the correct screening pathway, documentation and follow-up.

1. Decode the report before making a plan

hrHPV positive means one or more high-risk HPV types were detected; it does not prove new infection, infidelity, precancer or cancer. Negative cytology means no cytological abnormality was identified on the triage sample. Borderline/low-grade dyskaryosis describes mild nuclear abnormalities; high-grade dyskaryosis raises concern for significant precancer and needs timely assessment. Cytology is a screening tool; biopsy histology confirms CIN, glandular lesions or invasion.

Do not confuse screening and diagnosis. A person with abnormal vaginal bleeding or a suspicious cervix needs a symptomatic diagnostic pathway. A recent negative HPV screen does not rule out cervical or other gynaecological disease.

2. Mechanism: how HPV causes dysplasia

HPV infects basal squamous epithelial cells, particularly around the transformation zone where glandular epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia. Most infections clear through immune response. Persistent high-risk HPV (notably types 16/18) increases the chance of dysplasia over years. Viral E6 promotes p53 degradation and E7 functionally disables retinoblastoma protein (pRb); cells proliferate despite DNA damage. This underpins dyskaryosis, CIN and eventual invasive progression in a minority of untreated cases.

HPV vaccination substantially reduces risk of HPV-related lesions but does not replace cervical screening. A positive result cannot date the infection or identify who transmitted it.

3. Step-by-step NHS England screening pathway (2026)

  1. Verify identity, age, prior results and cervical status. Confirm the report, screening versus follow-up pathway, sample adequacy, and whether prior treatment (CIN2+, CGIN) alters recall.
  2. Determine hrHPV status. hrHPV-negative routine sample generally returns to a 5-year interval in England; post-treatment or previously positive individuals may follow different recall.
  3. If positive, assess triage cytology. Negative cytology at an initial routine screen generally means repeat screening at 12 months; repeat again at 24 months where indicated. Persistent positivity at 24 months with negative cytology generally leads to colposcopy. Any cytological abnormality with positive HPV requires referral as per pathway.
  4. Check for urgent symptoms. Postcoital, intermenstrual or postmenopausal bleeding, persistent abnormal discharge and visible cervical lesion are not resolved by waiting for routine recall.
  5. Close the loop. Explain the plan, confirm appointment method, ensure result acknowledgment and follow up nonattendance or failed contact through practice failsafe systems.

Programme details vary between the four UK nations and by age, previous HPV status, self-sampling, treatment and immunosuppression. Consult the current programme-generated recommendation rather than reconstructing a bespoke interval.

5. Examination when clinically indicated

Asymptomatic people with an abnormal screening result alone do not automatically need another speculum examination before an arranged colposcopy. When symptomatic, obtain consent, offer a chaperone, assess observations if unwell, and perform abdominal/pelvic examination as appropriate. With speculum examination, describe the cervix (normal, ectropion, polyp, friable lesion, ulceration, irregular growth), discharge, contact bleeding and vaginal tissue changes. Consider pregnancy testing, STI NAAT and other tests by history. Do not repeatedly scrape a suspicious visible lesion in place of referral.

Urgent referral: NICE NG12 advises considering a suspected-cancer pathway referral when the cervix has an appearance consistent with cervical cancer. A normal screening report never overrides a concerning examination.

6. Colposcopy and biopsy — what actually happens?

Colposcopy uses magnification with acetic acid and often iodine to assess the cervix and transformation zone. Acetowhite change, mosaicism, punctation and abnormal vessels guide biopsy, but are not definitive diagnoses. The colposcopist records visibility of the squamocolumnar junction, lesion extent, sampling and management. In some higher-risk cases, treatment may occur at the same visit (“see and treat”) where appropriate; lower-grade referrals generally should not be treated solely on screening cytology.

LLETZ/LEEP removes transformation-zone tissue with an electrosurgical loop and gives histology; it is not automatically required for every positive HPV result. Discuss transient pain/bleeding, infection, follow-up, and potential increased risk of preterm birth after excisional treatments, particularly larger/repeated excisions. Pregnancy usually changes timing; colposcopy may still be needed to exclude cancer, but routine excisional treatment for CIN is generally deferred unless invasion is suspected.

8. Patient counselling: correct misconceptions

  • “Do I have cancer?” Not necessarily. Most abnormal screening tests are HPV infection or precancerous changes, and the follow-up exists to prevent cancer developing.
  • “Does HPV mean my partner cheated?” No. HPV can remain dormant or persist for years; timing cannot be inferred.
  • “Can I give HPV antibiotics?” No antibiotic eradicates HPV; the programme monitors risk and treats tissue changes when appropriate.
  • “I had a vaccine.” Vaccination lowers risk but screening remains important.
  • “Will colposcopy make me infertile?” Diagnostic colposcopy and small biopsies are usually not associated with infertility. Excisional treatment has obstetric risk considerations that should be discussed individually.
  • “What if I miss follow-up?” Offer booking help, address access barriers, document the plan and arrange active recall, not passive reassurance.

9. Worked cases with explicit reasoning

Case 1 — HPV positive, normal cytology

A 29-year-old receives her first hrHPV-positive result and no abnormal cells. She has no symptoms. DDx: transient versus persistent hrHPV; cytological dysplasia is not identified. Reasoning: risk surveillance, not treatment for HPV alone. Plan: explain 12-month repeat via programme, confirm contact details, safety-net unusual bleeding.

Case 2 — HPV positive, high-grade dyskaryosis

A 37-year-old has abnormal cytology suggesting high-grade disease. DDx: CIN2/3, less commonly invasive disease; cytology cannot stage either. Plan: timely colposcopy, biopsy/histology, explain procedure and safety-net; ensure referral is acted upon.

Case 3 — Negative HPV screen but postcoital bleeding

A 46-year-old has recurrent bleeding after sex and persistent watery discharge. Her screening 8 months ago was negative. DDx: ectropion, cervicitis/STI, cervical polyp, atrophy, cervical cancer. Plan: pelvic/speculum examination, infection testing when appropriate, and suspected-cancer pathway if cervix looks concerning. A negative screen is not a diagnostic clearance.

Case 4 — Post-treatment follow-up

A 34-year-old underwent LLETZ for CIN3. Current HPV follow-up is positive. DDx: persistent/recurrent HPV-associated lesion versus a positive test without current dysplasia. Plan: follow the dedicated post-treatment test-of-cure pathway, often colposcopy; do not apply routine 5-year recall.

Case 5 — Pregnancy with abnormal result

A pregnant 31-year-old is told she has abnormal cytology. Reasoning: pregnancy does not eliminate concern about significant disease, but the risk/benefit of procedures changes. Plan: specialist colposcopy when indicated, generally defer treatment of CIN until postpartum unless cancer is suspected; collaborate with obstetrics.

10. PLAB 2 station: communicate a positive HPV result

Scenario: A 32-year-old feels distressed because a screening letter reports high-risk HPV and she fears cancer and relationship infidelity.

  1. Introduce yourself, check identity, setting, privacy and what she understands.
  2. Explore fears and symptoms before giving a scripted reassurance.
  3. Explain HPV prevalence, persistence, the distinction between infection, abnormal cells and cancer.
  4. Explain her specific result and follow-up interval; do not invent a deadline.
  5. Discuss the next test or colposcopy, consent and information resources; ask for teach-back.
  6. Safety-net abnormal bleeding, unexplained discharge, pelvic pain and worsening symptoms, and confirm follow-up arrangements.

Differential Diagnosis: abnormal cervical screening and related symptoms

First decide whether you are explaining an asymptomatic screening abnormality or investigating abnormal bleeding, discharge or a visible cervical lesion. These are different clinical problems.

Red flags: cervix suspicious for cancer, recurrent unexplained postcoital bleeding, postmenopausal bleeding, persistent unexplained watery or blood-stained discharge, pelvic pain with systemic illness, pregnancy with pain/bleeding. Assess immediately according to severity; never delay symptomatic assessment for routine recall.

3. DDx algorithm — from first clue

  1. Asymptomatic + abnormal screening: confirm exact HPV/cytology result → prior history → programme referral/surveillance.
  2. Postcoital bleeding: pregnancy possibility → history/STI risk → speculum examination → test cervicitis when appropriate; visible suspicious lesion → urgent pathway.
  3. Postmenopausal bleeding: evaluate endometrial cancer risk in addition to cervical disease, even if recent cervical screen was negative.
  4. Pelvic pain, fever, purulent discharge: consider PID and ectopic pregnancy, prioritising time-sensitive assessment.

4. Exam traps

  • Cytology is not a histological diagnosis.
  • HPV positivity is not proof of cancer or recent transmission.
  • CIN3 is precancer, not established invasive carcinoma.
  • HPV-negative screening is not a reason to dismiss a suspicious cervix.
  • Do not treat an asymptomatic first positive HPV / normal cytology with antibiotics or immediate LLETZ.

Molecular mechanisms and decision science

Deep Dive — HPV carcinogenesis, cytology and risk-based management

1. Transformation zone anatomy

The endocervix contains columnar epithelium and the ectocervix squamous epithelium. Their dynamic junction is the transformation zone, particularly susceptible to persistent oncogenic HPV infection. Sampling and colposcopy target this region; age and hormonal state affect visibility.

2. Why only some HPV infections progress

Entry via microabrasions allows infection of basal epithelial cells. In productive infections, viral genome replication follows epithelial differentiation and may resolve without damage. Persistent expression of E6 and E7 interferes with p53 and pRb checkpoints. E7-driven pRb inactivation releases E2F transcriptional activity; E6-mediated p53 degradation reduces apoptosis and DNA-damage responses. A surrogate marker of transforming infection is diffuse p16 overexpression on histology in appropriate contexts.

3. Cytology versus histology

Abnormal cytology (‘dyskaryosis’) describes nuclear alterations in cells recovered from a sample; it does not establish the depth of epithelial involvement or invasion. Histology from biopsy distinguishes CIN1/2/3 by architectural atypia and determines whether malignant cells breach the basement membrane. Glandular disease is particularly challenging because lesions may occur within the endocervical canal beyond direct view.

4. Screening as Bayesian risk selection

Primary hrHPV testing has high sensitivity for HPV-driven precancer but limited specificity because transient infection is common. Cytology triage improves specificity by selecting people with cellular changes. Repeating HPV after 12 months tests for persistence, increasing post-test risk. This explains why a first HPV-positive/normal-cytology result is monitored, not treated.

5. Why symptomatic disease is different

A screening programme estimates future risk in people without symptoms. New postcoital bleeding or a visible suspicious lesion changes the pretest probability and needs targeted diagnostic assessment even if screening was negative. Screening can miss lesions because of sampling limitations, low viral signal, non-HPV causes or disease outside the targeted compartment.

6. Excisional treatment and future pregnancy

LLETZ removes the transformation zone and permits margin assessment. More extensive or repeated excisions correlate with increased preterm birth risk; explain the absolute and individualised risk with specialists. Management prioritises adequate disease treatment while avoiding overtreatment of transient low-risk changes.

7. PLAB integration

PLAB 1: interpret screening algorithm correctly, separate CIN from invasive disease, select colposcopy for hrHPV-positive abnormal cytology, and refer a suspicious cervix rather than repeating a smear. PLAB 2: explain HPV without implying infidelity, address distress and fertility questions, confirm follow-up and document consent and safety-netting.

Clinical bottom line: HPV test = infection risk; cytology = screening cell changes; colposcopy/biopsy = structural diagnosis; symptoms/signs = separate diagnostic pathway.
