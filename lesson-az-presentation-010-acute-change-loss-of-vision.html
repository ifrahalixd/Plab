Acute visual loss: protect sight before diagnosing every detail

Sudden loss of vision is an emergency until a sight-threatening or vascular cause has been excluded. A systematic approach starts by asking whether the change is monocular or binocular, painful or painless, transient or persistent, and whether there is an afferent pupillary defect, retinal abnormality or neurological deficit.

PLAB safety rule: Sudden monocular blindness can be a retinal stroke, retinal detachment or arteritic ischaemic optic neuropathy. Painful red eye with halos, nausea and a mid-dilated pupil suggests acute angle closure. A new homonymous field defect can be an occipital stroke. These need immediate emergency/stroke/eye pathways—not routine outpatient optometry.

1. First 60 seconds: triage

  1. ABCDE and time last known well: New neurological deficits, severe headache or suspected retinal arterial occlusion trigger emergency stroke assessment.
  2. Laterality: Ask patient to cover each eye separately. True monocular loss generally localises to globe, retina or optic nerve; binocular homonymous field loss points retrochiasmally and may be misreported as one eye.
  3. Pain and redness: Painful red eye, corneal opacity, photophobia or nausea suggests high-risk anterior segment pathology.
  4. Time course: Seconds (vascular); flashes/floaters then curtain (retinal tear/detachment); hours/days with eye movement pain (optic neuritis); brief episodic loss (amaurosis fugax).
  5. Risk modifiers: Age over 50 with new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication or shoulder stiffness raises suspicion of GCA; contact lenses, eye injury and ocular surgery raise other risks.

Immediate escalation: Avoid delays for tests when CRAO, GCA with visual symptoms, acute angle closure, penetrating trauma, retinal detachment or stroke is suspected. Never apply pressure to a possible open globe.

3. Bedside examination: visual acuity first

  1. Visual acuity for each eye with usual corrective lenses/pinhole if appropriate; record counting fingers, hand movements, perception of light or no perception of light if chart cannot be read.
  2. Visual fields to confrontation. A homonymous deficit suggests retrochiasmal pathology; bitemporal loss suggests chiasmal disease.
  3. Pupils and RAPD: Relative afferent pupillary defect suggests asymmetric retinal/optic nerve dysfunction, but can be absent in bilateral symmetric disease.
  4. Colour vision: Reduced red saturation can support optic nerve dysfunction.
  5. External eye and cornea: Eyelids, injection, corneal clarity, pupils, anterior chamber if trained; evaluate photophobia and ocular motility.
  6. Fundoscopy where trained: Disc oedema/pallor, retinal whitening, haemorrhages, detached retina; a normal initial fundus does not exclude retrobulbar optic neuritis or early ischaemia.
  7. Neurology and temporal arteries: Cranial nerves, limb signs, speech, balance; temporal tenderness and systemic inflammatory clues if GCA suspected.

Do not delay transfer for ophthalmoscopy, intraocular pressure measurement or visual acuity if the patient is unstable or has immediate stroke/sight-threatening features. Avoid tonometry with suspected globe rupture.

5. Must-not-miss diagnoses and discriminating features

Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO): Abrupt, profound, painless monocular loss; may have RAPD and retinal whitening/cherry-red spot later. It represents acute retinal ischaemia and warrants immediate emergency stroke-capable assessment. Evaluate embolic sources and vascular risk. Do not rely on a normal early fundoscopy.

Giant cell arteritis (GCA): Patients commonly over 50 with a new temporal headache, jaw claudication or polymyalgia symptoms; visual loss may be sudden and irreversible, and a normal ESR/CRP cannot reliably exclude it. When strongly suspected with visual symptoms, urgent specialist-led glucocorticoid treatment is initiated without waiting for biopsy/imaging.

Retinal detachment: Sudden flashes and new floaters followed by a descending shadow or curtain; painless. The macula may be threatened; emergency ophthalmology determines timing of surgery. Even without a curtain, acute flashes/floaters warrant prompt dilated retinal assessment.

Acute angle-closure glaucoma: Severe painful red eye, blurred vision/halos, headache, nausea or vomiting, hazy cornea and often a mid-dilated sluggish pupil. Urgent pressure-lowering treatment is specialist-directed; avoid prolonged diagnostic delay.

Optic neuritis: Subacute monocular visual impairment in a younger adult, often pain on eye movement, colour desaturation and RAPD; fundus may look normal. Refer for ophthalmic/neuro-ophthalmic assessment and MRI as clinically indicated.

Retinal vein occlusion (CRVO): Painless monocular blurred vision, typically in a vascular-risk patient; dilated fundus may show widespread retinal haemorrhages. Prompt ophthalmology assessment for macular oedema and neovascular complications.

Ocular and cerebral TIA / stroke: Transient monocular blackout (amaurosis fugax) can reflect carotid embolism and requires urgent TIA/stroke pathway. A homonymous hemianopia, speech change or limb deficit is a possible acute brain stroke.

Pituitary apoplexy: Sudden severe headache, visual field impairment, ophthalmoplegia and haemodynamic/endocrine instability. Escalate immediately for resuscitation, endocrine/neurosurgical review and urgent neuroimaging.

7. Management and safety-netting

  1. Escalate immediately to emergency ophthalmology, stroke or neurosurgery/endocrinology according to pattern and available local pathways.
  2. Document symptom onset/last-known-well, each-eye acuity, field findings, pupils, relevant observations and referrals.
  3. For suspected GCA with sight-threatening features, initiate urgent high-dose glucocorticoid management with senior/specialist input; do not wait for ESR, CRP or biopsy before treatment.
  4. For suspected retinal detachment, facilitate urgent dilated retinal examination and retina-service management; do not reassure based on painlessness.
  5. In painful red eye, avoid inappropriate steroid drops and self-treatment before assessment; contact lens use increases concern for microbial keratitis.
  6. Arrange stroke/TIA secondary-prevention assessment when retinal or cerebral ischaemia is suspected; choice of drugs depends on diagnosis and specialist pathway.
  7. Advise urgent return for worsening vision, new flashes/floaters/curtain, pain/redness, headache with jaw claudication or new neurological signs.

8. Five worked clinical presentations

Case 1 · Sudden painless blindness: A 73-year-old with AF loses vision in one eye while eating. Prioritise CRAO and immediate stroke-capable assessment. RAPD and retinal changes may support diagnosis, but their absence initially does not justify delay.
Case 2 · Curtain after flashes: A myopic patient reports a shower of floaters followed by a dark field curtain. Suspect retinal detachment; arrange emergency retinal specialist evaluation.
Case 3 · Red eye and vomiting: A 64-year-old has abrupt painful blurred vision, halos and nausea. Suspect acute angle closure and seek emergency pressure-lowering ophthalmic treatment.
Case 4 · Jaw pain with blurred vision: A 77-year-old has new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication and transient monocular loss. Suspect sight-threatening GCA and initiate emergency specialist-directed steroid pathway immediately.
Case 5 · Pain on eye movement: A 29-year-old has 48 hours of reduced vision and red desaturation in one eye. Optic neuritis is possible; assess pupillary function, exclude atypical causes and arrange specialist evaluation.

9. PLAB 2 OSCE framework

Opening: Confirm identity; ask permission; establish when vision changed and whether this affects one eye or both. Immediate safety: Ask about pain, eye injury, headache, neurological symptoms, flashes/floaters, jaw pain, visual field loss and contact lenses. Focused tests: Each-eye visual acuity, pupils, fields and red-eye inspection. Explain: “A sudden change in sight can sometimes mean reduced blood flow or a problem with the retina. We need urgent specialist assessment today to protect your vision.” Close: Summarise, organise direct transfer, document handover and check whether the patient has support and safe transport.

Exam pearl: The most important clinical decision is often who needs immediate referral, not which subtle ophthalmoscopic sign is present.

Pattern recognition

  1. Painless, sudden, monocular: CRAO/BRAO, retinal TIA, retinal detachment, vitreous haemorrhage, CRVO or ischaemic optic neuropathy.
  2. Painful, red: Acute angle closure, keratitis, uveitis or endophthalmitis.
  3. Eye-movement pain with colour loss: Optic neuritis is likely but requires context.
  4. Homonymous binocular field deficit: Consider acute brain stroke.
  5. Age over 50 plus headache/jaw claudication: Consider GCA even without dramatic ocular signs.

Deep Dive: physiology, localising lesions and time-critical mechanisms

1. Retina as neural tissue

The central retinal artery supplies inner retinal layers; acute interruption leads to ischaemic dysfunction within minutes. The outer retina has choroidal support, explaining characteristic selective whitening patterns in established CRAO. Retinal arterial occlusion is an acute neurovascular syndrome: systemic carotid and cardiac embolic risks must be addressed urgently.

2. Relative afferent pupillary defect

In the swinging-flashlight test, move a bright light alternately between eyes. Paradoxical dilation of both pupils when illuminating the affected eye implies reduced afferent retinal/optic nerve input. RAPD does not occur with a purely media-opacity lesion such as uncomplicated cataract; bilateral symmetric optic nerve damage may show no RAPD.

3. Why flashes and floaters matter

Vitreoretinal traction stimulates retinal photoreceptors, producing photopsias. Posterior vitreous detachment may be benign but can tear retina; fluid through the tear can separate neurosensory retina from retinal pigment epithelium. A shadow/curtain reflects expanding detached retina; macular involvement threatens central vision.

4. Optic nerve versus cortex

Optic nerve pathology commonly causes unilateral acuity or colour loss and RAPD. A lesion after the optic chiasm typically affects the same half of the visual field in both eyes (homonymous hemianopia); confrontation testing must be performed in each eye, and missing a visual field stroke can cause major morbidity.

5. GCA and arteritic optic neuropathy

Large/medium vessel granulomatous inflammation can occlude posterior ciliary arteries supplying the optic nerve head. Untreated disease risks rapidly sequential bilateral blindness; glucocorticoids are urgently initiated on strong clinical suspicion with visual symptoms while confirmation is arranged. ESR and CRP are supportive rather than infallible screening tests.

6. Pressure dynamics in angle closure

Block of aqueous drainage at the iridocorneal angle elevates intraocular pressure, causing corneal oedema, pain and optic nerve risk. Urgent medical lowering of pressure and definitive laser or procedural care are ophthalmology-directed; do not treat it like routine conjunctivitis.

7. Imaging and decision thresholds

OCT is useful for macular and retinal nerve fibre changes, but it does not replace stroke assessment in suspected retinal ischaemia. MRI of orbits/brain helps evaluate optic neuritis and differential demyelinating or compressive lesions. Investigations must never postpone immediate emergency transfer for a threatened visual pathway.

Clinical pitfalls

  • Do not falsely reassure a patient who had complete transient vision recovery: this may have been a retinal TIA.
  • Do not dismiss first-ever aura as migraine without appropriate evaluation.
  • Do not use a red eye as proof of conjunctivitis when acuity is reduced or pain is severe.
  • Do not defer GCA treatment while waiting for confirmatory ultrasound/biopsy in a sight-threatening presentation.
  • Do not miss the possibility of a homonymous field defect when a patient describes “one eye” being blind.
