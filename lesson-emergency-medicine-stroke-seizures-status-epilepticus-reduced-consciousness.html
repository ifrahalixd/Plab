Acute Neurological Emergencies: Stroke, Seizures, Status Epilepticus & Reduced Consciousness

Emergency rule: Sudden focal neurological deficit, seizure lasting ≥5 minutes, thunderclap headache or falling GCS requires immediate assessment and escalation.

Initial neurological ABCDE

Assess airway patency, breathing, oxygenation, pulse, blood pressure, GCS or AVPU, pupils, limb movement, temperature and capillary glucose. Ask for immediate help with airway compromise, repeated seizures, shock or rapidly falling consciousness. Do not delay resuscitation for imaging.

PLAB application: Recheck glucose and neurological observations after each intervention.

Stroke recognition and time last known well

Sudden facial weakness, arm weakness, speech disturbance, visual loss, ataxia or focal sensory change may represent stroke. Use FAST as a screening aid but remember posterior circulation stroke can present with vertigo, diplopia and imbalance. Establish last known well, not simply when symptoms were discovered.

PLAB application: Activate the local stroke pathway immediately, including for wake-up stroke.

Stroke mimics and hypoglycaemia

Hypoglycaemia, seizures with Todd paresis, migraine, functional neurological symptoms and intracranial masses can mimic stroke. Check bedside glucose immediately; treat hypoglycaemia without delaying specialist stroke assessment if deficits persist.

PLAB application: Do not dismiss persistent focal deficit after glucose correction.

Imaging and reperfusion decisions

Urgent non-contrast CT differentiates intracranial haemorrhage from many ischaemic presentations; CT angiography identifies large-vessel occlusion when indicated. Specialist teams decide eligibility for thrombolysis and thrombectomy using time, imaging and contraindications. Selected patients may be eligible beyond conventional windows using advanced imaging.

PLAB application: Never give antiplatelet or thrombolytic treatment before excluding haemorrhage when indicated.

Acute ischaemic stroke supportive care

Maintain airway and physiological stability, screen swallowing before oral intake, monitor glucose, temperature and rhythm, and prevent aspiration. Blood pressure management differs depending on reperfusion plans; avoid reflex rapid lowering without specialist direction. Aspirin follows confirmation of ischaemic stroke and the relevant treatment pathway.

PLAB application: Keep the patient nil by mouth until a safe swallow is established.

Intracerebral haemorrhage

Consider intracerebral haemorrhage with sudden focal deficit, headache, vomiting, severe hypertension or reduced consciousness, although clinical features overlap with ischaemic stroke. Obtain urgent brain imaging, neurosurgical/stroke advice and assess anticoagulant reversal. Manage blood pressure using specialist protocols.

PLAB application: Never assume headache proves haemorrhage or its absence excludes it.

Subarachnoid haemorrhage

A sudden thunderclap headache reaching peak intensity rapidly is a key warning sign; neck stiffness, collapse, vomiting or focal signs may occur. Urgent CT head and specialist-directed further investigation are required when suspicion persists despite negative initial imaging.

PLAB application: A normal examination does not rule out subarachnoid haemorrhage.

Transient ischaemic attack

TIA produces transient focal neurological symptoms without persisting acute infarction by tissue-based definition. Symptoms may have resolved by assessment, but early stroke risk remains important. Arrange urgent specialist TIA assessment and follow local antiplatelet pathway after checking contraindications.

PLAB application: Do not reassure and discharge without an urgent TIA plan.

Seizure recognition and immediate first aid

Protect from injury, note onset and semiology, time the event, do not restrain or put objects in the mouth. Position safely when feasible, maintain oxygenation, check glucose and obtain witness history. A focal or generalised seizure may be followed by temporary confusion or weakness.

PLAB application: Treat ongoing convulsive seizure at five minutes as status epilepticus.

Convulsive status epilepticus

Convulsive seizure lasting five minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery is a time-critical emergency. Call for help, use ABCDE, check glucose, give first-line benzodiazepine according to the current local protocol, then escalate to second-line IV antiseizure treatment and critical care if ongoing. Monitor respiration after sedatives.

PLAB application: Do not repeatedly administer benzodiazepines without following the escalation algorithm.

First seizure evaluation

After stabilisation ask about preceding aura, syncope symptoms, drugs, alcohol, sleep deprivation, head injury, fever and pregnancy possibility. Check ECG and relevant blood tests; assess need for urgent imaging, infection work-up and specialist follow-up. Explain driving restrictions and safety precautions using current DVLA guidance.

PLAB application: A single seizure does not automatically establish epilepsy.

Reduced consciousness differential

Use a structured approach: hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hypercapnia, stroke, seizures/non-convulsive status, intoxication, sepsis, metabolic derangement, trauma and intracranial bleeding. Review medications and consider opioid toxicity with respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils.

PLAB application: GCS decline and airway risk require urgent senior/critical-care assessment.

Raised intracranial pressure and herniation

Worsening headache, vomiting, decreasing GCS, new pupillary asymmetry, abnormal posturing or Cushing physiology are concerning. Escalate urgently, avoid hypoxia and hypotension, and obtain emergency imaging/neurosurgical input. Do not perform lumbar puncture when there are signs suggesting dangerous mass effect.

PLAB application: Unilateral fixed dilated pupil with deterioration is a neurosurgical emergency.

Meningitis and encephalitis

Fever, headache, photophobia, neck stiffness, altered behaviour, seizures or reduced consciousness raise concern. Signs may be absent in older or immunocompromised patients. Obtain cultures promptly if feasible, but do not delay urgent antimicrobials for lumbar puncture or imaging in an unstable patient. Suspected encephalitis may require IV aciclovir.

PLAB application: Escalate possible bacterial meningitis or encephalitis immediately.

Neurological examination and handover

Document exact GCS components, pupils, cranial nerves, limb power, speech, sensation, coordination and relevant observations. Record onset and progression, anticoagulants, glucose, imaging and treatments. Use SBAR for rapid stroke, seizure or neurosurgical handover.

PLAB application: Serial examination is more useful than one isolated GCS score.

PLAB 2 communication and safety-netting

Explain suspected stroke or seizure without prematurely asserting a diagnosis. Ask permission to involve family, acknowledge uncertainty, describe urgent scans or medicines and avoid false reassurance. Discuss emergency return symptoms: recurrent focal weakness, new speech disturbance, prolonged seizure, severe headache or declining alertness.

PLAB application: Use clear language and involve the multidisciplinary stroke or neurology team.

PLAB 2 — Acute neurological assessment

Introduce yourself, call for help, assess ABCDE, check glucose and time of symptom onset. State your differential, urgent investigations, immediate safety measures and SBAR escalation. Communicate calmly with the patient and relatives.

Deep Dive — Neurological emergencies

FAST and beyond

FAST is useful but misses some posterior circulation presentations. Ask about sudden diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, gait ataxia and visual field symptoms. Time last known well precisely.

Stroke therapies are imaging-led

Thrombolysis and mechanical thrombectomy are specialist decisions, with imaging and eligibility criteria. Do not present one universal time window as applying to every patient.

Status epilepticus escalation

Time the seizure. Support airway, breathing and circulation, check glucose, use protocol-guided first-line benzodiazepine and second-line therapy, and prepare critical care support if seizures persist.

The unconscious patient

A low or falling GCS demands airway evaluation. Check glucose, blood gas, electrolytes, ECG, toxicology history and urgent neuroimaging where indicated. Consider non-convulsive status if persistent unexplained unresponsiveness.

LP safety and infection

Lumbar puncture is not a prerequisite for antibiotics in a critically unwell patient with suspected meningitis. Assess contraindications and follow local specialist guidance.

Counselling and follow-up

After a first seizure, explain temporary safety measures and driving restrictions using current DVLA rules. After TIA, emphasise urgent specialist review even if symptoms have resolved.

UK clinical references

Use current NICE, stroke-network, emergency and local prescribing protocols. Eligibility for reperfusion and seizure medication doses requires patient-specific specialist guidance.
