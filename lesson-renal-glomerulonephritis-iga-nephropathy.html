Glomerulonephritis & IgA Nephropathy

PLAB focus: Recognise nephritic presentations, distinguish IgA nephropathy from post-infectious GN, and urgently escalate rapidly progressive or pulmonary–renal disease.

01. What is glomerulonephritis?

Glomerulonephritis (GN) is inflammation or immune injury of the glomeruli. Presentation ranges from microscopic haematuria to nephritic syndrome, nephrotic-range proteinuria or rapidly progressive renal failure.

PLAB application: Recognise a renal inflammatory pattern rather than diagnosing from haematuria alone.

02. Clinical presentation

Ask about cola-coloured urine, frothy urine, oliguria, oedema, hypertension, fatigue and flank discomfort. Examine blood pressure, volume status, skin, joints, lungs and urine output.

PLAB application: Haematuria plus proteinuria, hypertension or falling eGFR warrants renal assessment.

03. IgA nephropathy

IgA nephropathy involves mesangial IgA-dominant immune deposits. Episodes of visible haematuria can occur during or within days of an upper respiratory infection (synpharyngitic haematuria); persistent microscopic haematuria and proteinuria are also common.

PLAB application: Haematuria during a sore throat suggests IgA nephropathy, but is not diagnostic.

04. IgA nephropathy versus post-streptococcal GN

Post-infectious GN classically occurs after a latent period following throat or skin infection, often with low C3. IgA nephropathy often causes haematuria close to infection onset and typically has normal complement. Neither timing nor complement alone proves diagnosis.

PLAB application: Distinguish synpharyngitic haematuria from delayed post-infectious nephritis.

05. Urinalysis and microscopy

Check dipstick blood and protein, urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR) or protein:creatinine ratio (PCR), and urine microscopy where available. Dysmorphic red cells and red-cell casts suggest glomerular bleeding.

PLAB application: Red-cell casts strongly suggest glomerular inflammation.

06. Initial blood tests

Request creatinine/eGFR, urea, potassium, bicarbonate, FBC, albumin and inflammatory markers when indicated. Compare with baseline and repeat to identify acute deterioration.

PLAB application: A normal creatinine does not rule out early glomerular disease.

07. Immunological and infection tests

Depending on presentation, consider C3/C4, ANA and anti-dsDNA, ANCA, anti-GBM antibodies, hepatitis B/C and other infection studies. These tests guide differential diagnosis, not replace clinical judgement.

PLAB application: Suspected pulmonary–renal syndrome needs urgent ANCA/anti-GBM testing and specialist input.

08. Kidney biopsy

A renal biopsy can confirm IgA nephropathy and distinguish other GN patterns, assess chronic scarring and guide treatment. Renal specialists weigh proteinuria, kidney function, progression and bleeding risk.

PLAB application: IgA nephropathy requires biopsy confirmation; do not diagnose definitively from timing alone.

09. Risk assessment in IgA nephropathy

Long-term risk relates to persistent proteinuria, reduced eGFR, hypertension and histological changes. Serial ACR/PCR, eGFR and BP matter more than a single urine dipstick.

PLAB application: Persistent proteinuria is a key marker of progression risk.

10. Supportive treatment

Specialist-guided care prioritises blood-pressure control, renin–angiotensin system blockade when indicated, proteinuria reduction, salt moderation and cardiovascular risk management. Monitor potassium and renal function after ACE inhibitor/ARB changes.

PLAB application: Supportive kidney-protective treatment is central to IgA nephropathy care.

11. Immunosuppression

Steroids or other disease-specific agents are not routine for everyone with IgA nephropathy; specialist selection depends on risk, biopsy, comorbidities and evolving guidance. Active infection and treatment toxicity require careful consideration.

PLAB application: Do not automatically prescribe high-dose steroids for every case of IgA nephropathy.

12. Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis

Rapid decline in kidney function over days to weeks, often with active urinary sediment, suggests crescentic GN. Causes include ANCA-associated vasculitis, anti-GBM disease and immune-complex GN.

PLAB application: Rapid creatinine rise with blood/protein in urine requires same-day urgent specialist discussion.

13. Pulmonary–renal syndrome

Haemoptysis, hypoxia or diffuse alveolar haemorrhage with GN may indicate anti-GBM disease or ANCA vasculitis. Assess ABCDE, oxygenation, FBC, renal function and urgent specialty escalation.

PLAB application: Haemoptysis plus AKI is a medical emergency.

14. Other key glomerular diseases

Consider lupus nephritis (systemic features, ANA/dsDNA, low complement), infection-related GN, membranoproliferative patterns and IgA vasculitis (palpable purpura, abdominal pain, arthralgia and kidney involvement).

PLAB application: Palpable purpura with haematuria raises concern for IgA vasculitis.

15. Monitoring and complications

Track BP, urine protein, eGFR, potassium and fluid balance. Complications include CKD, AKI, fluid overload and hyperkalaemia. Assess nephrotoxic medications and provide appropriate sick-day advice.

PLAB application: Hyperkalaemia, pulmonary oedema and severe hypertension need emergency treatment.

16. PLAB 2: explanation and safety-netting

Explain that the kidney filters may be inflamed, causing blood and protein in urine. Discuss repeat tests, possible biopsy and nephrology referral. Advise urgent help for reduced urine, breathlessness, coughing blood, severe headache or rapid swelling.

PLAB application: Use clear language, check understanding and arrange explicit follow-up.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Mesangial IgA deposits

Aberrantly glycosylated IgA1 and immune complexes can deposit in the mesangium, activating inflammatory pathways. Clinical severity varies widely.

Haematuria timing

IgA-related visible haematuria can coincide with mucosal infection, while classic post-streptococcal GN tends to follow a latent period. Neither feature is definitive.

Histology and risk

Renal biopsy assesses IgA deposition, mesangial/endocapillary activity, segmental sclerosis and tubulointerstitial scarring; specialists may use the Oxford MEST-C score.

Complement and serology

Low complement suggests selected immune-complex processes. ANCA and anti-GBM tests help assess potentially rapidly progressive causes; results must be interpreted clinically.

Why proteinuria matters

Proteinuria reflects filtration barrier injury and is linked with future renal decline; reducing persistent proteinuria is a major therapeutic goal.

PLAB 2 OSCE communication

Ask about urinary symptoms, infections, systemic features and medicines; examine BP and oedema; explain possible kidney-filter inflammation, follow-up tests, biopsy and emergency red flags.
