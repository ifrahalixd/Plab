Lymphoma: Hodgkin & Non-Hodgkin

Red flags: Stridor, rapidly progressive swelling, new limb weakness, SVC obstruction or sepsis require emergency assessment.

Definition and classification

Lymphomas are malignancies of lymphocytes. Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) differ in pathology and clinical behaviour. NHL includes many B-cell and T-cell subtypes.

PLAB application: Avoid treating all lymphomas as one disease.

Presenting symptoms

Painless persistent lymphadenopathy is common. Some patients report fatigue, itching, fevers, night sweats, unexplained weight loss or compressive symptoms.

PLAB application: Ask duration, growth, sites and associated symptoms.

B symptoms

Classical B symptoms are unexplained fever above 38°C, drenching night sweats and unexplained weight loss exceeding 10% over six months.

PLAB application: Document B symptoms precisely; they affect staging descriptors.

Hodgkin lymphoma

Classical HL is characterised by Reed–Sternberg cells in an appropriate inflammatory background. It often presents with cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy and may spread contiguously.

PLAB application: Histology is required for diagnosis.

Hodgkin histology

Classical HL Reed–Sternberg cells are commonly CD15+ and CD30+. Nodular lymphocyte-predominant B-cell lymphoma is biologically distinct and classification has evolved.

PLAB application: Recognise the classic PLAB association without overgeneralising.

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma

NHL includes indolent and aggressive diseases. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is a common aggressive subtype; follicular lymphoma is commonly indolent.

PLAB application: Rapid growth or systemic illness warrants urgent assessment.

Burkitt lymphoma

Burkitt lymphoma is highly proliferative, associated with MYC rearrangement, and may present with abdominal or extranodal disease.

PLAB application: Recognise tumour lysis risk in rapidly proliferating lymphoma.

Extranodal disease

NHL may involve gastrointestinal tract, skin, central nervous system, bone marrow and other organs. Symptoms depend on the involved site.

PLAB application: Ask about neurological, abdominal and respiratory symptoms.

Risk factors

Risk associations include immunosuppression, HIV, Epstein–Barr virus for some subtypes, Helicobacter pylori in gastric MALT lymphoma and certain autoimmune diseases.

PLAB application: Associations do not establish diagnosis.

Examination

Inspect and palpate cervical, supraclavicular, axillary and inguinal nodes; assess liver and spleen, general appearance, airway and neurological status.

PLAB application: Describe size, consistency, mobility and tenderness.

Initial investigations

FBC and blood film, LDH, renal/liver function, inflammatory markers and selected infection tests can support assessment but cannot confirm lymphoma.

PLAB application: Normal blood tests do not rule lymphoma out.

Tissue diagnosis

An excisional lymph node biopsy is often preferred where feasible because architecture matters; image-guided core biopsy may be appropriate depending on site and urgency.

PLAB application: Fine-needle aspiration alone is usually insufficient to fully classify lymphoma.

Staging

PET-CT is often used for FDG-avid lymphomas; CT and bone marrow assessment are selected by subtype. Ann Arbor/Lugano staging considers distribution and extranodal involvement.

PLAB application: Do not confuse stage with histological grade.

Treatment principles

Management depends on subtype, stage, fitness and patient preference. Options include observation for selected indolent disease, immunochemotherapy, radiotherapy and newer targeted/cellular therapies.

PLAB application: Refer to haematology; avoid promising one universal regimen.

Urgent complications

Airway compromise, superior vena cava obstruction, spinal cord compression, tumour lysis syndrome, severe cytopenias and neutropenic sepsis are emergencies.

PLAB application: Assess ABCDE and escalate immediately when unstable.

PLAB 2 communication

Explain that a persistent lump requires investigation, discuss urgent referral without declaring cancer before biopsy, address worries and arrange safety-netting.

PLAB application: Be clear, empathetic and specific about follow-up.

PLAB 2: explaining a suspicious lymph node

Introduce yourself, obtain consent and explore the lump and systemic symptoms. Examine lymph node groups, abdomen and signs of airway compromise. Explain why blood tests, imaging and a biopsy may be needed, arrange appropriate referral and provide clear safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Reed–Sternberg biology

Classical Hodgkin lymphoma has a minority of malignant cells within a reactive inflammatory background.

Indolent versus aggressive

Histological subtype and proliferation rate affect tempo and treatment; indolent does not mean harmless.

Lymph node architecture

Excision can reveal follicular, diffuse or other patterns important for classification.

PET-CT interpretation

FDG uptake helps stage many lymphomas but inflammatory disease can also be avid.

Tumour lysis syndrome

Rapid cell breakdown releases potassium, phosphate and nucleic acids, potentially causing arrhythmias and acute kidney injury.

Clinical communication

A suspicious lump merits timely assessment, but lymphoma should not be diagnosed without tissue confirmation.
