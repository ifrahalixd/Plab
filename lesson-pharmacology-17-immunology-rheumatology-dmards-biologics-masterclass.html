Deep Dive · Molecular and clinical immunopharmacology

Synovial inflammation

Innate and adaptive immune cells amplify inflammation through TNF, IL-6, IL-1, chemokines and matrix metalloproteinases. Osteoclast activation drives erosions. Conventional and targeted DMARDs interrupt different nodes in this network; clinical response is delayed because synovial immune activity and tissue changes resolve gradually.

Why methotrexate works at low doses

At anti-inflammatory doses, methotrexate increases extracellular adenosine signalling, which suppresses immune-cell activation; folate-related enzyme effects contribute to toxicity. Renal elimination and drug interactions explain why acute kidney injury or trimethoprim can cause sudden marrow suppression. Folic acid is protective but cannot rescue a major dosing error; folinic acid rescue is specialist-directed.

Biologic targets and host defence

TNF is important in granuloma maintenance, explaining latent TB reactivation risk with TNF inhibition. IL-6 drives hepatic CRP synthesis, explaining why IL-6 inhibitors can mask inflammatory markers. CD20 depletion reduces B cells and can reactivate hepatitis B. JAK inhibitors block multiple cytokine pathways and have broader infection and thrombotic risk considerations.

JAK–STAT signal transduction

Cytokine binding activates receptor-associated Janus kinases, which phosphorylate STAT proteins. STAT dimers enter the nucleus to regulate transcription. JAK inhibition can reduce inflammation but also affect antiviral and haematopoietic signalling, explaining shingles, cytopenias and infection risk.

Steroid receptor physiology

Glucocorticoid receptors alter transcription of inflammatory mediators. Exogenous steroids suppress hypothalamic CRH and pituitary ACTH, causing adrenal atrophy with sufficient exposure. Acute illness can therefore precipitate adrenal crisis after abrupt withdrawal or inadequate stress dosing.

Case-based prescribing calculations

Always verify whether a prescribed methotrexate quantity is for weekly rather than daily use. Example: four 2.5 mg tablets taken once on Monday equals 10 mg per week, not 10 mg daily. Tablet strengths and dosing schedules must be checked against the prescription and patient-held record; never infer a dose from this example.

PLAB OSCE approach

Clarify diagnosis and indication; screen infection and pregnancy; explain benefits, delay to effect, baseline and ongoing monitoring; review vaccinations, interactions and emergency warning signs. Arrange shared-care communication and document informed consent.

1. Clinical framework and inflammatory disease

Distinguish inflammatory arthritis from mechanical pain, crystal disease and septic arthritis. In rheumatoid arthritis (RA), persistent synovitis causes pannus formation, cartilage injury and bone erosion. Early specialist assessment and disease-modifying treatment limit irreversible damage. Check infection, pregnancy potential, vaccination status, comorbidities, current drugs and baseline blood tests before immunosuppression.

2. Immune pathways and therapeutic targets

Antigen-presenting cells activate T lymphocytes through T-cell receptors and co-stimulation. Activated T and B cells, macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes produce TNF, IL-6, IL-1 and other mediators. JAK–STAT signalling transmits several cytokine receptor signals. B cells make autoantibodies and present antigens. Drug targets include folate metabolism, purine synthesis, cytokines, co-stimulation, B cells and intracellular kinases.

3. Symptom relief versus disease modification

NSAIDs relieve pain and stiffness but do not prevent structural progression of RA. Glucocorticoids rapidly reduce inflammation and may be used as short-term bridging treatment or for specific flares; minimise cumulative exposure. Conventional synthetic DMARDs such as methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine and leflunomide are intended to control disease activity over weeks to months. Do not delay specialist treatment while repeatedly prescribing steroids.

4. Methotrexate: mechanism and weekly dosing

Low-dose methotrexate is a first-line anchor DMARD for many patients with RA. Its anti-inflammatory action involves adenosine signalling and folate-related pathways. In inflammatory disease it is prescribed ONCE WEEKLY, never daily. Confirm the exact weekday, strength, folic acid schedule and monitoring plan; errors can cause fatal marrow suppression and mucositis. Methotrexate is contraindicated in pregnancy and requires specialist reproductive counselling.

PLAB clinical reasoning: Confirm the indication, look for a time-critical contraindication, check drug interactions and monitoring, then document counselling and safety-netting.

5. Methotrexate monitoring, interactions and toxicity

Baseline and ongoing full blood count, liver function and renal function are required under local/shared-care protocols. Monitor for mouth ulcers, fever, bruising, dyspnoea and cough. Renal impairment increases exposure. Trimethoprim and co-trimoxazole can markedly increase marrow toxicity and should generally be avoided. Significant pulmonary symptoms require urgent assessment for infection or methotrexate-associated pneumonitis. Folic acid reduces some adverse effects but does not eliminate serious toxicity.

6. Sulfasalazine

Sulfasalazine is used in RA and selected other inflammatory disorders. Adverse effects include gastrointestinal upset, rash, reversible oligospermia, hepatotoxicity and blood dyscrasias. Check FBC and liver tests as specified by local protocols. Ask about sulfonamide/salicylate hypersensitivity and G6PD deficiency where relevant. Benefits take weeks; communicate monitoring and warning symptoms.

7. Hydroxychloroquine

Hydroxychloroquine modifies endosomal antigen processing and toll-like receptor signalling and is used in RA and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). It can cause gastrointestinal upset, skin changes, QT-related interactions and, importantly, retinal toxicity with long-term exposure. Arrange ophthalmic monitoring according to current UK Royal College of Ophthalmologists guidance and risk factors, including renal impairment and tamoxifen co-use. Dosing should account for actual body weight and specialist guidance.

8. Leflunomide

Leflunomide inhibits dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, reducing de novo pyrimidine synthesis in proliferating lymphocytes. Monitor FBC, liver function and blood pressure. It is teratogenic and persists because of enterohepatic recirculation; a specialist-directed cholestyramine washout may be required before conception or after serious toxicity. Do not assume stopping tablets rapidly eliminates fetal exposure.

9. Glucocorticoids and the HPA axis

Glucocorticoids bind intracellular receptors and alter inflammatory gene transcription, suppressing cytokine expression and immune-cell recruitment. Benefits are rapid, but prolonged systemic treatment can cause infection, hyperglycaemia, osteoporosis, hypertension, mood changes, cataracts and adrenal suppression. Risk of adrenal insufficiency depends on dose, duration, timing and route. Do not abruptly stop prolonged treatment; provide sick-day and steroid-emergency guidance where indicated.

10. TNF inhibitors

Adalimumab, etanercept and infliximab inhibit tumour necrosis factor signalling, improving disease activity and limiting joint damage in eligible patients. Screen for latent/active tuberculosis and hepatitis B, assess infection risk and review vaccinations before initiation. Serious infection can occur; withhold and urgently assess when a serious infection is suspected according to specialist guidance. Avoid live vaccines during significant immunosuppression unless specialist advice supports them.

11. IL-6 inhibitors and laboratory interpretation

Tocilizumab and sarilumab block IL-6 signalling. Adverse effects include infections, neutropenia, transaminitis and dyslipidaemia; gastrointestinal perforation is a rare concern, particularly with diverticular disease. IL-6 blockade may blunt fever and CRP response, so serious infection may occur despite modest inflammatory markers. Monitor according to the agent-specific specialist protocol.

12. B-cell depletion and co-stimulation blockade

Rituximab targets CD20-positive B cells and is used for selected refractory inflammatory conditions; infusion reactions, infection and hepatitis B reactivation are important concerns. Abatacept modulates T-cell co-stimulation by binding CD80/86. Screen for infection and viral hepatitis where indicated, review immunisations and follow specialist infusion and monitoring protocols.

13. JAK inhibitors and targeted synthetic DMARDs

Tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and other JAK inhibitors block intracellular cytokine signalling. They can increase risk of serious infection, herpes zoster, cytopenias and lipid changes. Regulatory warnings include major adverse cardiovascular events, malignancy, venous thromboembolism and mortality in certain populations; carefully assess age, smoking, cardiovascular and cancer risk and use current MHRA/BNF restrictions. Baseline screening and monitoring are mandatory.

14. Vaccination and infection prevention

Before immunosuppression, review routine immunisations and consider influenza, pneumococcal, hepatitis B and shingles recommendations according to patient risk and national guidance. Timing matters for rituximab and other therapies because immune responses can be reduced. Live vaccines are generally avoided during substantial immunosuppression; decisions depend on the agent, dose and patient. Fever, new cough, dysuria or painful rash warrants prompt clinical assessment.

15. Pregnancy, fertility and breastfeeding

Methotrexate and leflunomide are teratogenic and require specialist contraception and preconception plans. Hydroxychloroquine and sulfasalazine are often continued in pregnancy when clinically appropriate, with medicine-specific monitoring and folate advice. Some biologics have trimester-specific considerations; use specialist rheumatology–obstetric guidance. Do not stop essential lupus treatment without assessing maternal and fetal risks.

16. Systemic lupus erythematosus pharmacology

Hydroxychloroquine is a foundational therapy for many patients with SLE, alongside sun protection and organ-specific management. Corticosteroids and immunosuppressants such as mycophenolate, azathioprine or cyclophosphamide may be needed for organ-threatening disease. Mycophenolate and cyclophosphamide have major reproductive and toxicity risks. Lupus nephritis requires coordinated renal/rheumatology assessment, proteinuria monitoring and kidney-protective care.

17. Gout and urate-lowering medicines

Acute gout can be treated with NSAIDs, colchicine or corticosteroids according to contraindications and comorbidities. Colchicine toxicity is more likely in renal impairment and with strong CYP3A4/P-glycoprotein inhibitors such as clarithromycin. Allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase and lowers urate; initiate low and titrate to serum urate targets with flare prophylaxis when indicated. HLA-B*58:01 and severe cutaneous reactions are relevant in higher-risk ancestry groups. Do not confuse flare relief with long-term urate reduction.

18. Other immunosuppressants and transplant principles

Azathioprine impairs purine synthesis and may cause myelosuppression and hepatotoxicity; TPMT/NUDT15 status can influence toxicity risk. Allopurinol or febuxostat markedly interacts with azathioprine and requires specialist management. Ciclosporin and tacrolimus inhibit calcineurin, reducing T-cell activation; they can cause nephrotoxicity, hypertension and significant interactions. Transplant regimens are specialist-led and should not be altered casually.

19. Monitoring, shared care and medication reconciliation

Before a DMARD, document indication, screening, pregnancy plans, baseline FBC/LFT/U&E and vaccinations. During treatment, follow drug-specific shared-care schedules, review infection and adverse-effect symptoms, and ensure results are acted upon. A missed monitoring appointment is a safety issue. Check prescriptions for duplicate immunosuppression, interacting antimicrobials, recent steroid courses and changes in kidney function.

20. PLAB emergencies and patient counselling

A hot swollen joint with fever is septic arthritis until excluded: urgent assessment and joint aspiration where appropriate, not empiric escalation of immunosuppression. Methotrexate taken daily by mistake with oral ulcers and bruising is an emergency requiring withholding the drug and urgent FBC/renal assessment. A patient on a biologic with systemic illness needs infection evaluation even without a marked fever. Explain treatment goals, delayed DMARD effect, monitoring, pregnancy precautions and when to seek urgent help.

Worked clinical cases

Case 1: Methotrexate dosing error

A 65-year-old with RA has taken methotrexate 15 mg every day for four days instead of weekly. They have mouth ulcers and bruising. This is potentially life-threatening toxicity: withhold methotrexate, arrange urgent emergency assessment, FBC, kidney and liver tests, medication reconciliation and specialist/toxicology input. Do not simply reassure or wait for the next routine blood test.

Case 2: Fever on adalimumab

A patient on a TNF inhibitor presents with cough, weight loss and night sweats. Assess for TB and other serious infections urgently. Withhold the biologic pending specialist assessment as appropriate; do not label this an RA flare without infection evaluation.

Case 3: Low CRP on tocilizumab

A patient taking an IL-6 inhibitor has abdominal pain, hypotension and only mildly raised CRP. IL-6 blockade can suppress the acute-phase response. Escalate for possible sepsis or perforation despite the apparently reassuring CRP.

Case 4: Planning pregnancy on leflunomide

A patient taking leflunomide wishes to conceive. Explain teratogenic risk and long persistence, coordinate rheumatology and pregnancy planning, and consider a verified specialist washout and confirmation protocol before conception.

PLAB 2 counselling station

Explain the diagnosis and aim of preventing joint damage; confirm the exact weekly methotrexate day, folic acid instructions and blood-test schedule. Ask about alcohol, infections, pregnancy plans and interacting prescriptions. Safety-net for fever, mouth ulcers, unexplained bruising, new cough or breathlessness. Use teach-back: ask the patient to repeat when and how they take the medicine.

    NICE / BNF · Immunosuppression safety pathways

    Educational pathways: confirm current BNF, NICE, MHRA and local specialist protocols before prescribing.

    Before a DMARD

    Confirm inflammatory diagnosis → assess infection, pregnancy, vaccines and interactions → baseline FBC, LFT, renal function ± disease-specific screening → document dose and monitoring → counsel warning symptoms → arrange specialist/shared-care follow-up.

    Fever or serious illness during immunosuppression

    ABCDE and sepsis assessment → seek urgent medical evaluation and cultures as appropriate → check medicine-specific instructions about withholding therapy → assess for opportunistic infection → coordinate rheumatology/infectious disease follow-up.

    Daily methotrexate taken in error

    Withhold methotrexate → urgent hospital assessment → FBC, renal and liver function, symptom review → specialist toxicology/haematology advice including rescue therapy when indicated → incident reporting and safe weekly regimen reconciliation.

    Before TNF, IL-6, B-cell or JAK treatment

    Review prior DMARD response → TB/hepatitis screening and vaccinations → assess cancer, cardiovascular and VTE risks (especially JAK inhibitors) → baseline tests → specialist selection and informed consent → ongoing infection and laboratory monitoring.
