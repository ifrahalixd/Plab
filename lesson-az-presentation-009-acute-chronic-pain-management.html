Acute and chronic pain: assess, classify, treat, review

Pain is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with or resembling that associated with actual or potential tissue damage. Effective management begins with a diagnosis, compassionate validation, assessment of risk and impact, and shared goals—not merely a numerical score or a prescription.

1. Recognise pain mechanisms and time course

Acute pain generally accompanies recent injury or illness and can be protective; persistent pain may reflect healing complications, neuropathy or altered pain processing. Chronic pain usually lasts or recurs beyond three months. Chronic primary pain is characterised by significant emotional distress or functional disability and is not better accounted for by another diagnosis; chronic secondary pain is related to an underlying condition such as osteoarthritis, inflammatory disease or malignancy. A person may have both.

2. History and examination: SOCRATES plus function

  1. Symptom: Site, onset, character, radiation, associated symptoms, timing, exacerbators and severity; ask what is different today. Assess pain at rest and on movement.
  2. Causes and context: Trauma, infection, inflammation, cancer, surgery, diabetes, vascular risks, medications and substance use; prior interventions and their benefits or harms.
  3. Red flags: New progressive weakness, bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle symptoms, fever with painful joint or spine, sudden severe headache, chest pain, acute ischaemia or malignancy red flags.
  4. Impact: Walking, work, activities, sleep, appetite, relationships and goals; use a 0–10 scale as a communication tool rather than the sole marker of progress.
  5. Psychosocial: Mood, anxiety, trauma, beliefs, fear of movement, social support and risk of self-harm when appropriate; use nonjudgemental language.
  6. Examination: Observations and focused examination of affected system, neurological power/sensation/reflexes where relevant, pulses, joints, gait and functional limitations. Document baseline and re-examine when deterioration occurs.

Unnecessary investigations may amplify distress; however new or changing symptoms deserve appropriate investigation even in someone with a longstanding chronic pain diagnosis.

3. Dangerous causes that cannot wait

Urgent escalation: Suspected ACS, aortic catastrophe, acute limb ischaemia, compartment syndrome, septic arthritis, spinal infection or cord compression, cauda equina syndrome, meningitis, thunderclap headache, acute abdomen or major trauma. Treat physiology and symptoms while investigating. A high pain score alone is nonspecific, and a modest score never rules out a severe diagnosis.

In a patient with new back pain plus urinary retention, saddle sensory change or progressive bilateral deficits, arrange emergency spinal assessment and imaging per local protocol. In an acutely hot swollen joint, urgent aspiration and infection workup are needed; antibiotics should not be delayed for a severely unwell patient. In suspected sickle-cell crisis, follow the appropriate urgent analgesic and complication pathway.

Prescribing doses must be checked in the current BNF and local protocols for age, renal/hepatic function and setting.

5. Chronic pain: primary versus secondary changes the plan

NICE NG193 recommends person-centred assessment and a care plan addressing priorities and uncertainty. For chronic primary pain, offer a supervised group exercise programme and consider ACT/CBT for pain and selected acupuncture/dry needling. Consider discussing an antidepressant in adults after balancing harms and benefits; this can be for pain, sleep and wellbeing even without depression. Do not initiate opioids, NSAIDs, paracetamol, gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, antipsychotics, corticosteroids or other specified agents solely to manage chronic primary pain. If someone is already taking such medicines, review through shared decision-making; do not stop them abruptly.

Chronic secondary pain: Manage the diagnosed cause and use its guideline. For osteoarthritis, NICE NG226 prioritises therapeutic exercise and weight management when relevant, with topical NSAIDs for knee OA if appropriate. For low back pain, NICE NG59 supports exercise and condition-specific restrictions on medicine use. For inflammatory arthritis, treat active inflammation rather than merely escalating analgesics. Cancer-related and palliative pain need individualised specialist pathways—do not misapply NG193's chronic-primary prescribing restrictions.

6. Neuropathic pain: recognise the pattern

Neuropathic pain may cause burning, shooting pain, painful touch (allodynia), reduced sensation or pins and needles. Evaluate distribution (dermatomal, stocking-glove, nerve territory), motor changes and reversible causes. NICE CG173 recommends offering a choice of amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin as initial pharmacological treatment for most neuropathic pain outside trigeminal neuralgia; switch if ineffective or poorly tolerated and review function, adverse effects and adherence. Trigeminal neuralgia has a separate first-line carbamazepine recommendation and needs appropriate safety monitoring. Gabapentinoids can cause sedation and dependence/withdrawal; renal dosing and drug combinations matter. Neuropathic pain treatments do not automatically apply to nonspecific chronic primary pain.

7. Prescribing, dependence and safety

  • Opioid toxicity: respiratory depression with reduced consciousness and sometimes pinpoint pupils; support airway/ventilation, emergency response and titrated naloxone per local protocol when indicated; monitor for recurrence.
  • NSAIDs: avoid or use caution in peptic ulcer/bleeding, CKD, dehydration, heart failure and interacting anticoagulants; discuss gastrointestinal protection when appropriate.
  • Paracetamol: overdose can cause delayed severe liver injury; use toxicology assessment and treatment pathway rather than waiting for symptoms.
  • Dependence-forming drugs: assess benefit, harm and dependence/withdrawal risk; agree a gradual personalised reduction where indicated, consistent with NICE NG215.
  • Older adults/frailty: monitor delirium, falls, constipation and renal function; in pregnancy and breastfeeding seek specific prescribing advice.
  • Follow-up: document diagnosis, treatment trial, measurable goals and planned review; pain scores and medicine dose are not the only outcomes.

8. Five worked clinical cases

Case 1 — Fracture pain: A 76-year-old after a fall has hip pain and cannot mobilise. Examine neurovascular status, assess possible occult hip fracture, treat pain promptly and consider a specialist fascia iliaca block. Reassess for opioid-related delirium and respiratory depression.
Case 2 — Fibromyalgia: A 32-year-old has widespread pain for 8 months, fatigue and sleep disturbance; examination shows no focal neurological deficit. Evaluate for alternative pathology and impact, discuss chronic primary pain approaches, exercise and ACT/CBT. Opioid initiation is not indicated solely for chronic primary pain.
Case 3 — Neuropathy: A 61-year-old with diabetes describes burning feet worse at night, distal sensory loss and intact pulses. Assess neuropathy and foot risk; discuss CG173 options and review response and adverse effects.
Case 4 — Red flag back pain: A 48-year-old with back pain develops urinary retention and saddle numbness. Emergency cauda equina assessment and MRI take priority over a routine analgesic escalation.
Case 5 — Opioid harm: A patient on long-term high-dose opioids for noncancer pain reports limited function and sedation. Assess current risk, mental health, indications and alternatives, plan shared gradual review; abrupt discontinuation can precipitate withdrawal and harm.

9. PLAB 2 consultation framework

“I believe your pain and want to understand both what causes it and what it stops you doing.” Explore ICE (ideas, concerns, expectations), red flags, prior treatments, mood/sleep, daily function and goals. Explain uncertainty without dismissing symptoms. Offer a collaborative plan: treat causes; discuss benefits/risks of medicines; provide activity and self-management support; agree follow-up and specific urgent return symptoms. For a patient requesting stronger opioids, validate pain while explaining that long-term harms may outweigh benefits for some chronic conditions, and offer a supported alternative and review.

DDx decision tree

  1. Acute, rapidly worsening, feverish, vascular or neurological symptoms? Exclude emergency.
  2. Focal mechanical or inflammatory tissue signs? Investigate and treat underlying cause.
  3. Burning, electric, allodynia or sensory deficit? Consider neuropathic pain.
  4. Persistent widespread pain with substantial impact not explained by disease? Consider chronic primary pain after appropriate assessment.
  5. Coexisting mechanisms? Individualise treatment and avoid over-reliance on a single medicine class.
Do not miss: cauda equina, septic arthritis, ischaemia, compartment syndrome, ACS, meningitis or acute surgical abdomen. Validate pain while making a safe diagnosis.

Deep Dive · neurophysiology, pharmacology and clinical reasoning

Why pain persists—and why treatments differ

1. Peripheral transduction and sensitisation

Tissue injury activates Aδ and C-fibre nociceptors through mechanical, thermal and chemical signals. Inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins increase nociceptor excitability, explaining some benefit of NSAIDs for acute inflammatory pain. Reduced inflammation does not necessarily resolve all persistent pain.

2. Spinal transmission and modulation

Signals enter the dorsal horn, synapse and ascend in spinothalamic pathways. Descending brainstem circuits can facilitate or inhibit transmission; serotonin and noradrenaline are involved. Treatments such as duloxetine and some tricyclic antidepressants affect pain modulation and may be useful in specified neuropathic and chronic primary settings.

3. Central sensitisation and nociplastic pain

Repeated nociceptive input and altered central processing can increase responses to harmless stimuli (allodynia) and amplify painful stimuli (hyperalgesia). Persistent pain is real even when imaging and laboratory tests do not explain its intensity. Psychological and social context changes experience, distress and adaptation; it does not mean pain is imaginary.

4. Acute versus chronic primary pain

Acute pain often has a time-limited injury or inflammatory source. Chronic primary pain is defined by its persistent impact and not being better explained by another condition; management prioritises function, quality of life, graded activity, evidence-based psychological interventions and shared decisions rather than escalating analgesics.

5. Neuropathic mechanisms

Peripheral nerve damage or central somatosensory lesions can produce spontaneous ectopic firing, dysaesthesia and abnormal sensory gain. A burning, electric or allodynic phenotype prompts a nerve-distribution examination and mechanism-specific treatment under CG173.

6. Opioid physiology

Mu-opioid receptors reduce neuronal transmitter release and can relieve severe nociceptive pain but also suppress respiratory drive, slow bowel transit and cause tolerance and dependence. More dose is not necessarily more function; opioid-induced hyperalgesia is a possible explanation for diffuse worsening pain in some people on prolonged high doses.

7. Multimodal analgesia

Combining appropriate pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies can target different mechanisms and reduce reliance on one medication. The plan must account for age, kidney/liver function, pregnancy, other medicines, patient priorities and measurable benefits.

8. Deprescribing and withdrawal

Stopping an established opioid, benzodiazepine, gabapentinoid or antidepressant abruptly may cause withdrawal and destabilise health. NICE NG215 emphasises regular review, patient involvement and tailored gradual reduction when appropriate.
