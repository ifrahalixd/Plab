Duty of Candour & Medical Errors

Learning outcomes: Respond safely to mistakes; explain professional and organisational candour; apologise effectively; report and learn from incidents; manage complaints and near misses; know when to escalate.

1. Two overlapping duties

Professional duty of candour: GMC Good medical practice requires doctors to be open and honest with patients when things go wrong and a patient under their care has suffered harm or distress, or may do so. Put matters right where possible; apologise; explain promptly and fully what happened and the likely short- and long-term effects. Be open with colleagues and organisations, participate in reviews, and report incidents promptly (GMC Good medical practice, duty of candour provisions).

Statutory/organisational duty: In England, the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014, Regulation 20, imposes duties on regulated providers when a notifiable safety incident meets the applicable threshold. Organisations must follow notification, explanation, apology, record and written follow-up requirements. Thresholds and rules vary by provider type and UK nation. Do not assume every near miss is a statutory notifiable incident.

2. Immediate clinical response

  1. Recognise harm or potential harm; assess ABCDE and stabilise the patient.
  2. Call senior/specialist help early and prevent further harm.
  3. Tell the patient (or appropriate representative) promptly, honestly and sensitively.
  4. Apologise and explain known facts, effects, uncertainties, and next steps without speculation or blame.
  5. Arrange treatment, monitoring, support, follow-up and appropriate incident reporting.
  6. Make an accurate contemporaneous record; cooperate with investigation and learning.

3. Apology is not an admission of legal liability

A sincere apology acknowledges distress and is professionally expected. Say “I am sorry this happened,” not “these things happen” or “it was someone else’s fault.” Never delay disclosure until all investigations finish; be clear about what is known and what remains uncertain.

4. Errors, near misses and systems learning

Adverse event: an incident that caused harm. Near miss: a safety incident that could have caused harm but did not. Both may merit reporting under local patient-safety systems. The aim is safer care, not concealment or reflexive blame. Escalate repeated unsafe practice or attempts to suppress reporting.

5. Complaints and professional accountability

Listen, acknowledge concerns, explain the complaints pathway, and provide appropriate support. Never retaliate against a patient who complains. Keep records accurate: do not delete, backdate or covertly alter an entry; use a clearly dated correction according to policy. Where appropriate, inform supervisors, clinical governance and indemnity advisers.

Exam trap: Do not confuse apologising with accepting personal legal fault. Do not wait for an investigation before telling a harmed patient what is already known.

Decision-making pathways

After an error: SAFE → OPEN → LEARN

  1. SAFE: Identify immediate harm; stabilise; call senior help; correct the problem.
  2. OPEN: Tell the patient promptly, privately and compassionately; apologise; explain verified facts and uncertainty.
  3. SUPPORT: Discuss consequences, treatment options, questions and ongoing care.
  4. RECORD: Document clinical events and conversations accurately; submit the local safety incident report.
  5. LEARN: Cooperate with reviews, implement safety improvements and provide updates.

Does statutory organisational candour apply?

  1. Identify provider and relevant UK nation/regulator.
  2. Check whether the event meets that provider's notifiable safety incident criteria.
  3. Escalate to the responsible provider's governance/candour team; follow required notification and written records.
  4. Remember the professional duty may apply even if statutory criteria do not.

A safety incident report does not replace a clinical note, patient conversation or statutory notification where required.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Insulin dosing error

A nurse reports that a patient received twice their prescribed insulin dose and is sweaty and confused. Best response: urgently check glucose, treat hypoglycaemia, summon help, monitor, then explain and apologise to the patient when safe, document and report.

Case 2 · Delayed diagnosis

A scan was not reviewed for two days and a patient suffered avoidable deterioration. Best response: treat and escalate, inform the patient promptly about the delay and known consequences, apologise, discuss review and follow-up.

Case 3 · Near miss

A pharmacist catches a tenfold prescribing error before medication reaches the patient. Best response: correct the order, assess any residual risk, document and report according to local policy, and review why the error occurred. Do not automatically label it a statutory notifiable safety incident.

Case 4 · Pressure to change notes

A colleague asks you to replace an original clinical note to make an error disappear. Best response: refuse, preserve accurate records, report concerns through appropriate clinical and governance routes and seek senior support.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Disclosing a medication error

Station: A patient was given an incorrect antibiotic dose. They are now stable after assessment. Explain what happened and the plan.

Suggested opening

“Thank you for speaking with me. I need to tell you about an error in your care. You received a higher dose of your antibiotic than was prescribed. I am very sorry this happened. We have assessed you and are monitoring for any effects. I want to explain what we know, answer your questions, and tell you what we will do next.”

Station structure

  1. Check identity, privacy, preferred support person and readiness to talk.
  2. State the verified facts plainly; do not speculate or blame.
  3. Give an unambiguous apology and acknowledge feelings.
  4. Explain potential effects, what has been done, monitoring and follow-up.
  5. Explain reporting/review, how updates will be provided and whom to contact.
  6. Invite questions, check understanding, document and escalate appropriately.

Examiner checklist

Avoid “No harm was done” unless that is established. Do not promise an outcome of an investigation.

High-yield traps

  • Do not delay clinical treatment to complete a report.
  • Do not hide an error while awaiting an investigation.
  • Do not assume every error triggers statutory notification.
  • Do not alter or backdate notes to conceal mistakes.
  • Do not confuse apology with admission of negligence.
