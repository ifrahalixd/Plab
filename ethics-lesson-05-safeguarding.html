Safeguarding Children & Adults at Risk

Learning outcomes: Recognise abuse and neglect; respond safely to disclosure; understand consent, confidentiality, capacity and proportionate information-sharing; escalate concerns; distinguish child and adult safeguarding; document and follow up.

1. What is safeguarding?

Safeguarding means protecting people from abuse, neglect and exploitation, while respecting their rights and preferences. The GMC requires doctors to consider the welfare of vulnerable people and act promptly when concerned about abuse or neglect (Good medical practice, paragraphs 41–42).

2. Recognise patterns of harm

CategoryClues in PLAB scenarios
Physical abuseInjuries inconsistent with history, patterned bruising, unexplained fractures, delay in seeking care
Sexual abuse/exploitationDisclosure, genital symptoms or injury, coercion, grooming, exploitation indicators
Emotional abuseThreats, intimidation, humiliation, controlling behaviour
NeglectUnmet nutrition, hygiene, shelter, supervision or medical needs
Financial abuseUnexplained withdrawals, coercion over property, deprivation of funds
Domestic abuse/coercive controlFear of partner, isolation, repeated injury, restricted access to care
Organisational abuseUnsafe institutional practices, degrading care, repeated neglect

Exam trap: No single sign proves abuse. A reassuring explanation also does not automatically exclude abuse. Assess the whole context and seek specialist advice.

3. Children: a lower threshold to share concerns

Prioritise the child's safety and welfare. Listen calmly, use open questions, avoid leading or repeated investigative interviews, do not promise secrecy, and record the child's exact words where possible. Discuss promptly with the named/designated safeguarding professional and follow local child-protection procedures. Share relevant information without consent where necessary and justified to protect a child from significant harm; explain your actions when safe and appropriate.

4. Adults: autonomy and safeguarding together

Assess immediate danger, decision-specific capacity, coercion and the adult's wishes. A capacitous adult may refuse support, but a refusal does not remove the need to consider risks to children, other adults, or serious public-interest grounds for disclosure. Under the Care Act 2014 in England, adult safeguarding duties relate to adults with care and support needs who experience or are at risk of abuse/neglect and cannot protect themselves because of those needs. Rules differ across UK nations.

5. Respond to disclosure: LISTEN–PROTECT–ESCALATE–RECORD

  1. Listen: Believe the concern deserves assessment; remain calm, avoid blame and promises of absolute confidentiality.
  2. Protect: Treat urgent injuries, consider safe separation from an alleged perpetrator and emergency services when needed.
  3. Escalate: Seek safeguarding lead and relevant statutory agencies; do not investigate independently.
  4. Record: Facts, verbatim disclosure, observations, who was informed, reasons for sharing or not sharing, and agreed follow-up.

6. Confidentiality and sharing

Usually seek consent for adult referrals and explain what will be shared. Share without consent when legally required or justified, for example to prevent serious harm or protect a child, using the minimum necessary information and recording the rationale. Do not delay an urgent referral merely because a safeguarding lead is unavailable.

7. UK legal variation

England: Children Act 1989/2004, Care Act 2014 and statutory safeguarding guidance. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have distinct safeguarding legislation and procedures. The GMC principles apply UK-wide, but local legal routes and agency names differ.

Safeguarding decision pathways

Child with possible abuse

  1. Assess and treat immediate medical danger; arrange safe supervision.
  2. Take a careful, non-leading history; examine appropriately and document injuries objectively.
  3. Consider the child's developmental stage, communication needs, and any immediate danger from accompanying adults.
  4. Contact safeguarding lead and children's social care / police as indicated by urgency and local procedure.
  5. Consider consent and confidentiality, but do not allow refusal to prevent justified protective sharing.
  6. Document, hand over and verify that the concern reached the responsible team.

Adult at risk

  1. Check urgent safety and whether speaking privately is safe.
  2. Ask about abuse sensitively, coercion, wishes and available support.
  3. Assess capacity for the specific decision if there is reason to doubt it.
  4. Offer options, safety planning and referral; usually seek consent.
  5. Where serious harm, risks to others or a legal duty justify disclosure, escalate proportionately even without consent.
  6. Document the reasoning, safety plan and follow-up.

PLAB discriminator: Safeguarding is not the same as automatic police reporting for every concern. The appropriate action depends on immediate risk, age, capacity, legal duties and the patient's wishes.

Four clinical reasoning cases

Case 1 · Non-mobile infant with bruises

A 4-month-old infant has bruises on the torso. A caregiver gives inconsistent accounts. Best response: assess and treat injuries, urgently discuss with paediatrics and safeguarding, document carefully, and follow child-protection referral procedures. Do not send the infant home solely on reassurance.

Case 2 · Teenager discloses exploitation

A 15-year-old says an older adult pressures them into sex and asks you to keep it secret. Best response: listen without judgement, explain the limits of confidentiality, assess immediate danger, seek safeguarding advice and make a justified protective referral.

Case 3 · Adult with capacity refuses referral

An older patient describes financial exploitation but understands the options and declines social care involvement. Best response: explore coercion, risks to others, wishes and safety; offer support and a follow-up plan. Do not assume incapacity or automatically override the refusal. Escalate if other legal or serious-risk grounds apply.

Case 4 · Partner insists on staying

A pregnant patient with repeated injuries appears frightened and their partner refuses to leave the room. Best response: create a safe opportunity for private conversation, assess danger, offer domestic abuse support, consider children/unborn baby safeguarding and share information proportionately.

Station: A 29-year-old attends with bruising. Their partner answers all questions and refuses to leave.

Suggested opening

“I routinely speak to patients alone for part of their consultation. Would you mind waiting outside while we discuss some private health questions?”

Once alone: “Sometimes injuries happen because someone has hurt us. Has anyone made you feel unsafe at home? You can tell me as much or as little as you feel comfortable sharing.”

Examiner priorities

Empathy, privacy, non-judgemental open questions, immediate risk assessment, children/dependants, capacity and coercion, limits of confidentiality, appropriate referral, safety plan and accurate documentation.

Self-assessment checklist

Never confront the suspected perpetrator in a way that increases risk. Do not promise absolute secrecy.
