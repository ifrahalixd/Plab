Cardiovascular pharmacology · Deep Dive

Receptor physiology, haemodynamics, neurohormonal adaptation and practical calculations.

1. Mean arterial pressure and afterload

Mean arterial pressure is approximately cardiac output × systemic vascular resistance (ignoring central venous pressure). Cardiac output equals heart rate × stroke volume. Vasodilators reduce resistance; beta-blockers reduce heart rate and myocardial oxygen demand; diuretics change intravascular volume. A drug's haemodynamic effect does not automatically predict its effect on survival.

2. RAAS and glomerular physiology

Renin initiates angiotensin II generation, causing arteriolar constriction, aldosterone release and sodium retention. ACE inhibition reduces angiotensin II and increases bradykinin, explaining cough and part of angioedema risk. Angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole; RAAS blockade can lower intraglomerular pressure and produce an early creatinine rise. Consider volume depletion, NSAIDs and renovascular disease if deterioration is substantial.

3. Cardiac beta receptors

Beta-1 receptors are Gs-coupled, increasing cAMP and calcium entry. Blockade lowers chronotropy and inotropy acutely; long-term use in stable HFrEF counteracts harmful sympathetic activation and ventricular remodelling. This explains why beta-blockers should be started gently in stable patients rather than initiated during shock.

4. Vascular smooth muscle and nitrates

NO activates soluble guanylate cyclase, increasing cGMP and promoting smooth muscle relaxation. Venodilation lowers preload and wall tension, reducing oxygen demand. PDE5 inhibitors prevent cGMP breakdown, so co-administration can produce profound hypotension.

5. Calcium-channel physiology

L-type calcium channels are present in vascular smooth muscle, myocardium and conduction tissue. Dihydropyridines predominantly act on vessels; verapamil/diltiazem act more strongly on the myocardium and AV node. Distinguish peripheral oedema from fluid-overload oedema clinically.

6. Nephron targets

Loop diuretics block NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb; thiazide-like drugs block NCC in the distal convoluted tubule; MRAs antagonise aldosterone at the mineralocorticoid receptor in distal nephron cells. Predict electrolyte disturbances from the nephron site. SGLT2 inhibitors act in the proximal tubule, increasing urinary glucose and sodium excretion and exerting cardiorenal effects beyond glucose lowering.

7. Worked calculation: pulse and pressure

A patient with HR 100 beats/min and stroke volume 60 mL has cardiac output ≈ 6 L/min. If HR falls to 70 with unchanged stroke volume, output would be ≈ 4.2 L/min. In real physiology stroke volume and vascular resistance adapt, so do not use this simple arithmetic to infer a patient’s actual output after treatment.

8. Interpreting potassium and renal changes

RAAS blockers and MRAs can raise potassium, whereas loop/thiazide diuretics often lower it. The combination requires structured monitoring rather than assuming the effects cancel. A change in creatinine may reflect haemodynamics, dehydration, obstruction or intrinsic kidney injury; interpret alongside BP, fluid balance and trend.

9. HFrEF versus HFpEF

HFrEF involves reduced LV systolic ejection fraction and neurohormonal activation; guideline-directed multi-drug therapy modifies disease progression. HFpEF is heterogeneous and may involve impaired relaxation, elevated filling pressure and systemic comorbidities. Treatment evidence differs, so do not simply copy the HFrEF regimen into every HFpEF case.

10. Prescribing OSCE decision script

State the indication, check haemodynamics and contraindications, reconcile medicines, choose an evidence-based agent, explain benefits and harms, prescribe safely, plan potassium/renal/BP/pulse monitoring, document and safety-net. Ask the patient to repeat the plan in their own words.

1 · Clinical framework

Cardiovascular pharmacology is indication-specific: a drug can lower blood pressure without improving survival in heart failure, and a drug that relieves angina may not prevent myocardial infarction. Begin with the diagnosis, haemodynamic status, comorbidities, pregnancy potential, renal function, electrolytes, pulse and current medicines. Distinguish essential hypertension, stable angina, acute coronary syndromes, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and arrhythmias. Never substitute a memorised drug list for the current NICE pathway or local formulary.

2 · Hypertension: selecting initial therapy

Confirm hypertension using appropriate clinic and ambulatory/home measurements unless urgent management is required. NICE NG136 uses age, type 2 diabetes and family origin in its stepwise selection: an ACE inhibitor or ARB is generally first-line for adults under 55 without Black African or African–Caribbean family origin, and for adults with type 2 diabetes; a calcium-channel blocker is generally first-line for adults aged 55 or over without type 2 diabetes, or of Black African or African–Caribbean family origin without type 2 diabetes. In people of Black African or African–Caribbean family origin with type 2 diabetes, NICE prefers an ARB to an ACE inhibitor. Individualise for pregnancy, frailty, CKD and contraindications. Step 2 often combines ACEi/ARB with CCB; if CCB unsuitable consider thiazide-like diuretic. Step 3 combines ACEi/ARB + CCB + thiazide-like diuretic. For resistant hypertension confirmed on optimised triple therapy, check adherence and measurement and consider low-dose spironolactone when potassium is 4.5 mmol/L or lower, with appropriate renal monitoring; if potassium exceeds 4.5 consider an alpha- or beta-blocker and seek specialist input as indicated. Do not combine ACEi with ARB.

3 · ACE inhibitors and ARBs

ACE inhibitors (ramipril, lisinopril) reduce conversion of angiotensin I to II and reduce aldosterone; ARBs (losartan, candesartan, valsartan) block AT1 receptors. They lower afterload and have important indications in HFrEF, hypertension and selected CKD with albuminuria. Adverse effects include hypotension, hyperkalaemia, acute deterioration in renal function; ACE inhibitors additionally cause cough and rarely angioedema. Check blood pressure, creatinine/eGFR and potassium before initiation and after initiation or dose increase according to indication and guidance (commonly within 1–2 weeks). A modest creatinine rise can occur from efferent arteriolar dilation; a marked rise requires assessment for hypovolaemia, NSAIDs, renal artery stenosis and other causes. Avoid in pregnancy; do not use ACEi and ARB together. Prior ACEi-related angioedema needs careful alternative selection and specialist consideration.

4 · Calcium-channel blockers

Dihydropyridines (amlodipine, felodipine) predominantly dilate arterioles: useful for hypertension and angina; adverse effects include ankle oedema, flushing and headache. Non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) slow AV nodal conduction and reduce contractility: may treat certain rate-control problems and angina but can cause bradycardia, heart block and constipation (especially verapamil). Avoid verapamil and diltiazem in HFrEF unless a specialist directs otherwise. Combining verapamil with a beta-blocker risks severe bradycardia/heart block and is generally avoided. Amlodipine-related ankle oedema is not simply salt retention, so escalating loop diuretics may be ineffective.

5 · Beta-blockers

Beta-1 blockade (bisoprolol, metoprolol) reduces heart rate, contractility and renin release; carvedilol also blocks alpha-1 receptors. Indications depend on agent and condition: angina, selected arrhythmias, post-MI and evidence-based HFrEF therapy. Common risks: bradycardia, fatigue, hypotension and masking adrenergic warning signs of hypoglycaemia. Use caution in asthma, conduction disease and acute decompensated heart failure; cardioselectivity is relative, not absolute. In stable HFrEF start a licensed evidence-based beta-blocker at a low dose and up-titrate as tolerated. Do not abruptly discontinue long-term beta-blockers without a clinical plan, because rebound tachycardia/ischaemia can occur.

6 · Diuretics and mineralocorticoid antagonists

Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) inhibit NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb and relieve pulmonary/peripheral congestion; monitor weight, volume status, sodium, potassium and renal function. They improve symptoms but are not one of the four HFrEF disease-modifying pillars. Thiazide-like agents (indapamide) inhibit distal NaCl reabsorption; they can cause hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia and gout. Spironolactone and eplerenone block mineralocorticoid receptors and reduce mortality/hospitalisation in selected HFrEF patients; risks include hyperkalaemia and renal deterioration, plus gynaecomastia with spironolactone. Assess kidney function and potassium before and during therapy. Do not mistake rising creatinine during effective decongestion for a reason to automatically stop all therapy; assess the whole clinical picture.

7 · Nitrates and other antianginals

Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) releases nitric oxide, increases cGMP and causes predominantly venodilation, lowering preload and myocardial oxygen demand; sublingual GTN provides rapid symptom relief in stable angina. Headache, flushing and hypotension are common. Never combine nitrates with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors such as sildenafil or tadalafil because profound hypotension can occur; ask about recent use and follow the relevant interval guidance. Beta-blockers or calcium-channel blockers are commonly first-line maintenance treatments for stable angina; choice depends on pulse, BP, heart function and contraindications. Long-acting nitrates and other agents may be added when needed, with specialist input. Angina at rest, prolonged pain, syncope or instability requires urgent assessment for ACS rather than simply escalating GTN.

8 · Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction

HFrEF treatment is based on disease-modifying therapy plus symptom relief. Four key medication classes are: (1) renin–angiotensin system inhibition with an ACEi, ARB or, when indicated, sacubitril/valsartan (ARNI); (2) an evidence-based beta-blocker; (3) a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist; and (4) an SGLT2 inhibitor such as dapagliflozin or empagliflozin, including in people without diabetes when eligible. Follow current NICE recommendations, licensing and specialist initiation criteria, which may differ between classes and local pathways. Sacubitril/valsartan must not be co-administered with an ACE inhibitor; a 36-hour washout is required after stopping ACEi before starting ARNI. Monitor BP, renal function, potassium, fluid status and tolerability while titrating. Use loop diuretics for congestion. In acutely unstable or shocked patients, prioritise stabilisation before initiating or increasing negative inotropes.

9 · HFpEF and comorbidity

HFpEF management requires careful diagnosis and treatment of congestion, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, obesity, CKD and other contributing disease. SGLT2 inhibitors have evidence and NICE-recommended roles in appropriate HFpEF populations; do not assume all HFrEF medications have identical mortality benefits in HFpEF. Consider frailty, orthostatic hypotension and polypharmacy. A normal ejection fraction does not exclude heart failure.

10 · Pregnancy, renal disease and prescribing safety

ACEi, ARBs and ARNIs are contraindicated in pregnancy. For hypertension in pregnancy, NICE NG133 commonly uses labetalol, nifedipine or methyldopa depending on circumstances, with obstetric assessment. In CKD, ACEi/ARB may protect kidneys in selected albuminuric patients but require potassium and creatinine monitoring. NSAIDs combined with ACEi/ARB and a diuretic increase AKI risk (the “triple whammy”). Check pulse before beta-blockers and rate-limiting CCBs; consider ECG if conduction disease suspected. Record indications, targets, monitoring plan and safety-net advice; confirm interactions using current BNF.

11 · Clinical case: newly diagnosed hypertension

A 48-year-old without diabetes has confirmed persistent hypertension and normal potassium and renal function. An ACE inhibitor such as ramipril is commonly an appropriate first-line option under NICE NG136 if no contraindication. Discuss pregnancy risk where relevant, baseline blood tests, cough/angioedema warnings, dizziness and follow-up potassium/creatinine testing. A different first-line agent may be appropriate depending on family origin and comorbidities.

12 · Clinical case: oedema after amlodipine

A 67-year-old taking amlodipine develops symmetrical ankle swelling without breathlessness or raised JVP. Dihydropyridine-mediated precapillary vasodilation can cause dependent oedema. Examine for heart failure and venous disease, review dose and alternatives, and avoid assuming a loop diuretic is the solution. If the patient has hypertension, a regimen adjustment guided by NICE and clinical factors may help.

13 · Clinical case: worsening HFrEF

A 72-year-old with known HFrEF presents with orthopnoea, crackles, oedema and recent weight gain. Assess ABCDE, oxygenation, perfusion and precipitating factors; check ECG, renal function and electrolytes. Treat congestion appropriately, often with loop diuretics, while monitoring response. Reassess long-term disease-modifying treatment once stabilised; do not initiate or rapidly up-titrate beta-blockers in cardiogenic shock.

14 · Clinical case: dangerous interaction

A patient with angina requests GTN after taking sildenafil earlier that day. Do not administer GTN until the relevant safe interval has elapsed and a clinician has assessed the situation. If there is ongoing chest pain, activate urgent ACS assessment and explain the potentially life-threatening hypotension interaction. Ask specifically about erectile dysfunction medication without judgement.

15 · PLAB 2 counselling station

Explain the indication in plain language (“this medicine helps your heart pump with less strain”), expected benefits, dosing, adverse effects and blood-test monitoring. Ask about over-the-counter NSAIDs, supplements, pregnancy possibility and missed doses. Check understanding with teach-back. For ACEi: warn about facial/lip/tongue swelling requiring emergency help. For diuretics: discuss daily weights when indicated, dizziness/dehydration and an individualised sick-day plan from the treating team. Provide a clear follow-up plan and safety net.

16 · Exam pitfalls

Do not use verapamil with a beta-blocker casually; do not combine ACEi and ARB; do not prescribe nitrates with recent PDE5 inhibitor exposure; do not confuse symptom-relieving loop diuretics with disease-modifying HFrEF therapies; do not use ACEi/ARB in pregnancy; do not interpret every creatinine increase in isolation; and do not start a beta-blocker in shock. Always distinguish chronic stable treatment from emergency management.
