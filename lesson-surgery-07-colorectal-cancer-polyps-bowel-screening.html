Colorectal cancer: diagnosis, staging and prevention

PLAB priority: Consider colorectal cancer in unexplained rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, change in bowel habit, weight loss or abdominal mass. A negative FIT does not automatically override strong clinical concern; use safety-netting and appropriate referral.

1 · Clinical presentation and risk factors

PresentationClinical clue
Right-sided colon cancerOccult bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, fatigue, weight loss; sometimes a mass
Left-sided colon cancerChange in bowel habit, obstructive symptoms, visible bleeding
Rectal cancerRectal bleeding, tenesmus, urgency, altered stool calibre; may be palpable on DRE
Advanced diseaseObstruction, perforation, liver metastases, constitutional symptoms

Risk factors: increasing age, family history, Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), long-standing colonic inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, smoking and alcohol. Cancer can also occur in younger adults.

2 · Assessment and investigation

  1. History: bleeding (colour, duration), bowel changes, weight loss, pain, anaemia symptoms, family history, previous polyps, IBD and screening history.
  2. Examination: vital signs, pallor, abdomen, masses, digital rectal examination when appropriate with explanation, consent and chaperone.
  3. Bloods: FBC for iron-deficiency anaemia, renal and liver function as indicated. Do not use CEA alone to diagnose or exclude cancer.
  4. In UK primary care, use quantitative faecal immunochemical testing (FIT) to guide referral for adults with relevant symptoms according to NICE NG12. A FIT of at least 10 micrograms Hb/g faeces supports referral on a suspected cancer pathway; safety-net if below threshold or sample not returned, and do not delay referral when there is a rectal/anal mass or anal ulceration.
  5. Colonoscopy with biopsy is a key diagnostic test; CT colonography may be an alternative in selected patients. Once confirmed, stage with CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and pelvic MRI for rectal cancer, according to specialist pathways.

3 · Polyps and hereditary syndromes

Adenomatous polyps are premalignant; risk increases with size, number and dysplasia. Sessile serrated lesions also carry malignant potential. Colonoscopy can remove many polyps, followed by histology and risk-based surveillance. FAP involves numerous adenomas and very high lifetime cancer risk without appropriate management; Lynch syndrome causes hereditary mismatch-repair deficiency and increases colorectal and endometrial cancer risk. Suspected inherited risk requires genetics/specialist assessment.

4 · Management

Discuss in a colorectal multidisciplinary team. Localised colon cancer is generally treated with oncological resection and lymph-node assessment; adjuvant chemotherapy depends on stage and risk. Rectal cancer may require neoadjuvant radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy or total neoadjuvant therapy before surgery, depending on MRI stage and local protocol. Metastatic disease may be managed with systemic therapy and selected metastasis-directed treatment. Explain potential stoma, bowel/sexual/urinary effects and support needs.

5 · NHS bowel screening

In England, the NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme uses a home FIT kit, routinely offered every 2 years to people aged 50–74 (rollout and local arrangements may vary). Screening is for people without symptoms; symptomatic patients require clinical assessment rather than waiting for a screening kit. Positive screening FIT leads to further assessment, commonly colonoscopy.

6 · Emergencies

Suspected malignant large-bowel obstruction or perforation: ABCDE, nil by mouth, IV access, fluids and electrolyte correction, analgesia, urgent surgical review and CT when stable. Peritonitis, sepsis or threatened bowel viability require emergency management; stenting or diversion may be considered in selected obstruction cases.

Exam traps: Do not reassure solely on a normal haemoglobin or negative FIT when concerning symptoms persist. Do not confuse screening FIT with symptomatic FIT pathways. CEA is mainly used in monitoring, not as a stand-alone diagnostic test.

Decision pathways and revision slides

Pathway A — Symptomatic patient

Rectal bleeding / altered bowel habit / iron-deficiency anaemia → history, examination, FBC → symptomatic FIT under NICE NG12 where appropriate → suspected-cancer referral if FIT ≥10 µg Hb/g, or referral despite low FIT when ongoing clinical concern or relevant examination findings → colonoscopy + biopsy.

Pathway B — Confirmed colorectal cancer

Histology confirms malignancy → staging CT chest/abdomen/pelvis ± pelvic MRI for rectal tumour → colorectal MDT → surgery ± neoadjuvant/adjuvant systemic or radiotherapy depending on stage → surveillance and survivorship care.

Pathway C — Screening

Asymptomatic eligible person → NHS home FIT every 2 years → negative: routine screening as invited; positive: assessment and usually colonoscopy → remove polyps or biopsy suspicious lesions.

Right: occult blood/anaemia. Left: change in bowel habit/obstruction. Rectum: bleeding, urgency, tenesmus.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Right-sided cancer

A 69-year-old reports fatigue, weight loss and newly identified iron-deficiency anaemia. Approach: evaluate for gastrointestinal malignancy, examine, investigate and refer according to symptomatic pathway; arrange definitive endoscopic assessment.

Case 2 — Rectal bleeding and tenesmus

A 57-year-old has six weeks of rectal bleeding and urgency. Approach: history, examination including DRE where appropriate, symptomatic FIT/referral under NICE NG12, colonoscopy with biopsy; if rectal cancer confirmed, pelvic MRI and staging.

Case 3 — Positive screening FIT

A 62-year-old feels well but returns a positive NHS bowel screening kit. Approach: explain that this is not a cancer diagnosis, discuss follow-up assessment and colonoscopy, benefits, risks and possible polyp removal.

Case 4 — Obstructing tumour

A 74-year-old has progressive distension, vomiting and no stool or flatus. CT suggests obstructing sigmoid malignancy. Approach: ABCDE, fluids/electrolytes, nil by mouth, analgesia, urgent surgical assessment and planning for decompression, stenting or operation according to stability and local expertise.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute rectal bleeding station

Brief: You are an FY2 in GP. A 58-year-old has intermittent rectal bleeding and a recent change in bowel habit. Take a focused history, explain concerns and agree a safe plan.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, verify identity, obtain consent and acknowledge concerns.
  2. Characterise bleeding, bowel habit, stool appearance, pain, tenesmus and duration.
  3. Ask about weight loss, fatigue, anaemia symptoms, appetite and obstructive red flags.
  4. Explore family history of colorectal/endometrial cancer, polyps, IBD and previous screening.
  5. Review medications, anticoagulants, comorbidities and impact on daily life.
  6. Explain examination including abdominal examination and DRE with consent/chaperone.
  7. Discuss FBC, symptomatic FIT and urgent referral/colonoscopy when indicated; explain FIT is not definitive.
  8. Use non-alarming language, invite questions, provide safety-netting and document the plan.

Suggested explanation: “Bleeding can have several causes, including haemorrhoids, but because your bowel habit has changed we should check for more serious causes. The next steps may include a stool test and a camera examination of the bowel.”

Rapid revision and UK references

  1. Right-sided CRC often presents with iron-deficiency anaemia; left-sided disease with bowel change/obstruction.
  2. Rectal cancer may cause bleeding, tenesmus and urgency.
  3. Symptomatic FIT threshold in NICE NG12: ≥10 µg Hb/g faeces; maintain safety-netting for low FIT.
  4. Colonoscopy and biopsy establish diagnosis; staging CT and pelvic MRI for rectal cancer.
  5. Adenomas and sessile serrated lesions can be precancerous.
  6. FAP and Lynch syndrome require specialist hereditary cancer assessment.
  7. Screening does not replace investigation of symptoms.
  8. Management is MDT-led and stage-dependent.
