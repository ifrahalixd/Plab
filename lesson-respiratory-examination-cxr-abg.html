Respiratory examination, chest radiography and arterial blood gases

Emergency reminder: Severe respiratory distress, shock or suspected tension pneumothorax requires immediate escalation. Never delay life-saving care for imaging.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

OSCE respiratory examination: preparation

Wash hands, introduce yourself, confirm identity, explain examination, gain consent and ask about pain. Position at 45 degrees and expose chest appropriately with dignity.

PLAB application: Assess whether the patient is acutely unwell before a routine examination.

General inspection

Inspect respiratory rate, work of breathing, ability to speak, posture, cyanosis, cough, sputum, oxygen devices and surrounding equipment.

PLAB application: Look for pursed-lip breathing, tripod positioning, cachexia and signs of respiratory distress.

Hands and arms

Inspect clubbing, peripheral cyanosis, nicotine staining, tremor and asterixis (CO₂ retention). Check pulse and consider respiratory rate.

PLAB application: Clubbing suggests possibilities including lung cancer, bronchiectasis and fibrotic ILD; it is not typical of uncomplicated COPD.

Face, neck and lymph nodes

Look for conjunctival pallor, central cyanosis, Horner syndrome and cervical/supraclavicular lymphadenopathy. Assess tracheal position and JVP when indicated.

PLAB application: Tracheal deviation can occur with major volume loss or pressure; interpret alongside clinical stability.

Chest inspection and palpation

Inspect symmetry, scars, deformity and chest expansion. Palpate trachea and compare chest expansion; assess tactile vocal fremitus if indicated.

PLAB application: Reduced unilateral expansion may indicate effusion, collapse, pneumothorax or consolidation.

Percussion

Compare symmetrical areas from apices to bases on both sides, including lateral chest if feasible.

PLAB application: Dullness: effusion or consolidation; hyper-resonance: pneumothorax or hyperinflation. Percussion is supportive, not definitive.

Auscultation

Compare breath sounds systematically on both sides; listen for wheeze, crackles, bronchial breathing or reduced air entry. Check vocal resonance when indicated.

PLAB application: Bronchial breathing and increased vocal resonance can support consolidation; reduced breath sounds occur with effusion or pneumothorax.

Complete the examination

Check oxygen saturations and observations, inspect legs for oedema/DVT signs when relevant, and offer peak flow, sputum or other targeted tests. Summarise findings clearly.

PLAB application: In an OSCE say you would review observations, chest X-ray and relevant investigations.

CXR: systematic approach

Confirm patient identifiers, date/time, indication and projection (PA/AP). Assess image quality using rotation, inspiration and exposure before describing findings.

PLAB application: Use ABCDE: Airway, Breathing/lungs and pleura, Cardiomediastinal silhouette, Diaphragm, Everything else.

CXR quality and pitfalls

AP portable views magnify the heart; under-inspiration can mimic basal opacity. Rotation alters apparent mediastinal position.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose cardiomegaly from a portable AP film alone.

CXR common patterns

Consolidation: airspace opacity ± air bronchograms. Effusion: blunted costophrenic angle/meniscus. Pneumothorax: pleural line with absent peripheral markings. Collapse: volume loss.

PLAB application: Assess clinical urgency: suspected tension pneumothorax is treated clinically, without delaying for imaging.

CXR additional patterns

Pulmonary oedema may show bilateral perihilar opacities, septal lines and effusions; COPD may show hyperinflation. A normal CXR does not exclude PE.

PLAB application: Describe the pattern before suggesting the most likely differential.

ABG: what to check first

Check patient, sample type, time, oxygen delivery/FiO₂ and clinical context. Review pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, PaO₂ and lactate.

PLAB application: Typical adult reference ranges vary by laboratory: pH ~7.35–7.45, PaCO₂ ~4.7–6.0 kPa, HCO₃⁻ ~22–26 mmol/L.

ABG: acid–base steps

First determine acidaemia or alkalaemia; then identify whether PaCO₂ or HCO₃⁻ explains the primary process. Check compensation and consider mixed disorders.

PLAB application: Low pH + high CO₂ suggests respiratory acidosis; low pH + low bicarbonate suggests metabolic acidosis.

ABG: oxygenation and respiratory failure

Type 1 respiratory failure is hypoxaemia without hypercapnia; type 2 has hypercapnia with hypoxaemia commonly present. Interpret PaO₂ in relation to inspired oxygen.

PLAB application: Use prescribed oxygen targets: commonly 94–98%, or 88–92% in those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure pending ABGs, according to local protocols.

PLAB 2: present and escalate

Present key positive and negative findings, state likely diagnosis and differentials, and propose tests and immediate action. Use SBAR for deterioration.

PLAB application: Red flags: severe respiratory distress, new confusion, exhaustion, silent chest, shock or rapidly falling saturations require urgent escalation.

PLAB 2 OSCE checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent and check pain.
  2. Inspect, examine hands and face, assess neck, palpate, percuss and auscultate.
  3. Check observations and oxygen saturation; complete relevant examination.
  4. Interpret CXR systematically with technical quality and projection.
  5. Interpret ABG using pH, PaCO₂, HCO₃⁻, PaO₂, FiO₂ and lactate.
  6. Present findings, differential, management and red flags.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Vocal resonance and sound transmission

Consolidation may increase voice-sound transmission; fluid or air in the pleural space often decreases it.

Tracheal deviation

Mediastinal structures may shift towards substantial volume loss or away from a large space-occupying pleural process; interpretation must account for urgency.

CXR projection effects

PA films are preferable for estimating heart size; AP portable films magnify anterior structures.

ABG compensation

Renal compensation to chronic hypercapnia raises bicarbonate over time; acute and chronic processes can overlap.

Oxygenation versus ventilation

PaO₂ reflects oxygenation and PaCO₂ reflects alveolar ventilation; both are needed in suspected respiratory failure.

ABG safety

A normal pH does not exclude a clinically important mixed or compensated disorder; correlate with observations and trend.
