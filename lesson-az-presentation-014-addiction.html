Addiction: assess dependence, risk and recovery

Addiction is a chronic, relapsing but treatable pattern of compulsive substance use or behaviour despite harm. UK practice emphasises a non-judgemental assessment, recognition of intoxication and withdrawal emergencies, harm reduction, shared goals and ongoing psychosocial and pharmacological support.

Article audio
Urgent dangers: opioid overdose with slow breathing; severe alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal with seizure, hallucinations or delirium; stimulant-associated chest pain, hyperthermia or agitation; suicidal intent, safeguarding risk and severe infection from injecting drugs. Stabilise first and involve emergency teams.

1. Key concepts and diagnostic language

Hazardous use increases health risk; harmful use causes demonstrable harm; dependence / substance use disorder involves impaired control, prioritisation of use, continued use despite harm, craving and sometimes tolerance or withdrawal. Physical dependence can develop during appropriate prescribed treatment and is not, by itself, the same as addiction. Avoid stigmatising descriptions such as “drug seeker”.

Assess alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, cocaine and other stimulants, cannabis, nicotine, ketamine, prescribed medicines and non-substance behavioural addiction such as gambling. Polysubstance use is common and changes overdose or withdrawal risk.

2. First encounter: ABCDE and consent

If the patient is unwell, use ABCDE, check glucose and observations, ECG when indicated, consider pregnancy and head injury, and seek timely help. Prioritise airway and respiratory effort in opioid overdose, seizure management and autonomic instability in alcohol withdrawal, and temperature/cardiovascular assessment in stimulant toxicity. Clarify capacity when intoxicated, protect confidentiality, explain its safeguarding limits and offer a private conversation.

3. Focused history using a practical framework

  • Substance history: what, how much, route, frequency, last use, changing pattern, mixing with alcohol or sedatives, injecting equipment and possible adulterants.
  • Dependence indicators: craving, inability to cut down, time spent obtaining/using/recovering, tolerance, withdrawal, neglect of responsibilities and continued use despite harm.
  • Withdrawal: prior seizures or delirium tremens, morning drinking, tremor, sweats, insomnia, nausea, diarrhoea, agitation, body aches and time since last substance.
  • Consequences: injuries, overdose, work/education, relationships, debt, housing, legal issues, infection, liver disease and mental health.
  • Risk: self-harm, violence, exploitation, driving, pregnancy, domestic abuse, dependants or children at home and access to naloxone.
  • Motivation: goals and readiness, what has helped before, safe support network and treatment preference.

4. Screening and assessment tools

AUDIT-C can screen alcohol consumption; a full AUDIT assists assessment. CIWA-Ar can help assess alcohol withdrawal where appropriate, but does not replace clinical judgement. A COWS score helps assess opioid withdrawal. Neither a score nor a negative urine drug screen excludes risk. Urine toxicology is selective and should be explained and used consensually where appropriate; unexpected substances and false results occur.

5. Examination and investigations

Document consciousness, respiration, pupils, blood pressure, pulse, temperature, hydration, nutrition, mental state, trauma and injection sites. Consider blood glucose; U&Es, magnesium and phosphate; LFTs and clotting; FBC; pregnancy test; ECG; and HIV/hepatitis B/hepatitis C testing according to history and consent. Choose infection imaging and blood cultures if clinically indicated. Interpret results within the history and time course rather than diagnosing dependence from laboratory tests alone.

7. Emergency treatment principles

Opioid overdose: call emergency help; support airway and ventilation and give naloxone following local protocol. Repeat dosing or infusion may be needed because naloxone can wear off before the opioid; observe for recurrent respiratory depression. Do not replace ventilation with naloxone alone.

Alcohol withdrawal: clinically assess severity and previous complications. Severe dependence or withdrawal, seizures, delirium tremens, important comorbidity and safeguarding concerns may require inpatient management. Use protocol-led benzodiazepine treatment with monitoring when indicated, and give thiamine to at-risk people; suspected Wernicke encephalopathy needs parenteral thiamine urgently. Do not assume that a person with dependence can safely stop alcohol suddenly without medical assessment.

Benzodiazepine dependence: avoid abrupt discontinuation; plan gradual specialist-supervised reduction. Flumazenil is not routinely appropriate in mixed overdose or dependence because it can trigger seizures. Stimulant toxicity: urgently manage hyperthermia, chest pain, severe agitation and cardiovascular complications in monitored care.

8. Recovery: shared plan and evidence-based support

Ask the person what matters most, offer motivational interviewing, structured psychological treatment, practical housing/financial support, peer support and treatment of coexisting mental illness. Alcohol dependence treatment may involve medically assisted withdrawal followed by relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy such as acamprosate or naltrexone after specialist assessment; disulfiram is appropriate only for selected patients with informed consent and supervision. Opioid dependence treatment can include methadone or buprenorphine through specialist services, overdose prevention and naloxone access, infection prevention and regular review. Avoid fixed assumptions that complete abstinence is everyone's immediate goal; plan harm reduction when appropriate.

9. Five worked PLAB cases

Case 1 — Unresponsive person: A 31-year-old is cyanosed and breathing four times per minute after suspected heroin use. Reasoning: opioid toxidrome and impending respiratory arrest. Action: emergency help, ABCDE, airway/ventilation and naloxone with observation for recurrence.
Case 2 — Shaking after admission: A 49-year-old who drinks heavily daily develops tremor, sweating, tachycardia and agitation 18 hours after stopping. Reasoning: alcohol withdrawal; assess past seizures and signs of severe withdrawal. Action: monitored alcohol withdrawal pathway and thiamine risk assessment.
Case 3 — Confusion and gait difficulty: A malnourished patient with alcohol dependence develops confusion, gait ataxia and abnormal eye movements. Reasoning: suspected Wernicke encephalopathy; not all features need to be present. Action: urgent parenteral thiamine and specialist admission.
Case 4 — Chest pain after cocaine: A 26-year-old has crushing chest pain and marked tachycardia after cocaine use. Reasoning: stimulant-associated acute coronary syndrome is possible. Action: emergency ACS assessment, ECG/troponins and senior-led treatment.
Case 5 — Asking for help: A 42-year-old with opioid dependence wants treatment but reports previous overdose after a period of abstinence. Reasoning: reduced opioid tolerance increases future overdose risk. Action: collaborative treatment referral, naloxone, harm-reduction counselling and assessment of housing, mood and bloodborne virus risks.

10. PLAB 2 OSCE: language, empathy and safety netting

Open: “Would it be okay if I ask about alcohol and medicines, including any drugs you use? I ask everyone because it can affect treatment.” Reflect without blame, explain the confidential nature of the consultation and safeguarding limits, screen for immediate danger, establish the patient's goals and offer a specific follow-up plan. Document consent, risks, treatment and urgent return instructions. A person expressing suicidal thoughts or recent self-harm needs a timely psychosocial assessment rather than exclusion because of intoxication.

High-yield exam distinctions

Opioid intoxication → slow breathing; opioid withdrawal → autonomic/GI symptoms. Alcohol intoxication → incoordination; withdrawal → tremor and sympathetic activation following reduced intake. Delirium is an acute fluctuating disturbance of attention and cognition; do not assume it is psychiatric illness.

Deep Dive · neurobiology and clinical reasoning

Why addiction develops and why withdrawal matters

1. Reward learning and dopamine

The mesolimbic circuit connects the ventral tegmental area with the nucleus accumbens and related prefrontal networks. Dopamine supports salience and reinforcement learning; it is not simply a “pleasure chemical”. Repeated exposure strengthens cues associated with substance use, while stress and negative affect can intensify craving.

2. Dependence versus disorder

Neuroadaptation can produce tolerance and withdrawal. Physical dependence during prescribed therapy does not by itself establish substance use disorder; diagnosis also considers control, prioritisation, harms and context. Biological vulnerability interacts with trauma, social circumstances, psychiatric disorders and availability.

3. Alcohol withdrawal physiology

Chronic alcohol exposure shifts excitatory/inhibitory balance, including GABA and glutamate activity. Abrupt cessation may unmask neuronal hyperexcitability and cause autonomic activation, seizures and delirium tremens. Wernicke encephalopathy arises from thiamine deficiency, especially in malnourished patients, and is a separate emergency.

4. Opioid overdose and naloxone

Mu-opioid receptor activation in brainstem respiratory networks reduces respiratory drive. Naloxone competitively antagonises opioid receptors and may reverse respiratory depression, but its action can be shorter than that of some opioids. Repeated doses, ventilation and monitoring may therefore be required.

5. Why relapse is a medical risk

Tolerance decreases during abstinence or incarceration; resuming a previous opioid dose can cause fatal overdose. This underpins counselling on naloxone, treatment continuity and overdose prevention. Relapse should trigger reassessment and support rather than punishment.

6. Diagnostic traps

Do not miss sepsis, head injury, hypoglycaemia or self-harm in intoxicated patients. Do not assume everyone can withdraw safely at home; account for prior complicated withdrawal and comorbidity. Avoid assuming a positive urine drug screen proves dependence, or a negative screen excludes exposure.
