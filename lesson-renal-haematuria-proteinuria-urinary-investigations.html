Haematuria, Proteinuria & Urinary Investigations

UKMLA / PLAB-focused assessment, differential diagnosis, investigation, referral and safety-netting.

01. Clinical approach

Haematuria is blood in urine; proteinuria is excess urinary protein. Both may indicate glomerular, urological or systemic disease. Assess stability first, then identify the source and persistence.

PLAB application: Do not assume urinary blood is infection without supporting symptoms and tests.

02. Visible versus non-visible haematuria

Visible haematuria changes urine colour; non-visible haematuria is detected by dipstick. Consider menstruation, exercise, trauma, stones, infection, malignancy and glomerulonephritis. Confirm a positive dipstick with clinical context and exclude contamination.

PLAB application: Painless visible haematuria is a cancer warning symptom.

03. Red flags and emergency assessment

Clot retention, inability to pass urine, haemodynamic instability, severe flank pain with fever, acute kidney injury, rapidly progressive renal impairment or pulmonary haemorrhage require urgent assessment.

PLAB application: A febrile obstructed urinary tract is an emergency.

04. History

Ask about colour and timing of blood, clots, dysuria, frequency, fever, loin pain, recent sore throat, rash, oedema, hypertension, smoking, occupational exposures, trauma, anticoagulants and family history. Ask about menstrual contamination and pregnancy possibility when relevant.

PLAB application: Clots suggest a non-glomerular bleeding source, but are not diagnostic.

05. Examination

Record observations and volume status. Examine for pallor, oedema, rash, palpable bladder, abdominal masses, costovertebral tenderness and signs of systemic vasculitis. Measure blood pressure.

PLAB application: Hypertension plus oedema and haematuria suggests a nephritic process.

06. Urine dipstick and microscopy

Dipstick detects haem pigment, protein, leukocytes and nitrites. Microscopy may show red cells, dysmorphic red cells and red-cell casts suggesting glomerular disease. Myoglobin can cause blood-positive dipstick without urinary red cells.

PLAB application: Red-cell casts are a key clue to glomerulonephritis.

07. Quantifying proteinuria

Use urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR), preferably an early-morning sample when confirmation is needed. Protein:creatinine ratio (PCR) is useful in selected situations, including significant non-albumin proteinuria. Dipstick alone does not reliably quantify protein loss.

PLAB application: NICE CKD classification uses albuminuria categories A1, A2 and A3.

08. Albuminuria categories

ACR A1 is <3 mg/mmol, A2 is 3–30 mg/mmol, and A3 is >30 mg/mmol. ACR ≥3 mg/mmol may indicate kidney damage when persistent. Confirm ACR 3–70 mg/mmol on a subsequent early-morning sample; ACR ≥70 mg/mmol does not need repeat confirmation before action.

PLAB application: Interpret ACR alongside eGFR, duration and clinical context.

09. Transient and orthostatic proteinuria

Fever, strenuous exercise, acute illness and urinary infection may cause temporary proteinuria. Orthostatic proteinuria can occur in young people with normal early-morning urine. Persistent or significant proteinuria needs investigation.

PLAB application: Repeat testing when transient causes have resolved.

10. Initial blood tests

Consider FBC, creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes, inflammatory markers when indicated, glucose/HbA1c, and targeted autoimmune tests (ANA, ANCA, complement, anti-GBM) if glomerulonephritis suspected. Order urine culture when infection is plausible.

PLAB application: Do not order every autoimmune test for uncomplicated cystitis.

11. Urological versus glomerular source

Glomerular clues include proteinuria, red-cell casts, dysmorphic red cells, hypertension and reduced eGFR. Urological clues include clots, colicky pain, urinary symptoms and risk factors for malignancy. Both may coexist.

PLAB application: Renal and urological assessment can both be needed.

12. Imaging and cystoscopy

Renal tract ultrasound assesses hydronephrosis, masses and structural disease; CT may be needed for stones or cancer assessment. Cystoscopy evaluates the bladder and lower urinary tract in appropriate patients. Choose investigations according to risk, symptoms and local pathways.

PLAB application: Normal ultrasound does not reliably exclude bladder cancer.

13. Suspected cancer referral

Follow NICE NG12: adults aged ≥45 with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or persisting/recurring after successful UTI treatment, qualify for suspected cancer pathway referral. Adults ≥60 with unexplained non-visible haematuria plus dysuria or raised white cell count also meet criteria.

PLAB application: Do not delay a cancer referral by repeatedly prescribing antibiotics without evidence.

14. Proteinuria and kidney referral

Refer urgently for suspected rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis or systemic disease. NICE NG203 specialist assessment criteria include ACR ≥70 mg/mmol (unless appropriately managed diabetic kidney disease), ACR >30 mg/mmol with haematuria, and significant eGFR decline or other risk factors.

PLAB application: Heavy proteinuria with haematuria warrants nephrology review.

15. Management and follow-up

Treat the underlying cause, control blood pressure and cardiovascular risk, review nephrotoxic medicines, and monitor ACR/eGFR as indicated. Discuss ACE inhibitor/ARB and SGLT2 inhibitor eligibility in CKD according to NICE guidance and individual risks.

PLAB application: A positive test is a finding, not a complete diagnosis.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and safety net

Explain urine results without alarm, ask permission for sensitive questions, address cancer anxiety, explain repeat tests/referral and agree follow-up. Advise urgent help for inability to urinate, large clots, fever with flank pain, severe pain or deterioration.

PLAB application: Close with a clear plan and teach-back.

PLAB 2 OSCE checklist

Introduce yourself, obtain consent, assess red flags, ask about urinary symptoms and systemic illness, explain ACR and urine results in plain language, discuss investigations/referral, and safety-net.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Glomerular barrier

The filtration barrier comprises fenestrated endothelium, glomerular basement membrane and podocytes. Damage may cause albuminuria and red-cell leakage.

Red-cell morphology

Dysmorphic red cells and casts support glomerular bleeding, whereas clots favour a urological source. Neither should replace full clinical assessment.

Albuminuria and prognosis

Albuminuria predicts kidney and cardiovascular risk independently of eGFR; use the G and A categories together.

Dipstick limitations

A blood-positive dipstick can detect haemoglobin or myoglobin; protein dipsticks are insensitive for low-level albuminuria.

Cancer pathways

Risk-stratified assessment matters: age, visible bleeding and persistence after UTI treatment inform NG12 referral criteria.
