Gastroenteritis, Food Poisoning & Infectious Diarrhoea

PLAB priority: Assess dehydration, recognise bloody diarrhoea and HUS, and prescribe antibiotics selectively.

01. Clinical approach

Ask about onset, stool frequency and consistency, blood or mucus, vomiting, fever, abdominal pain, oral intake and urine output. Record recent travel, food exposure, contacts, antibiotics, admissions, immunosuppression and pregnancy.

PLAB application: Start with hydration and severity, not a presumed organism.

02. Acute gastroenteritis

Acute infectious gastroenteritis commonly causes diarrhoea, vomiting and cramps. Viral causes, especially norovirus, are frequent; bacterial causes include Campylobacter, Salmonella and Shigella.

PLAB application: Most uncomplicated cases need supportive care rather than antibiotics.

03. Dehydration assessment

Check heart rate, blood pressure, capillary refill, mucous membranes, mental state, weight when relevant and urine output. Postural symptoms, oliguria and lethargy suggest significant fluid loss.

PLAB application: Shock or inability to maintain oral hydration warrants urgent assessment.

04. Rehydration

Oral rehydration solution is preferred when the patient can drink; use small frequent sips if vomiting. IV isotonic crystalloid may be required for severe dehydration or shock, with frequent reassessment.

PLAB application: Rehydration is the priority intervention.

05. Norovirus

Norovirus typically causes abrupt vomiting and watery diarrhoea and spreads easily through contact and contaminated environments. Use soap-and-water handwashing and appropriate isolation and cleaning.

PLAB application: Advise avoiding work or school until 48 hours after symptoms stop, according to public-health advice.

06. Food poisoning and incubation

Foodborne illness may follow contaminated meat, poultry, eggs, seafood, dairy or produce. Very rapid vomiting after a shared meal suggests preformed toxin, whereas invasive bacterial disease may cause fever or blood.

PLAB application: Incubation helps narrow causes but does not confirm a pathogen.

07. Campylobacter and Salmonella

Campylobacter often causes fever, cramps and sometimes bloody diarrhoea; Salmonella can cause gastroenteritis after contaminated foods. Severe illness and vulnerable patients may need targeted treatment.

PLAB application: Avoid routine empirical antibiotics in otherwise uncomplicated illness.

08. Shigella and STEC

Shigella may cause fever, tenesmus and bloody stools. Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) may cause bloody diarrhoea and haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS).

PLAB application: Suspected STEC: urgently assess, test stool and avoid antibiotics and antimotility agents unless specialist advice indicates otherwise.

09. Clostridioides difficile

Consider C. difficile with new diarrhoea after antibiotics or healthcare exposure. Send an appropriate unformed stool sample, assess severity, review precipitating antibiotics and use infection-control precautions.

PLAB application: Treat confirmed or strongly suspected infection using current local/NICE antimicrobial guidance; severe disease needs urgent care.

10. Travel-associated diarrhoea

Ask about destination, drinking water, food, swimming, duration and systemic features. Persistent diarrhoea may indicate Giardia or other parasites; malaria must be considered separately in a febrile returning traveller.

PLAB application: Persistent, bloody or febrile diarrhoea requires investigation.

11. When to investigate

Stool PCR/culture is useful for bloody diarrhoea, severe or persistent illness, suspected outbreak, immunosuppression, travel-associated infection and C. difficile risk. Check U&Es, renal function and FBC when dehydration or complications are suspected.

PLAB application: Do not order routine stool tests for every brief mild episode.

12. Antibiotics and antimotility drugs

Antibiotics are not routinely indicated for uncomplicated gastroenteritis. Base use on suspected organism, severity, host risk and susceptibility. Avoid loperamide with bloody diarrhoea, high fever, suspected C. difficile or STEC.

PLAB application: Rehydration and diagnostic judgement come before prescriptions.

13. Children and vulnerable groups

Children, older adults, pregnant patients and immunocompromised people dehydrate or deteriorate more readily. In children assess red flags, continue breastfeeding, and use oral rehydration in accordance with NICE guidance.

PLAB application: Escalate for lethargy, reduced responsiveness, persistent vomiting or poor urine output.

14. Complications and red flags

Watch for sepsis, acute kidney injury, electrolyte disturbances, severe abdominal pain, peritonitis, HUS (anaemia, thrombocytopenia, renal injury) and toxic megacolon.

PLAB application: Bloody diarrhoea plus falling urine output after a diarrhoeal illness is an emergency.

15. Infection control and public health

Handwashing with soap and water, surface disinfection, safe food handling and contact precautions reduce transmission. Report suspected foodborne outbreaks through local infection-control/public-health routes.

PLAB application: Ask whether others who shared the meal or household are unwell.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain likely self-limiting illness, oral fluids/ORS, normal diet as tolerated, hygiene, return-to-work precautions and when to seek urgent help. Give tailored advice for children and high-risk patients.

PLAB application: Safety-net for blood in stool, severe pain, fainting, confusion, minimal urine or inability to drink.

Deep Dive

Secretory versus inflammatory diarrhoea

Watery diarrhoea often reflects secretion or malabsorption; blood, fever and tenesmus suggest mucosal inflammation. Clinical patterns overlap, so use history and tests.

How oral rehydration works

Sodium–glucose cotransport supports water absorption even during many secretory diarrhoeal illnesses, making correctly prepared ORS effective.

STEC and HUS

Shiga toxin damages endothelium, particularly in renal microvasculature, causing haemolysis, platelet consumption and acute kidney injury.

C. difficile pathophysiology

Disrupted gut microbiota permits toxin-producing C. difficile proliferation; disease ranges from mild diarrhoea to fulminant colitis.

Antimicrobial stewardship

Antibiotics can cause adverse effects, resistance and C. difficile; reserve them for clear indications and follow local susceptibility advice.

Distinguishing gastroenteritis from emergencies

Severe focal pain, guarding, shock, bloody stool with oliguria or disproportionate pain should prompt evaluation for surgical, vascular or systemic causes.
