Bleeding Disorders: Haemophilia, von Willebrand Disease & ITP

Emergency red flags: head injury or neurological symptoms in haemophilia, uncontrolled bleeding, airway bleeding, shock, or severe bleeding with thrombocytopenia need immediate hospital care.

Overview

Bleeding disorders arise from platelet number/function, coagulation factor defects, or vascular disease. History and bleeding pattern guide initial investigation.

PLAB application: Distinguish mucocutaneous bleeding from deep tissue bleeding.

Primary versus secondary haemostasis

Platelets and von Willebrand factor form the initial plug; coagulation factors stabilise it with fibrin.

PLAB application: Petechiae and epistaxis suggest platelet/vWF problems; haemarthrosis suggests factor deficiency.

Bleeding history

Ask about epistaxis, bruising, heavy menstrual bleeding, dental or surgical bleeding, family history, medicines, liver disease and previous transfusions.

PLAB application: Ask whether bleeding is spontaneous or follows procedures.

Initial investigations

Check FBC and platelet count, blood film, PT/INR, APTT and fibrinogen as appropriate. Normal screening tests do not exclude von Willebrand disease or mild haemophilia.

PLAB application: Urgently assess haemodynamic status in major bleeding.

Haemophilia A and B

Haemophilia A is factor VIII deficiency and haemophilia B is factor IX deficiency. They are commonly X-linked and severity depends on residual factor activity.

PLAB application: Suspect with recurrent haemarthroses or muscle haematomas.

Haemophilia presentation

Severe disease may cause spontaneous joint or muscle bleeding; milder disease may present after surgery or trauma. Intracranial and airway bleeding are emergencies.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary intramuscular injections and traumatic procedures.

Haemophilia laboratory findings

APTT may be prolonged with normal PT and platelets; mild disease can have normal APTT. Confirm with specific factor VIII or IX activity and specialist assessment.

PLAB application: A mixing study can help distinguish deficiency from inhibitor.

Haemophilia treatment

Specialists use factor replacement or other prophylactic agents; haemophilia A may be treated with emicizumab in selected patients. Desmopressin may help selected mild haemophilia A, not haemophilia B.

PLAB application: Never delay emergency specialist treatment for suspected major bleed.

Haemophilia inhibitors

Neutralising antibodies can reduce factor treatment efficacy. Acquired haemophilia A may present with new severe bleeding and isolated prolonged APTT in adults.

PLAB application: Urgent haematology involvement is required.

von Willebrand disease

Inherited vWF deficiency or dysfunction impairs platelet adhesion and may lower factor VIII. Type 1 is partial quantitative deficiency; type 2 qualitative; type 3 severe quantitative deficiency.

PLAB application: Common symptoms include nosebleeds, easy bruising and heavy periods.

von Willebrand testing

Specialist testing includes vWF antigen, vWF activity and factor VIII; levels fluctuate with illness, stress, pregnancy and hormones. Repeat testing may be needed.

PLAB application: Normal PT/APTT does not rule it out.

von Willebrand management

Depending on type and context, options include tranexamic acid, desmopressin after response assessment, or vWF-containing concentrates. Coordinate dental and surgical plans.

PLAB application: Desmopressin is not suitable for every vWD subtype.

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

ITP is an acquired immune-mediated reduction in platelets, usually an isolated thrombocytopenia diagnosis after excluding alternative causes. It can cause petechiae, purpura and mucosal bleeding.

PLAB application: Review blood film and medications; exclude pseudothrombocytopenia.

ITP investigation and treatment

Assess bleeding severity and platelet count, review film and secondary causes. Observation may suffice in low-risk patients; corticosteroids, IVIG and second-line treatments are specialist choices.

PLAB application: Treatment decisions depend on bleeding, risks and count—not count alone.

Emergency bleeding

Major haemorrhage, suspected intracranial bleeding, airway compromise or haemodynamic instability require ABCDE, resuscitation and immediate senior/haematology input. In ITP with critical bleeding, multimodal urgent treatment may include IVIG, steroids and platelet transfusion.

PLAB application: Do not wait for routine clinic review.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain the likely disorder without premature certainty, ask about impact and medicines, discuss precautions, emergency symptoms, menstrual care and genetic implications where relevant.

PLAB application: Provide clear return advice for head injury or uncontrolled bleeding.

PLAB 2 — Unexplained bruising or bleeding

Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent. Explore bleeding onset, sites, severity, menstrual and procedural history, medicines (including anticoagulants and NSAIDs), family history, and impact. Assess red flags, explain FBC/coagulation and specialist tests, avoid premature diagnosis, and safety-net for uncontrolled bleeding or head injury.

Deep Dive

Clinical patterns

Platelet and vWF disorders generally cause immediate mucocutaneous bleeding; coagulation factor deficiency can cause delayed deep tissue bleeding. Overlap occurs, so do not use bleeding site alone to diagnose.

Interpreting coagulation tests

PT assesses the extrinsic/common pathways and APTT the intrinsic/common pathways. Anticoagulants, inhibitors and pre-analytical factors affect results. A normal APTT does not exclude mild haemophilia or vWD.

Acquired haemophilia

New spontaneous extensive soft-tissue bleeding and an isolated prolonged APTT in someone without previous bleeding history raises concern for acquired factor VIII inhibitor; urgent specialist treatment is essential.

ITP differentials

Exclude platelet clumping, drugs, infections, marrow disease, TMA and other causes. Schistocytes plus thrombocytopenia and haemolysis suggest TMA rather than uncomplicated ITP.

Treatment safety

Avoid aspirin/NSAIDs when clinically inappropriate in bleeding disorders, but review individual indications with clinicians. Desmopressin carries hyponatraemia risk and is not appropriate in all vWD subtypes.

Genetic counselling

Haemophilia A/B are often X-linked, but family history can be absent; vWD has variable inheritance by subtype. Discuss testing, reproductive options and specialist support without assumptions.
