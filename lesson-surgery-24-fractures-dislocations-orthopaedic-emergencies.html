Fractures, dislocations and orthopaedic emergencies

Never miss: open fracture, compartment syndrome, neurovascular compromise, septic arthritis, cauda equina syndrome or an irreducible dislocation. Escalate immediately to orthopaedics and relevant emergency teams.

1 · First approach

In major trauma perform <C>ABCDE before limb assessment. Obtain mechanism, timing, pain, weight-bearing ability, comorbidities, anticoagulants, tetanus status and prior injuries. Inspect for deformity, wounds, swelling, skin tenting and bruising. Document distal pulses, capillary refill, temperature, motor and sensory function before and after splinting or reduction. Examine joint above and below suspected fracture.

2 · Imaging and initial treatment

Provide prompt analgesia, support/splint the limb, remove constricting items and obtain two orthogonal radiographic views including adjacent joints where appropriate. CT helps with complex articular or pelvic fractures; MRI detects occult fractures and soft tissue injury when indicated. Immobilise and reassess neurovascular status. Do not delay urgent treatment for imaging in a limb-threatening emergency.

3 · Open fractures

Open fracture means communication between fracture and outside environment, sometimes via a small wound. Cover with sterile saline-moistened dressing under an occlusive layer per local protocol; avoid repeated wound probing or bedside washout. Give IV antibiotics promptly following local open-fracture policy, check tetanus protection, realign/splint gently as needed, and arrange urgent orthoplastic assessment for debridement, fixation and soft-tissue coverage. Photograph wounds once if policy permits and avoid repeated exposure. NICE NG37 guides management.

4 · Acute compartment syndrome

Rising pressure in a closed fascial compartment impairs perfusion, often after tibial fractures or crush injury. Earliest clues: pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense swollen compartment and increasing analgesic requirement. Paraesthesia and weakness may develop; pulselessness is a late and unreliable sign. Remove tight dressings/casts, keep limb at heart level, maintain perfusion, call orthopaedics immediately; urgent fasciotomy is definitive when diagnosed. A palpable pulse does not exclude it.

5 · Dislocations

Assess and document neurovascular function before/after reduction. Shoulder anterior dislocation: flattened deltoid contour, limited movement; assess axillary nerve sensation over lateral deltoid. Posterior shoulder dislocation: consider after seizure/electrocution; often missed on AP film, obtain appropriate axillary/scapular views. Hip dislocation after high-energy trauma needs urgent specialist reduction (typically within hours) to reduce avascular necrosis risk, with sciatic nerve assessment. Knee dislocation can spontaneously reduce yet injure popliteal artery; urgent vascular assessment is vital. Do not force reduction without appropriate expertise and analgesia/sedation.

6 · Common high-yield fractures

Neck of femur: older person after fall, shortened externally rotated leg; urgent analgesia including fascia iliaca block when appropriate, imaging and orthogeriatric/orthopaedic pathway. If radiographs negative but suspicion remains, MRI is preferred (CT if MRI unavailable within 24 hours or contraindicated) per NICE CG124. Scaphoid: anatomical snuffbox tenderness after FOOSH; immobilise and arrange early MRI if radiographs negative per NICE NG38. Distal radius: FOOSH with wrist deformity, assess median nerve. Supracondylar humerus in children: assess brachial artery and median/anterior interosseous nerve. Tibial shaft: monitor for compartment syndrome.

7 · Septic arthritis and osteomyelitis

Hot, swollen, exquisitely painful joint with reduced range of movement is septic arthritis until excluded. Fever may be absent. Obtain urgent senior assessment, blood cultures, and aspirate joint for microscopy, culture and crystals when feasible before antibiotics if this does not delay care; start empiric IV antibiotics and arrange urgent drainage/washout with specialist team. Crystals do not exclude coexistent infection. Osteomyelitis may present with focal bone pain, fever and raised inflammatory markers; MRI and microbiology guide management.

8 · Cauda equina and spinal injury

New urinary retention or altered urinary sensation, saddle sensory loss, bilateral sciatica, leg weakness or bowel dysfunction suggests cauda equina syndrome. Perform focused neurological exam, document bladder symptoms and arrange emergency MRI and spinal surgical referral. Do not rely on normal rectal tone to exclude it. Immobilise suspected unstable spinal injuries appropriately while following major trauma protocols.

9 · Fragility fractures and prevention

Consider osteoporosis and falls risk after low-energy fracture. Assess pain, mobility, home safety and secondary prevention; use fracture liaison services and bone-health evaluation. For hip fractures, coordinate prompt surgery when medically appropriate, delirium prevention, VTE prophylaxis per risk assessment and early rehabilitation.

10 · Safety-net and documentation

Explain red flags: escalating pain despite analgesia, numbness, weakness, cold/pale limb, new inability to move digits, fever, or tight cast. Recheck neurovascular observations after interventions. Provide clear follow-up for repeat imaging and orthopaedic review.

Decision pathways and rapid comparison

Injured limb

ABCDE if major trauma → analgesia → inspect skin and deformity → document neurovascular status → immobilise → X-rays (two views) → reassess neurovascular status → orthopaedic plan.

Open fracture

Sterile cover + IV antibiotics promptly + tetanus assessment + splint → urgent orthoplastic referral → operative debridement and fixation. Avoid bedside irrigation/probing.

Suspected compartment syndrome

Disproportionate pain/passive stretch pain → remove constriction, limb at heart level → emergency orthopaedic review → urgent fasciotomy if confirmed. Pulses may remain present.

Dislocation

Pre-reduction neurovascular exam → radiographs when safe → specialist-guided reduction → post-reduction exam and imaging → immobilisation and follow-up.

Occult hip fracture

Unable to bear weight + negative X-rays → MRI (or CT if MRI unavailable within 24 hours/contraindicated) → hip fracture pathway.

Hot swollen joint

Suspect septic arthritis → urgent aspiration/cultures if feasible without delay → IV antibiotics and drainage with specialist input.

Cauda equina red flags

Bladder/bowel or saddle sensory change ± leg deficits → emergency MRI and spinal team; do not wait for complete paralysis.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Pain after tibial fracture

A 28-year-old in a cast has escalating leg pain despite opioids and pain on passive toe stretch; foot pulses remain palpable. Decision: suspected acute compartment syndrome; loosen/remove constriction, call orthopaedics immediately and prepare for urgent fasciotomy.

Case 2 — Open lower-leg fracture

A cyclist has tibial deformity with a small wound and visible bone. Decision: assess neurovascular status, sterile dressing, prompt IV antibiotics, tetanus review, splint and urgent orthoplastic assessment.

Case 3 — Elderly fall, normal X-ray

An 82-year-old cannot weight-bear after a fall; hip radiographs are normal. Decision: suspected occult hip fracture; provide analgesia, keep safe from weight bearing and arrange MRI or CT as specified by NICE.

Case 4 — Hot swollen knee

A 54-year-old has acute severe knee pain, swelling and reduced range of motion, with fever. Decision: septic arthritis until proven otherwise; urgent aspiration/cultures, antibiotics and orthopaedic washout planning.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Injured leg with compartment syndrome

Station: A 30-year-old with a tibial fracture was placed in a cast 4 hours ago. Pain is worsening and the toes tingle.

  1. Introduce yourself, recognise a time-critical limb-threatening emergency and call the senior orthopaedic team.
  2. Assess ABCDE if clinically unwell; provide appropriate analgesia and monitor observations.
  3. Ask about pain severity, progression, numbness, weakness and tightness; examine for swelling, tense compartments and pain with passive toe movement.
  4. Document motor, sensation, perfusion and pulses, remembering that normal pulses do not exclude compartment syndrome.
  5. Release constrictive cast/dressings promptly with appropriate help; maintain limb at heart level and adequate systemic perfusion.
  6. Explain suspected rising compartment pressure and need for urgent specialist assessment and potentially emergency surgery (fasciotomy).
  7. Keep patient nil by mouth when surgery likely, obtain IV access and relevant bloods without delaying urgent review.
  8. Give SBAR handover and repeat neurovascular examinations with times documented.

Examiner priorities: pain on passive stretch, normal pulses do not exclude diagnosis, urgent decompression and escalation.

Rapid revision — 12 essentials

  1. Check and record distal neurovascular status before and after interventions.
  2. Open fracture: prompt IV antibiotics, sterile dressing, tetanus review and orthoplastic input.
  3. Compartment syndrome: disproportionate pain and passive-stretch pain; pulses may be normal.
  4. Urgent fasciotomy is definitive for established compartment syndrome.
  5. Anterior shoulder dislocation: assess axillary nerve.
  6. Posterior shoulder dislocation: suspect after seizure or electrocution.
  7. Knee dislocation: popliteal artery injury risk, even after spontaneous reduction.
  8. Hip dislocation: urgent reduction reduces avascular necrosis risk.
  9. Occult hip fracture: MRI if X-rays negative and suspicion persists.
  10. Suspected scaphoid fracture: immobilise and arrange appropriate imaging.
  11. Septic arthritis: urgent aspiration/cultures, antibiotics and joint drainage.
  12. Cauda equina symptoms: emergency MRI and spinal referral.
