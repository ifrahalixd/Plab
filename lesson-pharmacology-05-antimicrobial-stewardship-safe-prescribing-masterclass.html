Antimicrobial stewardship · Clinical deep dive

The microbiology and pharmacology behind choosing the right antibiotic and reviewing its effect.

MICROBIOLOGY

1 · Colonisation, contamination and infection

Colonisation is microbial presence without tissue invasion or symptomatic disease. Contamination is accidental introduction into a sample. Infection implies microbial invasion or harmful host response. Culture positivity alone cannot distinguish them; use site, sampling method, symptoms, immune status and inflammatory findings.

PHARMACODYNAMICS

2 · MIC and PK/PD targets

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is a laboratory measure of growth inhibition. For many beta-lactams, efficacy relates to the fraction of time free drug concentration remains above MIC; for aminoglycosides, peak concentration relative to MIC matters. For some agents, total exposure (AUC/MIC) is important. These relationships inform dose scheduling but do not replace local dosing guidance.

PHARMACOKINETICS

3 · Site penetration and route

Antibiotics must reach the infected compartment. Nitrofurantoin concentrates in urine, not renal parenchyma. Some antibiotics have poor cerebrospinal-fluid penetration unless meninges are inflamed. Oral bioavailability varies; an oral switch is appropriate only when the selected drug reaches effective exposure and the patient can absorb it.

RESISTANCE

4 · Selection pressure and resistance mechanisms

Resistance can arise through beta-lactamase production, target modification, porin loss, efflux pumps or bypass pathways. Exposure to unnecessarily broad antibiotics selects for resistant flora. Horizontal gene transfer spreads resistance genes between bacteria; hospital infection prevention helps reduce transmission.

IMMUNOLOGY

5 · Why inflammatory markers mislead

CRP is produced in response to inflammatory cytokines and rises in infection, autoimmune disease and tissue injury. Procalcitonin may assist some protocols but cannot alone establish bacterial infection or dictate treatment. Clinical stability and organ dysfunction remain central.

SOURCE CONTROL

6 · Biofilms and abscesses

Abscess cavities, obstructed organs and infected devices can persist despite apparently susceptible organisms. Biofilms reduce antimicrobial penetration and permit persistent microbial populations. Drainage, removal of infected material or relief of obstruction may be essential.

SAFETY

7 · Kidney injury and drug monitoring

Renal dysfunction changes exposure to renally cleared antibiotics. Aminoglycosides can cause nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity; vancomycin monitoring depends on regimen and local method. Record sample time, last dose, kidney function and clinical response before adjusting therapy.

PLAB REASONING

8 · The antibiotic timeout

Ask: Is infection still the most likely diagnosis? What do cultures show? Can we narrow or stop? Is IV still needed? Is source control adequate? What is the planned duration and review date? This prevents the common error of continuing empiric treatment without reassessment.

Antimicrobial stewardship means achieving the best outcomes for people with infection while minimising avoidable antibiotic harm and resistance. It is a bedside prescribing skill: decide whether antibiotics are indicated, choose the appropriate agent, document the plan and actively review treatment.

In 60 seconds: the essentials

  • First decide whether there is an infection requiring an antimicrobial, not merely a positive test or elevated CRP.
  • Use the syndrome, severity, likely organisms, previous cultures, allergies, renal function, pregnancy and local formulary to select treatment.
  • Obtain useful specimens before antibiotics when this will not delay time-critical care.
  • Suspected sepsis needs immediate risk assessment and escalation; antibiotic urgency is guided by the patient's clinical risk and current local sepsis protocol.
  • Document indication, drug, dose, route, intended duration, review date and source-control plan.
  • At review: stop, switch, change, continue with a documented rationale, or refer for specialist input.

1. Why stewardship matters

Antibiotics exert selection pressure: susceptible bacteria are suppressed, allowing resistant organisms to survive and spread. Unnecessary or excessively broad therapy also causes immediate harm, including allergy, diarrhoea, Clostridioides difficile infection, kidney injury, drug interactions and disruption of the microbiome. Conversely, delayed appropriate treatment in a critically ill patient can be dangerous. Stewardship balances both risks, rather than simply reducing antibiotic use.

Antimicrobial resistance is the ability of a microorganism to survive exposure to an antimicrobial that would normally inhibit it. Resistance may be intrinsic or acquired by mutation or horizontal gene transfer. Local antibiograms and individual microbiology results matter because resistance patterns vary by hospital, region and prior antibiotic exposure.

Clinical principle: Broad-spectrum is not synonymous with better. A narrow agent that reliably reaches the infected site and covers the pathogen can be safer and more effective.

2. Decide whether antibiotics are needed

Begin with history, examination and observations. Identify a plausible infectious focus, its severity and dangerous mimics. Fever and CRP are inflammatory markers, not bacterial-specific tests. A positive swab or urine culture may represent colonisation, contamination or asymptomatic bacteriuria. The decision to treat depends on symptoms, host factors and the clinical syndrome.

What cultures can and cannot tell you

Culture results identify organisms and may report susceptibility, but must be interpreted alongside sampling quality and the site of infection. Blood cultures are important in suspected bloodstream infection; superficial swabs can reflect colonising flora. Collect specimens before therapy if practicable without delaying urgent treatment. A negative culture does not exclude infection, especially after prior antibiotics. If the clinical picture contradicts a laboratory report, speak with microbiology.

3. A systematic framework for antibiotic selection

Step 1 — Define the infection and severity.

Is this lower UTI, pyelonephritis, community-acquired pneumonia, cellulitis, meningitis, intra-abdominal sepsis or another syndrome? Is the patient stable, immunocompromised or in shock? Site and severity determine route, spectrum and urgency.

Step 2 — Predict pathogens and review resistance risk.

Use local guidelines, prior cultures, recent antibiotic exposure, healthcare-associated infection, travel and relevant devices. Avoid guessing a single organism when the syndrome is polymicrobial.

Step 3 — Check the patient.

Document allergy phenotype, age, weight, pregnancy or breastfeeding, renal/hepatic function, QT risk, interacting medicines, ability to swallow and likelihood of adherence. A history of nausea is not equivalent to anaphylaxis.

Step 4 — Choose agent, dose, route and duration.

Use the current NICE condition-specific guidance, local antimicrobial policy and BNF. Ensure adequate site penetration. For example, nitrofurantoin is used for selected lower UTIs but not pyelonephritis because renal tissue concentrations are inadequate.

Step 5 — Document and plan reassessment.

Record indication, relevant cultures, start date, dose, route, review date, intended stop date or criteria for duration, and whether source control is needed. Tell the patient what to expect and when to seek help.

Empiric, targeted and prophylactic therapy

Empiric treatment is started before an organism is confirmed, based on the likely pathogens and illness severity. Targeted treatment follows organism identification and susceptibility assessment; narrowing therapy is often possible. Prophylaxis prevents infection in defined high-risk settings, such as selected surgical procedures, and should not become indefinite treatment without a clear indication.

4. Severe infection and sepsis: stewardship without delay

Use ABCDE, observations and a validated local sepsis pathway to identify high-risk patients. Escalate immediately if there is shock, altered mental state, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive infection or other signs of organ dysfunction. Obtain blood cultures and relevant specimens when feasible, but do not postpone life-saving treatment. Give appropriate empiric IV antibiotics within the urgency indicated by the current risk-stratified protocol, alongside fluids, oxygen when needed and source-control assessment.

After initial stabilisation, review whether the infection diagnosis remains likely and whether broad-spectrum coverage is still needed. Severe illness justifies prompt empiric treatment; it does not justify continuing unnecessary broad therapy after new evidence arrives.

PLAB safety: In suspected bacterial meningitis, do not delay appropriate antibiotics for lumbar puncture or imaging if doing so would postpone treatment. Follow local emergency meningitis guidance, including isolation and adjunctive treatments when indicated.

5. The antimicrobial review: stop, switch, change, continue

Review treatment within the interval specified by local policy, commonly at 48–72 hours for inpatient empiric therapy, and sooner if the patient deteriorates. Reassess observations, fever trend, organ function, microbiology, imaging, diagnosis, source control and adverse effects.

When can IV antibiotics become oral?

Assess clinical improvement, ability to take and absorb oral medication, availability of an effective oral agent, infection site and specific conditions requiring prolonged IV treatment. Bacteraemia alone is not a universal reason to continue IV indefinitely; equally, meningitis, endocarditis and some bone/joint infections need specialist-directed strategies. Never switch solely because the patient is afebrile once.

7. Prescribing safety and patient-specific adjustments

Renal function: check estimated kidney function, whether acute kidney injury is present and whether the medicine needs dose adjustment or avoidance. For aminoglycosides and vancomycin, use regimen-specific therapeutic monitoring. Pregnancy: verify condition-specific options and fetal safety; do not generalise an entire antibiotic class as universally safe. Allergy: record drug, reaction, timing, severity and treatment. Interactions: macrolides may prolong QT and interact with other medicines; rifampicin is a potent enzyme inducer; some antibiotics alter warfarin anticoagulation. Adverse effects: monitor for diarrhoea, rash, liver injury, renal injury and drug-specific toxicity.

Prescribe exact doses and durations using the current BNF and local formulary rather than memorising a single regimen for every patient. Antibiotic duration should be the shortest evidence-based effective course for the syndrome and clinical response—not automatically seven or fourteen days.

8. Worked clinical cases

Case A · A positive urine culture without symptoms

A 68-year-old non-pregnant woman has no dysuria, frequency, fever or flank pain. Urine sent during a routine assessment grows E. coli. Reasoning: asymptomatic bacteriuria is common and generally should not be treated in this setting. Check for special exceptions, explain the result and avoid unnecessary antibiotics. New symptoms would require reassessment.

Case B · Pyelonephritis and an unsuitable antibiotic

A 30-year-old has fever, flank pain, vomiting and costovertebral tenderness. Someone suggests nitrofurantoin because it treats UTIs. Reasoning: this is suspected upper urinary tract infection, possibly severe. Assess observations and sepsis risk, obtain urine culture, assess ability to take oral treatment and choose a kidney-penetrating agent under current guidance. Nitrofurantoin is unsuitable for pyelonephritis.

Case C · De-escalation after blood cultures

A hospitalised patient received broad-spectrum empiric antibiotics for severe infection. Cultures identify a susceptible organism; the patient is stable and improving. Reasoning: review source, susceptibility and any deep infection, then narrow therapy when appropriate, plan route and duration, and document the change. Do not continue broad coverage purely because it was started initially.

Case D · Fever persists despite antibiotics

A patient with abdominal infection remains febrile after 48 hours; imaging shows a collection. Reasoning: consider inadequate source control rather than simply adding more antibiotics. Escalate to surgery/interventional radiology and microbiology, assess for sepsis, and review the regimen in context.

9. PLAB 2 · Explain why antibiotics are not needed

Station: A patient requests antibiotics for three days of a runny nose, sore throat and cough, with reassuring observations and no bacterial red flags. Introduce yourself, confirm concerns, explore symptoms and risk factors, assess for red flags and explain that the illness is most likely viral. Describe expected recovery, symptom relief, hydration, and why antibiotics would not speed recovery but could cause diarrhoea, allergy or resistance. Agree on a safety-net: worsening breathlessness, persistent high fever, dehydration, confusion or other concerning symptoms require reassessment.

Communication example: “I understand you want to feel better quickly. From your symptoms and examination, an antibiotic is unlikely to help this infection. It can cause side effects, so the safer plan is symptom treatment and a clear review plan if things change.”
