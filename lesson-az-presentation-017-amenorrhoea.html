Amenorrhoea: the clinical approach

Amenorrhoea is the absence of menstrual bleeding. A clinician must distinguish normal physiological states from pregnancy, hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian dysfunction, endocrine disease and anatomical outflow abnormalities. The first test in a person with pregnancy potential is a pregnancy test, irrespective of reported contraceptive use.

Article audio
Never miss: Ectopic pregnancy when amenorrhoea accompanies abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, shoulder-tip pain or collapse; pituitary apoplexy with severe headache or visual deterioration; significant malnutrition/bradycardia; and rapidly progressive virilisation suggesting an androgen-secreting tumour. A negative urine pregnancy test does not safely rule out early pregnancy when clinical suspicion remains high.

1. Definitions and why they matter

  • Primary amenorrhoea: absence of menstruation by age 15, or more than 3 years after breast development; earlier assessment is appropriate when secondary sexual characteristics are absent by about age 13 or obstruction is suspected. Local paediatric pathways may use slightly different age thresholds.
  • Secondary amenorrhoea: absence of menses for at least 3 months after previously regular cycles or at least 6 months after irregular cycles.
  • Oligomenorrhoea: infrequent periods; investigate persistent cycle disturbance as clinically appropriate.
  • Physiological causes: pregnancy, lactation and menopause; hormonal contraception can also suppress bleeding without underlying disease.

2. Physiology: map the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis

Pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH drives anterior pituitary FSH and LH. FSH promotes follicular growth; LH supports ovulation and corpus luteum formation. Ovarian oestradiol proliferates endometrium, while progesterone stabilises secretory endometrium after ovulation. Loss of GnRH pulsatility, hyperprolactinaemia, ovarian follicle depletion or absence/outflow obstruction of the uterus can each remove bleeding through a distinct mechanism.

3. History: ask questions that change diagnosis

  • Pregnancy possibility: sexual history sensitively, contraception, last menstrual period, pain/bleeding and prior ectopic risk.
  • Puberty: thelarche and pubarche age, growth, primary amenorrhoea, cyclic pelvic pain and family pubertal history.
  • Energy balance: rapid weight loss, restricted eating, training volume, psychosocial stress and chronic disease.
  • Hyperandrogenism: hirsutism, acne, scalp hair changes, rapid onset, voice deepening or clitoromegaly.
  • Hypo-oestrogenism: hot flushes, vaginal dryness, reduced libido, fragility fractures and infertility concerns.
  • Pituitary: galactorrhoea, headache, visual field symptoms and use of dopamine-antagonist medicines.
  • Other endocrine: thyroid symptoms, Cushing features and prior chemotherapy or pelvic surgery.
  • Medication/contraception: progestogen implant/injection, levonorgestrel IUS, combined contraception, antipsychotics, opioids.

4. Examination: targeted and respectful

Record vital signs, BMI or weight trend, nutritional state, growth chart when appropriate, thyroid signs, visual fields if pituitary symptoms, galactorrhoea when relevant, skin/acne/hirsutism, signs of virilisation and features of Turner syndrome. With consent and an appropriate chaperone, pelvic examination is reserved for relevant symptoms and should not automatically be performed in adolescents. External genital examination and imaging may help suspected outflow obstruction; discuss sensitively.

7. Managing the common causes

  • Pregnancy: provide appropriate antenatal or pregnancy-options counselling; pain/bleeding with possible ectopic pregnancy needs urgent assessment.
  • Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea: restore energy availability, assess eating-disorder risk, involve dietetics/mental health when needed and protect bone health. Avoid offering combined hormonal contraception solely to mask the problem.
  • PCOS: address individual priorities—cycle regulation, endometrial protection, metabolic risk, acne/hirsutism and fertility; offer lifestyle care without stigma.
  • Hyperprolactinaemia: treat cause, review medicines with prescriber and refer when persistent/symptomatic; dopamine agonists are specialist treatment for prolactinoma.
  • Premature ovarian insufficiency: confirm per NICE NG23 and counsel about fertility, bone/cardiovascular health, and sex-steroid replacement if appropriate; menopausal hormone therapy is not contraception.
  • Primary amenorrhoea/outflow disorders: seek paediatric/adolescent gynaecology or endocrine expertise; genetics and imaging should be individualised.

8. Five worked PLAB cases

Case 1 — Pregnancy first

A 26-year-old with 8 weeks of absent menses has mild spotting and unilateral pelvic pain.

Reasoning and action: Do β-hCG and urgent early-pregnancy assessment, including transvaginal ultrasound where appropriate. Do not assume a benign hormonal cause; ectopic pregnancy is a potentially fatal diagnosis.

Case 2 — Energy deficiency

A 20-year-old distance runner lost 9 kg, has no menses for 5 months and a low resting pulse.

Reasoning and action: Consider functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea / relative energy deficiency, assess haemodynamic and eating-disorder risk, check pregnancy and hormones, and arrange multidisciplinary treatment.

Case 3 — PCOS

A 23-year-old has irregular cycles since adolescence, acne and gradual facial hair growth.

Reasoning and action: Exclude pregnancy, thyroid and prolactin disorders; consider PCOS criteria and assess metabolic risk and priorities including fertility and endometrial protection.

Case 4 — POI

A 34-year-old has 6 months of amenorrhoea, hot flushes and vaginal dryness. FSH is elevated.

Reasoning and action: Consider POI; NICE requires elevated FSH on two samples 4–6 weeks apart for diagnosis in under-40s. Arrange specialist counselling about hormone therapy and bone/fertility implications.

Case 5 — Primary with obstruction

A 15-year-old has normal breast development, monthly severe lower abdominal pain but has never menstruated.

Reasoning and action: Urgently consider genital outflow tract obstruction such as imperforate hymen; pelvic ultrasound and adolescent gynaecology review are appropriate.

9. PLAB 2 consultation structure

Open with a non-judgemental explanation and consent for sensitive questions. Clarify patient goals (pregnancy planning, concerns about fertility, body image, pain or long-term health), screen safely for pregnancy and emergency symptoms, explain targeted tests and uncertainty, agree follow-up, and safety-net urgently for pelvic pain with collapse/bleeding, severe headache or new visual changes.

Deep Dive · reproductive endocrinology

Why periods stop: mechanisms and diagnostic nuance

1. Pulsatility is fundamental

GnRH is released in pulses; continuous stimulation does not reproduce normal pituitary signalling. Energy deficiency, stress and chronic illness can alter hypothalamic signalling, often producing low or inappropriately normal gonadotropins and hypo-oestrogenism. This can happen without extreme low BMI, so assess trends and clinical state.

2. The ovarian feedback loop

Declining ovarian follicular function reduces oestradiol and inhibin B feedback, increasing FSH. Persistent high FSH in the correct context supports ovarian insufficiency. Hormonal medication can distort results. NICE NG23 specifies two elevated FSH samples separated by 4–6 weeks for people under 40 being evaluated for POI; alternative specialist consensus criteria may differ.

3. Why prolactin disrupts cycles

Elevated prolactin interferes with hypothalamic reproductive signalling. Antipsychotics may elevate prolactin through dopamine D2 receptor blockade; hypothyroidism can do so via increased TRH. Never stop essential psychiatric treatment abruptly: coordinate medication review with the treating clinician.

4. PCOS is not simply an ultrasound finding

PCOS involves variable androgen excess, ovulatory disturbance and sometimes polycystic ovarian morphology. Diagnosis requires age-appropriate criteria and exclusion of alternative endocrine conditions. Persistent anovulation can expose the endometrium to unopposed oestrogen, so cycle/endometrial protection matters even when fertility is not currently desired.

5. Anatomy changes the differential

A person may have ovaries and secondary sexual development but no menstrual flow because of an outflow obstruction or congenital uterine anomaly. Cyclic pain in primary amenorrhoea is a key clue to retained menstrual blood. Normal breasts with absent uterus requires specialist differentiation, including MRKH syndrome and androgen insensitivity.

6. Misleading patterns and pitfalls

  • A normal BMI does not exclude energy deficiency or an eating disorder.
  • A negative urine pregnancy test may be misleading early or with dilute urine; use serum β-hCG when clinical suspicion persists.
  • FSH/LH levels can be suppressed by hormonal contraception; do not interpret uncritically.
  • AMH is not a stand-alone diagnostic test for POI; fertility and ovarian reserve are not identical concepts.
  • HRT used for ovarian insufficiency is not reliable contraception.
  • With severe malnutrition, bradycardia or electrolyte abnormalities, medical stabilisation precedes routine infertility investigations.

7. Shared decision-making and patient goals

Absent periods may be welcome while using contraception, distressing when trying to conceive or frightening in adolescence. Ask what the patient wants to know, explain the likely uncertainty, discuss pregnancy plans without assumptions, and consider psychological wellbeing alongside clinical and long-term endocrine outcomes.
