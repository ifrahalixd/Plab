Abnormal urinalysis: make the result answer a clinical question

A urine dipstick is a screening and decision-support tool, not an independent diagnosis. A positive result must be interpreted with the person's symptoms, hydration, collection quality, medications, kidney function and risk of serious disease. Treat the patient, not the strip.

Article audio
60-second PLAB summary: Nitrite/leucocyte positivity supports infection when symptoms fit; it does not automatically justify antibiotics in an asymptomatic adult. Positive blood requires confirmation and risk assessment, particularly persistent visible haematuria. Protein on dipstick requires quantitative urine albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR), eGFR and blood-pressure review. Glucose suggests hyperglycaemia or renal glycosuria; ketones raise concern for starvation or diabetes-related ketoacidosis in the right setting. Blood + protein + hypertension + falling eGFR can indicate glomerular disease. Recognise sepsis, obstruction, AKI and DKA immediately.

2. Focused history and contextual clues

  1. Why was urine tested? Dysuria, frequency, urgency, fever, loin pain, haematuria, oedema, pregnancy, diabetes review or incidental screen?
  2. Symptoms of upper tract infection: fever, rigors, flank pain, vomiting, systemic illness; ask about pregnancy and urinary obstruction.
  3. Haematuria: visible red/brown urine, clots, timing in stream, associated pain, recurrent episodes, smoking history, malignancy risk and prior UTI treatment.
  4. Renal symptoms: frothy urine, ankle/facial oedema, reduced urine output, hypertension, rash, arthralgia, recent sore throat or systemic inflammatory disease.
  5. Drugs and confounders: anticoagulants do not exclude malignancy; NSAIDs can affect kidneys; SGLT2 medicines can cause glycosuria and euglycaemic DKA; menstrual blood or recent exercise can confound testing.
  6. Previous kidney function and family history: diabetes, chronic kidney disease, polycystic kidneys, Alport syndrome, recurrent stones or nephritis.

3. Examination and triage

Assess appearance, temperature, HR, BP, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, hydration and conscious state. Consider capillary glucose immediately if unwell. Look for suprapubic and costovertebral tenderness, oedema, rash, joint signs, pulmonary oedema and evidence of bladder retention. In pregnancy or children, use the relevant age/pregnancy-specific pathway.

4. Red flags: when the strip must not delay treatment

Urgent hospital assessment: sepsis with suspected urinary source; fever with obstructing stone/anuria; AKI or marked oliguria; hyperkalaemia; severe hypertension with acute organ injury; blood/protein with rapidly worsening renal function and suspected glomerulonephritis; symptomatic DKA including possible euglycaemic DKA; pregnancy with systemic UTI symptoms. Do not delay stabilisation for repeat dipsticks.

Cancer recognition: NICE NG12 recommends a suspected-cancer pathway for people 45 and over with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or visible haematuria persisting or recurring after successful treatment of UTI. For adults 60 and over, unexplained non-visible haematuria with dysuria or a raised white-cell count meets bladder-cancer referral criteria. Assess individual risk and current guidance.

6. Practical management and follow-up

UTI: Decide infection probability from symptoms and guideline-directed testing. Culture before antibiotics where appropriate without delaying sepsis treatment; check prior cultures and antimicrobial advice, renal function and allergies. Consider alternative causes of dysuria including STI, vaginitis and prostatitis.

CKD and albuminuria: NICE NG203 recommends urine ACR over PCR for initial detection of low-level proteinuria. Confirm ACR 3–70 mg/mmol with a subsequent early morning sample; initial ACR ≥70 mg/mmol need not be repeated to confirm. Evaluate BP, eGFR trends and renal/cardiovascular risk. Referral depends on thresholds, KFRE and associated signs; don't use a protein dipstick alone to stage CKD.

Isolated non-visible haematuria: assess for infection, contamination and renal/urological causes. NICE NG203 recommends annual monitoring of haematuria, ACR/proteinuria, GFR and BP when persistent unexplained haematuria exists without proteinuria, alongside appropriate malignancy evaluation.

7. Common PLAB traps

Never equate: nitrite-positive urine with definite symptomatic UTI; leucocytes with an antibiotic indication; blood-positive dipstick with proven red cells; protein-positive dipstick with a measured ACR; urine ketones with DKA without acid-base evaluation. Never dismiss painless visible haematuria because of warfarin/apixaban, or assume a young person's urinary abnormalities are harmless without reviewing context.

8. Five worked clinical cases

Case 1 — painless visible haematuria: A 67-year-old smoker has red urine, no dysuria or fever. Assess promptly and refer by the suspected-cancer pathway; do not simply give antibiotics.
Case 2 — incidental protein: A 45-year-old with diabetes has 2+ protein on dipstick. Obtain laboratory ACR, eGFR, BP and previous results, then manage CKD risk according to NG203.
Case 3 — dipstick with confusion: An 84-year-old without urinary symptoms has leucocyte positivity and confusion. Investigate delirium causes; urine dipstick alone cannot establish symptomatic UTI, especially in older people.
Case 4 — swollen eyes after illness: A child has cola-coloured urine, oedema and hypertension after infection. Consider nephritic syndrome; check renal function, urine protein and urgent paediatric assessment.
Case 5 — vomiting on SGLT2 inhibitor: A patient taking empagliflozin has vomiting, tachypnoea and ketones, with only mildly raised glucose. Obtain blood ketones and venous gas urgently to exclude euglycaemic DKA.

9. PLAB 2 station: explain unexpected blood/protein in the urine

Candidate task: A 56-year-old has a positive urine dipstick for blood found during a routine check. Explain the uncertainty and next steps. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, ask why urine was tested, explore visible blood, dysuria, flank pain, fevers, medications, periods and smoking, discuss targeted testing and when urology referral is indicated; explain the result does not by itself prove cancer.

Pattern-based DDx

  1. Nitrite and/or leucocytes: symptoms first; UTI, inflammation, STI or contamination; assess specimen quality and culture indications.
  2. Blood: distinguish visible from non-visible; consider cancer criteria, stone, trauma, glomerulonephritis and pigmenturia.
  3. Protein: measure ACR and consider CKD, diabetes, glomerular disease or transient causes.
  4. Blood + protein: prioritise BP, renal function and nephritic syndrome if acute.
  5. Glucose and/or ketones: check blood glucose and consider DKA, fasting and medication effects.
Safety: suspected urosepsis, infected obstructed kidney, rapidly progressive renal disease, metabolic acidosis or dangerous potassium abnormalities are emergencies. An isolated laboratory finding should never replace assessment of symptoms and observations.

Deep Dive · nephron physiology, diagnostic accuracy and clinical interpretation

Deep Dive: where do urinary abnormalities originate?

1. Glomerular filtration and albumin

The glomerular filtration barrier restricts albumin passage through endothelial, basement-membrane and podocyte structures. Diabetes, immune-mediated inflammation and hypertension can damage the barrier, producing albuminuria even before substantial eGFR reduction. NICE recommends laboratory ACR because a standard dipstick misses low-grade albuminuria.

2. Why ACR is better than concentration alone

A urine protein concentration varies with hydration. Dividing albumin by creatinine partly corrects for variable urine concentration, enabling a spot sample to approximate albumin excretion. ACR is interpreted in mg/mmol in the UK. CKD categories are A1 (<3), A2 (3–30) and A3 (>30 mg/mmol); eGFR and chronicity also determine diagnosis and risk.

3. Dipstick chemistry and false positives

Blood reagent detects haem-related peroxidase activity; it cannot reliably distinguish red cells from free haemoglobin or myoglobin. Leucocyte esterase identifies an enzyme from white cells; nitrite relies on bacteria reducing urinary nitrates, which needs appropriate organisms and bladder dwell time. Thus false negatives and positives occur. A poorly collected sample can contaminate the result.

4. Where kidney-related blood comes from

Glomerular bleeding may coexist with albuminuria, hypertension or renal impairment; microscopy may reveal dysmorphic red cells or casts. Nonglomerular causes include stones, tumours, infection and structural damage. Visible haematuria demands careful risk assessment regardless of whether pain accompanies it.

5. Renal glucose handling and SGLT2 inhibitors

Glucose is filtered then mainly reabsorbed proximally. Hyperglycaemia can overwhelm transport, causing glycosuria; SGLT2 inhibitors deliberately reduce glucose reabsorption, causing glucose-positive urine even with non-markedly elevated blood glucose. These medicines also increase the need to recognise euglycaemic DKA in symptomatic patients.

6. Ketone physiology

Fasting or insulin deficiency promotes lipolysis and ketogenesis. Urine dipsticks largely detect acetoacetate and may underestimate β-hydroxybutyrate during acute ketoacidosis. Blood ketones and venous blood gas are clinically more relevant for suspected DKA; starvation ketosis and DKA differ in metabolic context and degree of acidosis.

7. Infection vs colonisation

Pyuria or bacterial growth does not always equal symptomatic UTI. Asymptomatic bacteriuria becomes common in older and catheterised patients. Antibiotic stewardship requires clinical symptoms and specific exceptions, rather than automatic treatment of a positive dipstick.

8. Clinical integration

Follow the chain: symptom or incidental finding → identify emergency signs → interpret urine result → choose confirmatory laboratory testing and renal function assessment → decide treatment or referral → arrange monitoring and safety-netting. This avoids both missing high-risk disease and unnecessary antibiotics or scans.
