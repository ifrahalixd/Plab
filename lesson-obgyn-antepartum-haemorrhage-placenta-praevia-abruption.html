Antepartum Haemorrhage: Placenta Praevia & Placental Abruption

Emergency: Significant bleeding in later pregnancy needs immediate obstetric assessment. Never perform a digital vaginal examination until placenta praevia has been excluded.

Definition and triage

Antepartum haemorrhage (APH) is bleeding from or into the genital tract from 24+0 weeks of pregnancy until birth. Bleeding earlier in pregnancy follows an early-pregnancy pathway. Treat significant bleeding as a potential obstetric emergency.

PLAB application: Call for senior obstetric help early; assess maternal stability before diagnostic labels.

Major causes

Important causes include placenta praevia, placental abruption, vasa praevia, uterine rupture and local cervical or vaginal causes. A normal-looking amount of vaginal bleeding does not exclude concealed abruption.

PLAB application: Use history and examination to distinguish causes, but never delay resuscitation.

Immediate ABCDE assessment

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure; monitor pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and consciousness. Position with left uterine displacement where appropriate, obtain large-bore IV access, send bloods and arrange crossmatch. Activate major haemorrhage protocol for severe bleeding.

PLAB application: Maternal resuscitation comes first; fetal wellbeing is assessed in parallel.

Placenta praevia

Placenta praevia means placenta covering the internal cervical os; low-lying placenta is near but not covering the os. Classically causes painless, bright-red recurrent vaginal bleeding in the second half of pregnancy, though pain can occur.

PLAB application: Never perform a digital vaginal examination until placenta praevia has been excluded.

Placenta localisation

Transabdominal ultrasound assesses placental position; transvaginal ultrasound is safe and more accurate for confirming low-lying placenta or praevia. A low-lying placenta on the mid-pregnancy scan often resolves as the uterus grows; repeat imaging is arranged.

PLAB application: Ultrasound can identify praevia but a normal scan cannot reliably exclude abruption.

Placental abruption

Premature separation of a normally sited placenta can cause abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, a tense or hypertonic uterus, vaginal bleeding, fetal distress and maternal shock. Bleeding may be concealed.

PLAB application: Consider abruption when abdominal pain and fetal compromise occur even with little visible blood.

Risk factors

Praevia is associated with previous caesarean delivery, prior praevia, multiparity and assisted reproduction. Abruption risk factors include hypertension, pre-eclampsia, smoking, trauma and previous abruption. Risk factors do not confirm diagnosis.

PLAB application: Ask about previous uterine surgery, hypertension and trauma.

Vasa praevia

Unprotected fetal vessels run through membranes near the cervix. Membrane rupture may tear vessels, causing bleeding with rapid fetal compromise; maternal observations can remain stable. This is an immediate obstetric emergency.

PLAB application: Bleeding after rupture of membranes with fetal bradycardia suggests vasa praevia.

Uterine rupture

Suspect with sudden abdominal pain, abnormal fetal heart rate, bleeding, maternal shock or loss of fetal station, especially in labour after previous uterine surgery. Presentation varies.

PLAB application: Escalate immediately for emergency operative management.

History and examination

Ask gestation, onset, amount and colour of bleeding, pain, contractions, trauma, membrane rupture, fetal movements, prior scans and placental position, caesarean history, medicines and blood group. Examine abdomen gently and use speculum examination only when clinically appropriate and safe.

PLAB application: Avoid digital vaginal examination until praevia is excluded.

Investigations

Obtain full blood count, group and save or crossmatch, coagulation tests and fibrinogen in significant bleeding; consider renal function and blood gases according to severity. Perform ultrasound for placental location and fetal assessment. Cardiotocography is used when gestational age and clinical context support it.

PLAB application: Initial haemoglobin may underestimate acute blood loss; reassess clinically and repeat tests.

Maternal and fetal complications

Maternal risks include haemorrhagic shock, coagulopathy, transfusion, hysterectomy and death. Fetal risks include hypoxia, growth restriction in some placental disorders, prematurity and stillbirth. Abruption can cause consumptive coagulopathy.

PLAB application: Monitor maternal circulation and fetal heart rate; involve neonatal team when preterm birth is likely.

Management of placenta praevia

Care is individualised according to bleeding, stability, gestation and placental position. Involve obstetrics, anaesthesia and blood bank. Stable cases may be monitored with a planned caesarean birth; significant bleeding can require urgent delivery.

PLAB application: Do not assume every bleed mandates immediate delivery; stability and specialist assessment matter.

Management of abruption

Resuscitate, prepare blood products when indicated and involve senior obstetric and anaesthetic teams. Delivery timing and mode depend on maternal condition, fetal status and labour progress. Severe abruption or fetal compromise may require emergency birth.

PLAB application: Abruption is principally a clinical diagnosis; do not wait for ultrasound confirmation.

Anti-D and rhesus care

For RhD-negative, non-sensitised pregnant patients, APH may be a sensitising event requiring anti-D immunoglobulin according to gestation and local protocol. Fetomaternal haemorrhage testing may guide additional dosing.

PLAB application: Check blood group and follow local anti-D guidance; do not delay emergency resuscitation.

Communication and safety-netting

Explain uncertainty, the need for hospital assessment and monitoring, and possible emergency delivery. Provide compassionate counselling, ask about fetal movements and give explicit return advice for recurrent bleeding, pain, contractions, dizziness or reduced movements.

PLAB application: PLAB 2: acknowledge anxiety, explain why vaginal examination may be deferred and use teach-back.

PLAB 2 — Late-pregnancy bleeding consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm gestation, assess immediate stability and obtain a focused bleeding and pain history. Ask about fetal movements, previous scans, placenta position and caesarean deliveries. Explain the need for urgent obstetric review, blood tests and ultrasound. Discuss avoiding digital examination until praevia is excluded. Acknowledge anxiety, explain possible outcomes and provide safety-net advice.

Deep Dive

Praevia versus abruption

Praevia classically causes painless, bright-red bleeding and a non-tender uterus. Abruption more often causes pain, tenderness, uterine hypertonus and fetal compromise. Neither pattern is absolute: clinical features overlap and both can cause severe haemorrhage.

Why ultrasound does not rule out abruption

Retroplacental blood may be difficult to see. Abruption is therefore primarily diagnosed from maternal symptoms, abdominal findings, fetal status and the overall clinical picture. Never delay urgent treatment to obtain a positive scan.

Placenta accreta spectrum

Placenta accreta spectrum describes abnormal placental adherence or invasion. It is more likely when praevia coexists with a previous caesarean scar and can cause catastrophic bleeding at delivery. Refer for specialist imaging and planned multidisciplinary birth.

Coagulopathy in abruption

Severe abruption can trigger disseminated intravascular coagulation. Falling fibrinogen, prolonged clotting times and thrombocytopenia may develop. Coordinate with blood bank and use local obstetric major haemorrhage protocols.

Timing of birth

Management depends on maternal haemodynamics, ongoing bleeding, fetal status, gestational age and placental findings. Stable praevia may permit planned caesarean delivery, while major bleeding, unstable abruption or fetal compromise may require urgent delivery.

PLAB 2 communication

Ask permission, explain the rationale for avoiding a digital examination until praevia is excluded, address fears about the baby, communicate uncertainty and involve senior obstetric staff. Give specific safety-net advice.
