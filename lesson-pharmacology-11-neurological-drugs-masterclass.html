Neurological Pharmacology

Antiseizure medicines, Parkinson disease, migraine and neuropathic pain: mechanisms, clinical decisions and prescribing safety.

Neurological Pharmacology · Deep Dive

Membrane excitability

The resting membrane potential depends chiefly on potassium gradients and selective membrane permeability. Voltage-gated sodium channels generate the rising phase of the action potential; sodium-channel blockers preferentially stabilise inactive states during high-frequency firing. Their actions differ across syndromes, and some sodium-channel blockers can worsen certain generalised epilepsies.

GABA and glutamate

GABA-A receptors are ligand-gated chloride channels. Benzodiazepines enhance the frequency of channel opening in the presence of GABA, rapidly increasing inhibition; this explains their emergency role and risk of respiratory depression. Glutamate acts at AMPA and NMDA receptors; excessive excitatory synchrony contributes to seizure propagation.

SV2A and calcium channels

Levetiracetam binds SV2A on synaptic vesicles. Gabapentin and pregabalin bind alpha-2-delta auxiliary calcium-channel subunits; neither directly stimulates GABA-A receptors. These distinctions explain why names and molecular mechanisms can be misleading.

Phenytoin nonlinear kinetics

At low concentrations, elimination approximates first-order kinetics. As metabolising enzymes saturate, small dose increases can cause large concentration rises. Total phenytoin can misrepresent active free drug in hypoalbuminaemia or uraemia; specialist interpretation is necessary.

Basal ganglia circuitry

Dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta facilitates D1 direct-pathway output and suppresses D2 indirect-pathway output, generally promoting movement. Dopamine loss increases inhibitory thalamic output and impairs movement initiation. Dopaminergic replacement improves motor symptoms but can induce dyskinesia and hallucinations.

Triptan physiology

Triptans stimulate 5-HT1B/1D receptors, inhibiting trigeminal neuropeptide release and affecting cranial vessels. Vascular contraindications matter. Migraine aura is thought to involve cortical spreading depolarisation; not every headache with visual symptoms is benign migraine.

Neuropathic pain physiology

Peripheral nerve injury can cause ectopic firing, sodium-channel redistribution and central sensitisation. Noradrenergic descending inhibition helps explain the benefit of some antidepressants, while alpha-2-delta ligands reduce transmitter release. Outcomes should be assessed with pain intensity, sleep, function and adverse effects.

Clinical calculations and monitoring

Renal clearance affects levetiracetam, gabapentin and pregabalin dosing. Use the drug-specific BNF renal metric and current creatinine, not a one-size-fits-all eGFR rule. Consider interactions between enzyme-inducing antiseizure medicines and contraception, anticoagulants and other drugs. Check appropriate blood counts, liver tests, sodium and levels only where indicated.

Emergency prioritisation

A prolonged convulsion is time-critical. ABCDE, capillary glucose and benzodiazepine treatment should proceed together with escalation. A patient with Parkinson disease who cannot swallow needs an urgent alternative medication plan; missed doses may produce a severe withdrawal syndrome.

PLAB exam traps

Do not confuse gabapentin with a direct GABA agonist; do not treat a 6-minute convulsion as a routine self-limiting seizure; do not prescribe valproate without considering current MHRA restrictions; do not use metoclopramide casually in Parkinson disease; do not dismiss lamotrigine rash with mucosal symptoms.

• Lamotrigine requires slow titration; rash with fever or mucosal lesions is an emergency.

• Valproate has major reproductive risks and strict current UK prescribing safeguards.

1. Neurological prescribing: the clinical framework

Begin with a diagnosis, not a drug. Identify the syndrome, urgency, pregnancy potential, comorbidities, renal and hepatic function, drug interactions and patient goals. For seizures, distinguish provoked seizures from epilepsy and classify focal versus generalised onset. For headache, screen for thunderclap onset, meningism, new neurological deficit, papilloedema and other red flags before prescribing migraine therapy. For neuropathic pain, look for a treatable cause such as diabetes, shingles or nerve compression.

2. Neuronal signalling and drug targets

An action potential arises from voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels. Calcium influx triggers vesicular neurotransmitter release. Glutamate mediates most fast excitation through AMPA and NMDA receptors, whereas GABA-A receptor activation increases chloride conductance and generally inhibits neuronal firing. Antiseizure drugs may stabilise sodium channels, enhance GABA signalling, bind synaptic vesicle protein SV2A or modulate calcium-channel subunits. A drug mechanism alone does not establish its suitability for a seizure type.

3. Epilepsy: diagnosis, initial treatment and safety

After a suspected first seizure, assess airway and injuries, obtain a witness history, check capillary glucose, review medications and arrange appropriate specialist evaluation. EEG and neuroimaging are guided by the clinical assessment; a normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy. Treatment selection depends on seizure type, syndrome, age, interactions and pregnancy risk. NICE NG217 provides seizure-specific options. Explain adherence, sleep, alcohol, driving restrictions and local DVLA rules. Abruptly stopping regular antiseizure medicines can precipitate seizures.

4. Lamotrigine

Lamotrigine blocks voltage-sensitive sodium channels and reduces excitatory transmitter release. It is used for several focal and generalised epilepsies, but suitability varies by syndrome. Titrate slowly because serious rash, including Stevens–Johnson syndrome, is a potentially life-threatening adverse effect. Valproate inhibits lamotrigine metabolism and substantially raises lamotrigine exposure, requiring a different titration schedule. Oestrogen-containing contraceptives may lower lamotrigine levels, and levels can change in pregnancy. Seek urgent review for rash with mucosal lesions, fever or systemic illness.

5. Levetiracetam

Levetiracetam binds synaptic vesicle protein SV2A and is used for several seizure types. It has relatively few CYP-mediated interactions, but dose adjustment is needed in renal impairment. Irritability, agitation, mood changes and behavioural adverse effects are important counselling points; assess psychiatric history and ask about new suicidal thoughts. It is not automatically the correct choice for every epilepsy syndrome.

6. Sodium valproate and reproductive safety

Valproate increases GABA-related inhibitory activity and has other actions; it is effective for several generalised epilepsies. It carries substantial fetal malformation and neurodevelopmental risks. UK MHRA restrictions apply to starting valproate in people younger than 55, with additional reproductive-risk requirements and specialist oversight. Do not initiate or change valproate without checking current MHRA rules, the Pregnancy Prevention Programme where applicable and specialist guidance. Never advise a patient to stop established treatment abruptly; urgent specialist review is safer. Other adverse effects include weight gain, tremor, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis.

7. Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine and phenytoin

Carbamazepine is a sodium-channel blocker used for focal epilepsy and trigeminal neuralgia; enzyme induction causes important interactions, including reduced effectiveness of some hormonal contraception. It may cause hyponatraemia, blood dyscrasias and severe cutaneous reactions; genetic risk varies by ancestry. Oxcarbazepine can also cause hyponatraemia. Phenytoin has nonlinear saturable metabolism and a narrow therapeutic index: small dose changes may cause disproportionate concentration increases. Toxicity may present with nystagmus, ataxia, confusion and dysarthria. Drug levels must be interpreted with albumin, timing and clinical context.

8. Status epilepticus: emergency sequence

A convulsive seizure lasting 5 minutes or recurrent seizures without recovery is an emergency. Call for help, protect from injury, assess ABCDE, oxygenation and glucose, establish IV access where possible, and give a protocol-approved benzodiazepine promptly (e.g. IV lorazepam if access and facilities allow, or buccal midazolam according to the care plan). If convulsions continue, use an appropriate second-line IV antiseizure medicine under the local emergency protocol and senior guidance. Monitor airway and respiratory depression. Refractory status needs critical care and anaesthetic expertise. Do not delay emergency treatment while awaiting blood tests.

9. Parkinson disease: dopamine and basal ganglia

Degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons leads to bradykinesia, rigidity and resting tremor, alongside non-motor symptoms. Dopamine normally modulates direct and indirect basal ganglia pathways. Treatment is individualised according to motor impact, age, cognitive status and adverse effects. Levodopa offers the strongest symptomatic motor benefit but can produce wearing-off and dyskinesia with long-term treatment.

10. Levodopa, carbidopa and benserazide

Levodopa crosses the blood–brain barrier and is converted to dopamine in the brain. Peripheral dopa-decarboxylase inhibitors such as carbidopa or benserazide reduce peripheral conversion, improving CNS delivery and limiting nausea and hypotension. Monitor postural hypotension, hallucinations, confusion, dyskinesia and motor fluctuations. In hospital, Parkinson medicines are time-critical: missed or delayed doses can cause severe immobility and potentially a neuroleptic malignant-like syndrome. Avoid abrupt withdrawal.

11. Dopamine agonists, MAO-B and COMT inhibitors

Pramipexole and ropinirole stimulate dopamine receptors; adverse effects include sleep attacks, hallucinations, oedema and impulse-control disorders (gambling, shopping, hypersexuality). Rasagiline and selegiline inhibit MAO-B, reducing dopamine breakdown; assess serotonergic drug interactions. Entacapone inhibits peripheral COMT and prolongs levodopa effect; it may increase dyskinesia and cause diarrhoea or orange-brown urine. Anticholinergics are rarely preferred in older adults because of cognitive and urinary adverse effects.

12. Parkinson medicines and drugs to avoid

Dopamine-blocking antiemetics such as metoclopramide and prochlorperazine can worsen parkinsonism and are generally avoided. Antipsychotics can also worsen motor function; seek specialist advice when psychosis occurs. Assess swallowing and appropriate formulations if the patient is nil by mouth. A missed Parkinson dose is not a routine scheduling inconvenience: escalate and use an approved local alternative-route plan.

13. Migraine diagnosis and acute treatment

Migraine is usually episodic headache with features such as nausea, photophobia or phonophobia, sometimes preceded by reversible aura. For acute attacks, NICE guidance supports an oral triptan plus an NSAID or paracetamol where appropriate, with an antiemetic if needed. Triptans are 5-HT1B/1D agonists that inhibit trigeminovascular signalling and cause cranial vasoconstriction. They are contraindicated in some vascular diseases and should not be used for an undiagnosed thunderclap headache. Check contraindications, pregnancy status and interactions.

14. Migraine prevention and medication-overuse headache

Consider preventive therapy for frequent or disabling attacks after reviewing triggers, sleep and medication use. Options may include propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline depending on the patient and current guidance; specialist options include CGRP-targeted treatments and botulinum toxin for selected chronic migraine. Topiramate has important pregnancy-related restrictions and requires counselling about cognition, paraesthesia, weight loss, kidney stones and contraception interactions. Frequent acute analgesics or triptans may cause medication-overuse headache; discuss withdrawal and preventive strategies.

15. Neuropathic pain: first-line choices

Neuropathic pain is burning, electric, shooting or allodynic pain from a lesion or disease of the somatosensory system. NICE CG173 offers amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin as initial options for most neuropathic pain (trigeminal neuralgia is an exception). Choose based on comorbidities and adverse-effect profile. Set realistic goals: improved function and partial pain reduction rather than guaranteed complete relief. Review benefit, sedation, falls, driving safety and misuse potential.

16. Gabapentin and pregabalin

These bind the alpha-2-delta subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing excitatory neurotransmitter release; they do not act directly as GABA receptor agonists. Adjust doses for renal impairment. Adverse effects include dizziness, somnolence, oedema and ataxia. Co-prescription with opioids or other sedatives increases risk of respiratory depression. Both are controlled drugs in the UK (Schedule 3), with relevant prescribing requirements. Taper rather than abruptly discontinue after sustained use.

17. Amitriptyline and duloxetine

Amitriptyline inhibits serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake and has antimuscarinic and antihistaminic effects; it may cause dry mouth, constipation, sedation, postural hypotension and cardiac conduction problems. Use caution in older adults, glaucoma and cardiac disease. Duloxetine is an SNRI useful in selected neuropathic pain, particularly when depression coexists; watch for nausea, blood pressure changes, serotonin syndrome risk and discontinuation symptoms. Evaluate interactions before combining serotonergic medicines.

18. Trigeminal neuralgia and other special situations

Trigeminal neuralgia causes brief unilateral electric-shock-like facial pain triggered by light touch or chewing. Carbamazepine is a standard initial treatment, but assess contraindications, interactions and monitoring. Atypical features, sensory loss or young onset may warrant investigation for secondary causes. For postherpetic neuralgia, consider localised treatments where appropriate. Opioids are not routine first-line therapy for chronic neuropathic pain.

19. Clinical case 1: first seizure in a student

A 20-year-old has a witnessed tonic–clonic seizure and recovers fully. Check glucose and injuries, document witness account and possible triggers, and arrange urgent specialist assessment. Explain safety precautions and driving advice. Do not label epilepsy or prescribe lifelong therapy solely from a single event without appropriate evaluation.

20. Clinical case 2: rash after lamotrigine titration

A patient develops fever, painful rash and oral ulcers after a rapid lamotrigine increase. Suspect a severe cutaneous adverse reaction. Stop the suspected drug, arrange immediate emergency assessment, document and report the reaction, and involve specialists. Do not reassure or simply prescribe an antihistamine.

21. Clinical case 3: Parkinson inpatient missed doses

An 81-year-old with Parkinson disease is nil by mouth and has missed several levodopa doses; rigidity and swallowing worsen. Escalate urgently, reconcile the exact schedule, involve the Parkinson team and pharmacy, and use an approved alternative formulation or route plan. Avoid dopamine-blocking antiemetics.

22. Clinical case 4: painful diabetic neuropathy

A 62-year-old with burning feet and poor sleep requests morphine. Assess neuropathy and other causes, examine feet, optimise diabetes care, discuss a first-line neuropathic pain medicine and agree functional goals. Consider renal function, falls and sedating drugs; arrange follow-up and gradual dose adjustment.

23. PLAB 2 communication: antiseizure medicine counselling

Confirm the medicine, indication, dose and adherence; explore seizure frequency, driving, occupation and pregnancy intentions sensitively. Explain the most relevant adverse effects, interactions, missed-dose plan and urgent red flags. Discuss seizure first aid: protect the head, time the seizure, do not restrain or put anything in the mouth, and call emergency services for prolonged seizures or breathing concerns. Use teach-back and document shared decisions.

24. High-yield safety checklist

Before prescribing, ask: Is the diagnosis correct? Is this the right drug for this seizure type or neurological syndrome? What are the pregnancy and contraception implications? Are renal or hepatic adjustments needed? Is there a serious interaction? Does the drug require slow titration, blood tests or drug-level interpretation? What symptoms require urgent review? For current doses and restrictions, consult BNF, NICE, MHRA and local specialist protocols.

    NICE / BNF · Neurological prescribing pathways

    Teaching algorithms only. Verify current UK restrictions, specialist advice and local protocols.

    Suspected first seizure

    ABCDE and glucose → witness history, provoking causes and injuries → urgent specialist assessment → classify seizure type → individualised antiseizure treatment and driving/safety counselling.

    Convulsive status epilepticus

    Seizure ≥5 minutes or repeated without recovery → emergency call, ABCDE, glucose → protocol-approved benzodiazepine → second-line IV medicine and senior escalation if persistent → critical care for refractory seizures.

    Missed Parkinson medicines

    Reconcile exact doses and times → treat as time-critical → identify swallowing or nil-by-mouth barrier → contact Parkinson team/pharmacist → approved alternative route/formulation → avoid dopamine blockers.

    Neuropathic pain

    Confirm phenotype and treat causes → discuss amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin where appropriate → assess renal function, interactions, falls and misuse → titrate gradually → review function and adverse effects.
