Overdoses, antidotes, toxidromes and emergency management · Clinical Masterclass

Deep Dive · Molecular toxicology, kinetics and clinical reasoning

1 · Why dose, route, formulation and time change danger

Toxicity follows absorbed dose at target tissues, not simply the number of tablets. Modified-release products can cause delayed peaks. A lipid-soluble medicine may distribute into tissue and re-enter plasma long after ingestion. Saturable metabolism can shift a drug from apparent first-order elimination to nonlinear or capacity-limited kinetics, so small dose changes produce disproportionate concentrations. Kidney or liver impairment prolongs exposure. Enterohepatic recirculation, active metabolites and accumulation make a single early level potentially misleading. Serial clinical observations often predict danger better than an isolated reported dose.

2 · Receptor pharmacology and antidote logic

Naloxone competitively displaces agonists at opioid receptors, rapidly improving respiratory drive but not other sedatives. Flumazenil competitively blocks benzodiazepine action at the GABA-A benzodiazepine site, potentially unmasking proconvulsant co-ingestants or withdrawal. Atropine blocks muscarinic cholinergic effects but does not directly reverse nicotinic paralysis. Fomepizole inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing formation of formic or glycolic toxic metabolites. Acetylcysteine replenishes hepatic glutathione availability, enabling NAPQI detoxification. The mechanism determines when a drug is life-saving versus hazardous.

3 · Sodium channels, depolarisation and QRS widening

Fast ventricular sodium channels mediate phase-0 depolarisation. Tricyclics and certain other medicines slow conduction through channel blockade, prolonging QRS and predisposing to re-entry and hypotension. Acidaemia favours cardiotoxic binding effects. Sodium bicarbonate can improve conduction through extracellular sodium loading and alkalinisation, but requires careful ECG, potassium and pH monitoring. QRS prolongation suggests ventricular depolarisation delay, whereas QT prolongation raises concern about repolarisation and torsades. Both require immediate review, but interventions differ.

4 · Acid–base calculations: anion and osmolar gaps

A practical anion gap without potassium is Na − (Cl + HCO₃), using all values in mmol/L; normal range depends on laboratory and albumin. Example: Na 140, chloride 100 and bicarbonate 12 give a gap of 28 mmol/L, indicating accumulation of unmeasured anions. Low albumin can mask a raised gap. A measured osmolar gap estimates unmeasured osmotically active small molecules, but varies by formula and test; a normal gap does not exclude late methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning after conversion to acidic metabolites. Evaluate actual clinical context, not a single threshold.

5 · Salicylate kinetics and the danger of acidemia

Salicylate weak-acid ionisation depends on pH. As extracellular pH falls, relatively more nonionised drug enters the CNS. Alkalinising serum and urine helps keep salicylate ionised and increases renal clearance in selected cases; potassium repletion may be essential to achieve urine alkalinisation. Respiratory alkalosis can initially dominate, but toxicity worsens with metabolic acidosis. Intubation can be hazardous if ventilation becomes too low relative to the patient’s pre-intubation compensatory hyperventilation.

6 · Activated charcoal, dialysis and what they actually remove

Activated charcoal adsorbs many organic drugs in the gut before absorption, so it cannot reverse a drug already distributed into tissues. Its use is limited by time, binding properties, formulation and aspiration risk. Some metals, lithium and alcohols are poorly adsorbed. Haemodialysis is most useful for toxins with relatively low molecular mass, low volume of distribution, suitable protein binding characteristics, or metabolites whose removal corrects severe acid–base derangement. Lithium, salicylate and toxic alcohols are classic examples for selected severe cases, but toxin concentration alone is rarely the entire decision.

7 · Repeated exposure and special populations

Repeated supratherapeutic paracetamol dosing is not equivalent to a single acute overdose for nomogram interpretation. Older adults, young children, pregnant patients, individuals with malnutrition or those with kidney/liver dysfunction may require specialised assessment. Children can be exposed through small volumes of concentrated products; doses are interpreted by weight, formulation and time. The practical goal is to avoid both undertreatment and antidote complications through accurate history and current poison-specific guidance.

8 · Clinical communication and safe disposition

A good toxicology OSCE balances physical safety with sensitive questioning. Explore the patient’s account without blame, ask directly and respectfully about suicidal thoughts and intent, establish what support they want and explain tests and antidote decisions in accessible language. Have a low threshold to obtain senior advice where capacity is impaired by intoxication. Follow NICE NG225 for psychosocial assessment early and collaborative safety planning; do not label a patient “low risk” solely on a predictive score. A discharge checklist must cover medical observation, co-ingestions, return precautions, medication access, follow-up and the patient’s immediate safety.

1. The first ten minutes: a structured emergency response

Think physiological threats before a drug name. Airway obstruction from reduced consciousness, hypoventilation, arrhythmia, shock, seizures, aspiration and temperature disturbance can kill before laboratory confirmation. Resuscitate with an ABCDE approach, call for senior/critical care help early, and consider alternative or additional diagnoses such as intracranial haemorrhage, hypoglycaemia, sepsis and trauma.

Airway and breathing: assess protective reflexes, rate, tidal volume, capnography if available, oxygenation and blood gas. Give oxygen when appropriate and support ventilation; intubation may be needed for persistent coma, seizures or loss of airway protection. Pulse oximetry can appear reassuring in carbon monoxide poisoning. Circulation: obtain IV access, repeat blood pressure, ECG and fluid assessment; identify shock and conduction abnormalities. Disability: check capillary glucose immediately, pupils, GCS, seizure activity, tone and clonus. Exposure: temperature, skin moisture, patches, injection marks, possible chemical contamination.

Do not wait to start supportive care. Check what was taken, preparation, strength, dose, time and intention as soon as safe; seek packaging, dispensing records and witnesses. Staggered dosing, modified-release drugs, alcohol and multiple agents change risk. Document who supplied the history and how certain timing is.

2. History, investigations and hidden co-ingestions

Ask about substance, formulation, route, time, estimated maximum amount, co-ingestions, regular prescriptions, medical history, renal/liver disease, pregnancy possibility and symptoms. A reported dose may be inaccurate. Deliberate overdose may coexist with trauma, alcohol or non-prescription medicines. Do not let a plausible toxidrome exclude sepsis, stroke or metabolic illness.

Initial investigations commonly include 12-lead ECG (repeat if required), venous or arterial blood gas including pH, glucose and lactate, U&Es, creatinine, magnesium, liver tests and INR. Consider FBC, CK, pregnancy testing, salicylate concentration, measured osmolality and toxin-specific levels. In deliberate or unidentified poisoning, measure a paracetamol concentration even when it was not volunteered; follow timing advice. Some routine urine drug screens have significant false negatives/positives and rarely guide immediate resuscitation.

Interpret serial trends: a single normal ECG does not exclude evolving sodium-channel blockade, and levels of modified-release drugs may rise later. pH and anion gap can change over time. Only measure drug concentrations if they influence treatment or are indicated in guidance; a positive result is not automatically proof of clinically important toxicity.

3. Recognising toxidromes at the bedside

A toxidrome is a pattern of autonomic, neurological, pupillary, respiratory and cardiovascular findings caused by related toxic mechanisms. Mixed ingestions can obscure patterns, so use them to guide initial care without locking onto a single diagnosis.

Opioid: depressed consciousness, bradypnoea and often miosis (but pupils may vary). Sympathomimetic: agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, sweating and dilated pupils. Anticholinergic: agitation/delirium, mydriasis, dry flushed skin, urinary retention, reduced bowel sounds and tachycardia. Cholinergic: salivation, lacrimation, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, diarrhoea, urination, sweating and sometimes fasciculations.

Sedative-hypnotic: reduced conscious level and impaired coordination; severe respiratory depression suggests additional drugs or high-risk agents. Serotonin toxicity: clonus/hyperreflexia, agitation, sweating and hyperthermia, often after serotonergic dose increase/interaction. Examine for inducible/ocular clonus; compare with neuroleptic malignant syndrome, which usually evolves more slowly with severe rigidity.

4. Paracetamol overdose: why an initially well patient may be at risk

At normal doses paracetamol is mostly glucuronidated and sulfated. A small proportion is oxidised by CYP enzymes to the reactive metabolite NAPQI, detoxified by glutathione. In overdose the main conjugation routes become saturated, glutathione is depleted and NAPQI injures hepatocytes. A patient may initially have few symptoms while hepatocellular injury evolves over the following days.

Immediate-release, single, acute ingestion with known time: a plasma paracetamol concentration obtained at or after the appropriate post-ingestion interval (commonly at least four hours) is interpreted on the current UK treatment nomogram. Samples before four hours cannot reliably exclude subsequent toxicity. Unknown-time, staggered, repeated supratherapeutic or modified-release ingestion: do not apply the single-ingestion nomogram mechanically. Seek TOXBASE advice and use liver biochemistry/INR and repeat concentrations as directed.

IV acetylcysteine (NAC) restores detoxification capacity and markedly reduces hepatic injury when given promptly. If potentially significant overdose and delay to results would postpone treatment, follow local/NPIS advice on starting NAC before a level returns. Treatment regimens vary, including modern two-bag pathways; use the local approved administration chart and reassess markers before stopping. Anaphylactoid reactions can occur and are managed according to severity and protocol; never simply abandon necessary treatment.

PLAB trap: The standard paracetamol nomogram is not a universal overdose calculator. Staggered and unknown-time ingestion require a different risk assessment. Never reassure someone solely because they feel well at presentation.

5. Opioids, naloxone and observation after reversal

Opioid agonists act predominantly at μ-receptors, causing analgesia and brainstem respiratory depression. Morphine, heroin, methadone, oxycodone, fentanyl and other opioids differ greatly in potency and duration. Classic features are coma, slow or absent breathing and often pinpoint pupils, but mixed ingestions and hypoxia can alter the picture.

Open the airway, oxygenate and ventilate immediately. Use bag-mask ventilation when needed and call for resuscitation support. Naloxone competitively antagonises opioid receptors; in patients with opioid dependence, titrate to adequate breathing rather than aiming for full wakefulness, because abrupt reversal can precipitate acute withdrawal, agitation and vomiting. Routes and doses depend on context and local protocols.

Re-sedation is a major trap: naloxone may wear off before a long-acting opioid. Some patients need repeated doses or infusion with monitored care. Evaluate aspiration, hypoxic injury, pulmonary oedema and other sedatives. Take-home naloxone and harm-reduction referral should be offered where appropriate.

6. Benzodiazepines, sedatives and the flumazenil hazard

Benzodiazepines amplify GABA-A receptor activity, producing sedation, ataxia and amnesia. Isolated benzodiazepine overdose often responds to supportive observation; severe respiratory compromise should prompt consideration of alcohol, opioids or other co-ingestants. Secure airway, monitor ventilation, temperature and cardiovascular status.

Flumazenil is not a routine “wake-up” drug. It can precipitate seizures or withdrawal in benzodiazepine-dependent patients and in mixed overdoses, particularly when proconvulsant or tricyclic medicines are present. Specialist-selected uses may exist, such as carefully characterised iatrogenic sedation. A history of chronic benzodiazepine use or uncertain co-ingestion makes indiscriminate flumazenil unsafe.

Similar supportive principles apply to many sedative agents, but individual substances may produce distinctive hypotension, bradycardia, seizures or metabolic effects. Use TOXBASE for the actual agent.

7. Tricyclic antidepressants and sodium-channel blockade

Amitriptyline and other TCAs can block fast cardiac sodium channels, producing intraventricular conduction delay and malignant arrhythmias. They also inhibit muscarinic receptors and α1 receptors, causing an anticholinergic toxidrome and hypotension; central nervous system effects include seizures and coma.

ECG is essential. QRS widening, terminal right-axis deviation or abnormal terminal R in aVR, hypotension, ventricular arrhythmia and seizures identify serious poisoning. Repeat ECGs and blood gases as the syndrome can evolve. Acidosis increases toxicity by favouring active drug effects.

IV sodium bicarbonate is used under resuscitation/toxicology guidance for dangerous sodium-channel blockade; it provides sodium loading and alkalinisation. Secure the airway, correct hypoxia and acidosis, and involve critical care. Do not interpret a broad-complex tachycardia as routine VT without considering TCA toxicity; toxin-specific management is required.

8. Salicylates and mixed acid–base disturbance

Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) and other salicylates uncouple oxidative phosphorylation, increasing heat and metabolic acid generation; they stimulate the medullary respiratory centre, producing respiratory alkalosis initially and later a mixed disorder with high anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Features may include tinnitus, vomiting, tachypnoea, diaphoresis, fever, confusion and pulmonary oedema.

Check serial salicylate concentrations, glucose, acid–base status, electrolytes and renal function. A single early concentration may underestimate delayed absorption, especially with enteric-coated products or bezoar formation. Clinical severity and acid-base state matter as much as the numerical level.

Urine alkalinisation with sodium bicarbonate enhances salicylate elimination in selected cases under careful potassium and pH monitoring. Severe poisoning, renal impairment, pulmonary oedema or neurological deterioration may require haemodialysis. Avoid reflexive intubation without planning to maintain compensatory minute ventilation: worsening acidemia can rapidly increase tissue toxicity.

Physiology pearl: Salicylate causes both direct respiratory-centre stimulation and uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation. The blood gas may show a near-normal pH despite simultaneous respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis.

9. Beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers and cardiovascular collapse

Beta-blockers can cause profound bradycardia, AV block, hypotension and reduced contractility; some agents also block sodium channels or cause seizures (e.g., propranolol). Calcium-channel blocker overdose causes vasodilation and/or myocardial depression, potentially with hyperglycaemia due to impaired pancreatic insulin release, especially with verapamil and diltiazem.

Monitor ECG, blood pressure, glucose, potassium, calcium, lactate and perfusion. Begin resuscitation and involve critical care/NPIS early. Management may include carefully chosen IV fluids, vasopressors, calcium for calcium-channel blocker toxicity, and high-dose insulin–euglycaemia therapy in severe cases, requiring intensive glucose/potassium monitoring. Glucagon sometimes has a role in beta-blocker poisoning; choice is toxin-specific.

A seemingly well patient after ingesting a modified-release formulation may deteriorate hours later. Observe for delayed cardiotoxicity according to TOXBASE rather than relying on an initial normal pulse or ECG.

10. Digoxin, lithium and other narrow therapeutic index agents

Digoxin: Na+/K+-ATPase inhibition changes intracellular sodium and calcium, increasing cardiac inotropy but producing dysrhythmias when excessive. Nausea, confusion, visual disturbance, bradyarrhythmia, AV block and ectopy are clues. Check potassium, magnesium, renal function and a suitably timed digoxin level; level interpretation depends on sampling time, clinical state and prior Fab use. Digoxin-specific Fab fragments may be indicated for life-threatening toxicity, guided by NPIS and specialist advice.

Lithium: toxicity ranges from coarse tremor, vomiting and ataxia to confusion, seizures and coma. Dehydration, renal impairment, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and thiazide diuretics can increase exposure. Chronic toxicity may be severe despite a less dramatic concentration. Assess serial lithium concentrations, renal function, ECG and neurological status; consider haemodialysis in selected severe cases with toxicology/renal input.

Activated charcoal does not meaningfully bind lithium or iron. Do not apply the standard one-hour charcoal assumption to these substances. Follow agent-specific guidance for decontamination and elimination.

11. Serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome and hyperthermia

Serotonin syndrome is a pharmacodynamic toxicity from serotonergic drugs or interactions (e.g., SSRIs/SNRIs with certain other serotonergic agents). Onset can be rapid, often within hours of a change. Triad: altered mental state, autonomic hyperactivity (sweating, tachycardia, fever) and neuromuscular findings (clonus and hyperreflexia, often legs). Stop triggers, provide active cooling, IV fluids when appropriate and benzodiazepine sedation; escalate severe cases and consider toxin-specific therapy under specialist guidance.

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is associated with dopamine blockade or dopaminergic withdrawal, generally with slower onset, marked rigidity, altered consciousness, hyperthermia and raised CK. Both may lead to rhabdomyolysis, renal injury and shock. Do not mislabel all febrile agitation as “serotonin syndrome”; examination of clonus and rigidity and detailed medication chronology are crucial.

Hyperthermia is not ordinary fever: antipyretics do not reverse excessive muscular heat production. Stop the trigger, manage agitation and urgently cool while protecting airway and circulation.

12. Toxic alcohols, carbon monoxide and inhalational poisoning

Methanol is metabolised to formic acid (visual impairment, high anion-gap acidosis). Ethylene glycol produces toxic organic acids and renal injury; hypocalcaemia may occur. Early on, an osmolar gap can be high before metabolism; later, a severe anion gap may occur even when the osmolar gap is small. Do not use a normal osmolar gap to exclude late toxic alcohol poisoning.

Request acid–base analysis, measured osmolality, electrolytes, kidney function and agent-specific assays when available. Fomepizole inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase and limits formation of toxic metabolites; severe cases may need haemodialysis. Consult NPIS/critical care promptly. Ethanol is an alternative antidote in selected situations under specialist protocols, not routine self-treatment.

Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin with high affinity and also impairs cellular oxygen use. Headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, syncope and myocardial injury may follow exposure to faulty heaters or fires. Standard pulse oximetry cannot distinguish oxyhaemoglobin from carboxyhaemoglobin and may read falsely normal. Remove from exposure, give high-concentration oxygen and use laboratory co-oximetry; obtain specialist advice for severe exposure, pregnancy or neurological symptoms. A low measured COHb later does not exclude earlier poisoning after oxygen or delay.

13. Organophosphates, iron, caustics and toxic exposure safety

Organophosphates inhibit acetylcholinesterase, producing a cholinergic crisis with bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, bradycardia or variable heart rate, salivation, lacrimation, diarrhoea, sweating, miosis and muscular fasciculations/weakness. Staff safety comes first: use appropriate PPE and decontamination to avoid secondary exposure. Protect airway and suction secretions; atropine treats muscarinic effects and an oxime may be recommended according to pesticide, timing and NPIS advice.

Iron overdose can cause severe vomiting, abdominal pain, shock, acidosis and delayed hepatic injury. There may be a deceptive lull between phases. Serum iron timing and toxicity assessment require specialist guidance; deferoxamine is an antidote in selected severe poisoning. Activated charcoal does not bind iron effectively.

Caustic ingestion can injure upper GI tissues; assess airway early. Do not induce vomiting or attempt neutralisation. For hydrocarbon ingestion, aspiration pneumonitis is a key danger; charcoal is generally avoided. Chemical contamination demands scene and staff safety, removing clothes and irrigation according to the substance.

14. Decontamination, enhanced elimination and antidotes

Activated charcoal may reduce absorption of certain substances when given early after ingestion (often within roughly one hour), but benefit depends on substance, amount, formulation, timing and whether the airway is protected. Later dosing can occasionally be appropriate for modified-release agents under specialist direction. Contraindications include an unprotected airway and substances posing aspiration/caustic hazards. It is not effective for lithium, iron and many alcohols.

Gastric lavage and induced vomiting are not routine. Multiple-dose charcoal, whole-bowel irrigation, urinary alkalinisation, haemodialysis and lipid emulsion have narrow, toxin-specific roles and potential complications. These are specialist-led interventions, not generic steps in all overdoses.

Mechanism-based antidotes are highly valuable when correctly chosen: NAC for paracetamol, naloxone for opioid respiratory depression, Fab for selected digoxin toxicity, hydroxocobalamin for severe cyanide poisoning, fomepizole for toxic alcohols, and atropine for cholinergic organophosphate effects. Some antidotes can worsen the wrong poisoning (e.g., flumazenil in TCA co-ingestion).

15. Disposition, self-harm, safeguarding and PLAB 2 communication

Medical safety before discharge: ensure symptoms and physiological abnormalities resolve, complete any poison-specific observation and repeat ECG/levels as indicated, and establish a safe follow-up plan. Some products cause delayed deterioration; early clinical improvement alone is insufficient. Discuss unclear timing, co-ingestions and complex cases with NPIS.

NICE NG225: intentional self-poisoning warrants compassionate assessment of physical risk and mental health needs in parallel. Offer age-appropriate liaison psychiatry or suitably skilled mental health assessment as soon as possible, without delaying it for an arbitrary blood alcohol level or “medical clearance”. Ask openly about current suicidal thoughts, intent, access to medicines, safety at home and support; use a collaborative safety plan rather than judging or punishing.

PLAB 2 station: introduce yourself, assess immediate safety and whether the patient can participate, use non-judgemental language, explain what tests and treatment are needed, offer privacy, gain consent where possible, involve senior/mental health teams and document discussions, capacity and safeguarding concerns. Never promise confidentiality when imminent safety risks require proportionate sharing.

Four worked clinical cases

Case A · The patient with no symptoms after paracetamol

Presentation: A 21-year-old attends two hours after taking an unknown quantity of paracetamol tablets following an argument. They feel entirely well; they may also have taken a cold-and-flu sachet.

Reasoning and action: ABCDE and an immediate safety assessment. Clarify formulation, maximum dose, timing and co-ingestions; obtain an appropriately timed paracetamol concentration and relevant blood tests under TOXBASE, and start NAC if indicated. Do not use an early concentration to exclude poisoning. Offer timely psychosocial assessment alongside physical care.

Exam pearl: Why is it dangerous to discharge now? Hepatotoxicity is initially silent and additional formulations may contain paracetamol.

Case B · Unresponsive with slow breathing

Presentation: A 39-year-old is found unresponsive with shallow breathing and small pupils; a friend reports methadone use and alcohol intake.

Reasoning and action: Call for resuscitation support, open airway, provide ventilation/oxygen as needed, check glucose, administer and titrate naloxone per protocol and consider mixed intoxication. Observe for re-sedation because methadone may outlast naloxone.

Exam pearl: The clinical endpoint of naloxone in dependent patients is restored adequate ventilation, not necessarily complete arousal.

Case C · Widened QRS and seizures

Presentation: A 46-year-old with access to amitriptyline develops agitation, a seizure, hypotension and a very broad QRS complex on ECG.

Reasoning and action: Protect airway and treat seizure using resuscitation guidance; urgently discuss IV sodium bicarbonate for sodium-channel blockade, serial ECG/blood gases, vasopressors if indicated and critical care input. Consider co-ingestion.

Exam pearl: A widened QRS is a mechanism clue; do not treat this as uncomplicated anticholinergic delirium.

Case D · Tinnitus and mixed acid–base disorder

Presentation: A 68-year-old taking frequent aspirin for pain presents with vomiting, tinnitus, tachypnoea and confusion. Blood gas shows low CO₂ and low bicarbonate.

Reasoning and action: Suspect salicylate poisoning. Resuscitate, obtain serial salicylate levels, glucose, electrolytes and acid–base profile. Seek NPIS advice for alkalinisation and possible haemodialysis. If intubation is required, maintain adequate ventilation with expert support.

Exam pearl: An apparently near-normal pH can conceal opposing acid–base processes.

Exam checklist: For every poisoning question ask: ① Is physiology unstable? ② What toxidrome/ECG pattern fits? ③ What delayed harms need monitoring? ④ Is there a justified antidote? ⑤ What psychosocial and discharge safety is required?

    UK emergency pathways · TOXBASE, NPIS and NICE

    Decision aids summarise principles, not prescribing charts. Check TOXBASE/BNF and local emergency protocols at the bedside, especially for dose, sampling times, antidote use and observation. NPIS provides individualised advice for severe, mixed or uncertain poisoning.

    Unidentified poisoning / mixed ingestion

    1. Immediate

    ABCDE, oxygenation/ventilation, glucose, ECG, observations, senior help; address seizures, shock and dangerous hyperthermia.

    ↓
    2. Investigate

    Exposure history and packaging; gas, glucose, U&amp;Es, Mg, liver tests/INR; paracetamol concentration in deliberate or uncertain ingestion; targeted tests and serial ECGs.

    ↓
    3. Interpret

    Use toxidromes, acid–base profile, QRS/QT abnormalities and serial changes. Exclude non-toxicological emergencies.

    ↓
    4. Escalate

    Consult TOXBASE; contact NPIS for severe, mixed, unknown or atypical exposure; arrange observation and psychosocial assessment when relevant.

    Paracetamol overdose risk assessment

    1. Identify pattern

    Single timed acute dose versus repeated/staggered, unknown-time or modified-release exposure; check co-formulations.

    ↓
    2. Sample correctly

    For eligible single acute exposures, measure at the time recommended in TOXBASE (commonly ≥4 hours) and interpret on the current nomogram; request LFT/INR as indicated.

    ↓
    3. Treat promptly

    Start IV acetylcysteine when indicated or when delaying results would be unsafe under current guidance. Use local infusion chart; monitor treatment complications.

    ↓
    4. Reassess

    Repeat paracetamol concentration/liver tests/INR as directed, complete treatment endpoints, and provide psychosocial care if self-poisoning.

    Suspected opioid-induced respiratory depression

    1. Support life

    Open airway, ventilate if needed, provide oxygen, check glucose, call for help. Do not delay ventilation to locate naloxone.

    ↓
    2. Reverse carefully

    Administer naloxone per route/local protocol; in dependence aim for adequate breathing, not necessarily full consciousness.

    ↓
    3. Watch recurrence

    Long-acting opioids can outlast naloxone. Monitor breathing, consciousness, aspiration and co-ingestants; repeat treatment or infusion if indicated.

    ↓
    4. Aftercare

    Provide substance-use advice, take-home naloxone where appropriate and a safe discharge/follow-up plan.

    ECG changes or cardiovascular collapse after overdose

    1. Resuscitate

    Continuous ECG/defibrillator access, IV access, serial gases and electrolytes; senior critical care input.

    ↓
    2. Classify mechanism

    Wide QRS suggests sodium-channel blockade; QT prolongation suggests torsades risk; bradycardic shock may fit beta-blocker/CCB/digoxin toxicity.

    ↓
    3. Target

    Use poison-specific expert-guided measures: e.g., sodium bicarbonate for toxic QRS widening, magnesium for torsades, or insulin-euglycaemia therapy for selected severe CCB/beta-blocker poisonings.

    ↓
    4. Reassess

    Repeat ECG, haemodynamics, glucose, electrolytes, pH and drug levels as indicated. Contact NPIS and consider critical care.

    Remember: A normal first ECG, a normal pulse oximeter reading after carbon monoxide exposure or absence of early paracetamol symptoms cannot independently rule out dangerous poisoning.
