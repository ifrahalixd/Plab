Retinal Detachment & Retinal Vascular Occlusion

Emergency red flags: new flashes/floaters with curtain, sudden painless monocular vision loss or transient visual blackout. Refer urgently.

Presentations and triage

Sudden painless monocular visual loss, new flashes and floaters, or a shadow/curtain in vision are urgent retinal warning symptoms. Assess onset, laterality, pain, visual acuity and neurological symptoms.

Retinal detachment (RD)

The neurosensory retina separates from the retinal pigment epithelium. Rhegmatogenous detachment follows a retinal break; tractional detachment can occur in proliferative diabetic retinopathy; exudative detachment occurs without a retinal break.

RD risk factors

High myopia, posterior vitreous detachment, ocular trauma, prior cataract surgery, lattice degeneration, family history and previous detachment increase risk.

RD symptoms

Flashes (photopsia), new floaters and a progressive peripheral field defect described as a dark curtain. Central acuity may be preserved until the macula becomes detached.

RD examination

Check visual acuity in each eye, pupils including relative afferent pupillary defect, visual fields and fundus where trained. Do not delay urgent ophthalmology referral for a normal undilated fundus view.

Retinal tear and vitreous detachment

Acute symptomatic posterior vitreous detachment can cause flashes and floaters and may coexist with a retinal tear. New symptoms require prompt dilated retinal examination to exclude tear or detachment.

RD management

Suspected retinal detachment needs immediate/same-day discussion with ophthalmology and urgent assessment. Options include laser or cryotherapy for retinal breaks and pneumatic retinopexy, scleral buckle or vitrectomy for detachment.

Macula-on versus macula-off

Macula-on detachment threatens central vision and is particularly time-critical. Macula-off detachment has already affected central vision; timing and technique are determined urgently by the retinal team.

Central retinal artery occlusion (CRAO)

CRAO produces sudden, severe, painless monocular vision loss. Fundoscopy may show a pale retina with a cherry-red spot, though early findings may be subtle.

CRAO emergency pathway

Treat suspected CRAO as an ocular stroke: activate emergency stroke assessment according to local protocol, urgently involve ophthalmology/stroke services, and assess for embolic and vascular causes. Do not delay transfer for office-based manoeuvres.

Amaurosis fugax

Transient monocular loss, often a descending curtain lasting minutes, may represent retinal transient ischaemia and requires urgent TIA/stroke pathway assessment.

Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO)

CRVO often causes sudden or subacute painless monocular blur. Fundoscopy may reveal widespread retinal haemorrhages, dilated tortuous veins, cotton-wool spots and disc swelling.

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO)

BRVO produces sectoral haemorrhages and visual impairment corresponding to the affected retinal territory. Hypertension, diabetes and glaucoma are risk factors.

RVO treatment and follow-up

Urgent ophthalmic assessment is needed. Management may include intravitreal anti-VEGF for macular oedema, monitoring for neovascularisation, and treatment of cardiovascular risk factors.

Other causes of sudden visual loss

Consider vitreous haemorrhage, ischaemic optic neuropathy, optic neuritis and neurological stroke. Giant cell arteritis is an emergency in an older person with headache, jaw claudication or scalp tenderness.

PLAB 2 consultation and safety-net

Ask about flashes, floaters, curtain, duration, pain, trauma, vascular risks, headache and neurological symptoms. Check acuity, pupils and fields. Explain urgency, arrange referral, and advise immediate return for new/worsening visual loss.

PLAB 2: Sudden visual loss OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm patient identity and obtain consent.
  2. Clarify timing, monocular versus binocular loss, pain, flashes, floaters, curtain and neurological symptoms.
  3. Ask about diabetes, hypertension, trauma, eye surgery, myopia and GCA symptoms.
  4. Assess visual acuity, pupils and visual fields; examine fundus if trained without delaying referral.
  5. Explain suspected diagnosis, urgency, escalation and safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Retinal anatomy

Photoreceptors rely on the retinal pigment epithelium. Detachment interrupts normal support and can impair vision.

Posterior vitreous detachment

Vitreous traction may cause photopsia and can create a retinal tear. Not every PVD is a detachment.

CRAO pathophysiology

Arterial obstruction causes retinal ischaemia. Prompt emergency stroke evaluation is essential even if fundoscopy is initially inconclusive.

CRVO versus BRVO

Central vein obstruction affects much of the retina; branch occlusion produces sectoral signs. Macular oedema is a key cause of reduced vision.

Macular status

Macula-on and macula-off status helps retinal specialists determine urgency and expected visual outcomes.
