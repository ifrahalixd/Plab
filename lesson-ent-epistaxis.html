Epistaxis (Nosebleeds)

Emergency: Severe bleeding, airway compromise, syncope or shock requires urgent ABCDE assessment and escalation.

Definition and anatomy

Epistaxis is bleeding from the nasal cavity. Most bleeds arise anteriorly from Kiesselbach’s plexus (Little’s area) on the anterior septum.

Clinical application: Posterior bleeding is less common but may be severe and harder to control.

Common causes

Nose picking, trauma, dry air, URTI, rhinitis and intranasal medication can damage fragile vessels.

Clinical application: Ask about recurrent bleeds, recent injury, cocaine use and nasal sprays.

Risk factors for severe bleeding

Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, bleeding disorders, liver disease and frailty increase risk or complicate management.

Clinical application: Record drug name, dose and last administration; do not automatically stop anticoagulation without assessment.

First assessment: ABCDE

Check airway, breathing, circulation, haemodynamic status and active bleeding. Assess shock, aspiration risk and blood loss.

Clinical application: Call for senior/ENT help early for major or persistent bleeding.

Immediate first aid

Sit upright, lean forward and pinch the soft cartilaginous part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes. Spit out blood.

Clinical application: Do not tilt the head back or pinch only the bony bridge.

If first aid fails

Reassess stability, ensure adequate lighting and suction if available. A topical vasoconstrictor/local anaesthetic may be used under local protocol.

Clinical application: Continued heavy bleeding warrants urgent assessment and escalation.

Anterior rhinoscopy

Look for an identifiable anterior bleeding point after clearing clots when safe. Inspect septum and both nostrils.

Clinical application: Do not repeatedly disturb an effective clot or perform unsafe instrumentation.

Chemical cautery

If a discrete anterior source is visible and bleeding is controlled enough, trained clinicians may use silver nitrate cautery.

Clinical application: Avoid cauterising both sides of the septum because of perforation risk.

Anterior nasal packing

If bleeding persists or the source cannot be cauterised, an appropriate nasal pack may be needed according to local protocol.

Clinical application: Assess airway, analgesia and observation needs; follow local policy for pack removal and admission.

Posterior epistaxis

Suspect with brisk bleeding into the pharynx, failure of anterior measures or no anterior source. Posterior bleeds can cause significant blood loss.

Clinical application: Urgent ENT involvement is needed; posterior packing and definitive procedures require experienced teams.

Investigations

Mild self-limited anterior bleeds often need no tests. In significant/recurrent bleeding consider FBC, coagulation testing where relevant, renal function and group-and-save/crossmatch if severe.

Clinical application: Investigations should reflect clinical severity, medication history and suspected coagulopathy.

Anticoagulants and antiplatelets

Check indication, bleeding severity, INR if taking warfarin, and other relevant tests. Reversal decisions depend on major bleeding protocols.

Clinical application: Discuss significant bleeding urgently with senior clinicians and relevant specialties; do not give blanket advice to stop treatment.

When to admit or escalate

Persistent haemorrhage, haemodynamic compromise, suspected posterior source, aspiration risk, substantial anaemia or significant comorbidity may require admission.

Clinical application: ENT may use endoscopic control, arterial ligation or embolisation when conservative measures fail.

Aftercare and prevention

Avoid nose blowing, picking, strenuous exertion and hot drinks for a short period according to local advice; consider nasal moisturisation for dryness.

Clinical application: Provide clear written safety-net advice and address reversible triggers.

Special populations and differentials

Children commonly have anterior bleeding from local trauma. Recurrent unilateral bleeding, obstruction or a mass warrants further evaluation.

Clinical application: Consider hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia with recurrent bleeds and telangiectasias.

PLAB 2 OSCE and communication

Explain first aid calmly, ask about blood loss, trauma, medicines, bleeding history and red flags; obtain consent before examination or procedures.

Clinical application: Demonstrate forward lean and firm pressure; safety-net for recurrent heavy bleeding, dizziness, breathing difficulty or failure to stop.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Epistaxis

  1. Introduce yourself, assess stability and call for help if needed.
  2. Ask about duration, amount, trauma, recurrence, medicines and bleeding disorders.
  3. Demonstrate forward lean and uninterrupted pressure to the soft nose.
  4. Explain cautery, packing and referral if first aid fails.
  5. Safety-net for ongoing heavy bleeding, dizziness, fainting or breathing difficulty.

Deep Dive

Vascular supply

Anterior septal plexus receives branches from both internal and external carotid circulations.

Why lean forward

This reduces blood passing into the pharynx, nausea and aspiration risk.

Cautery versus packing

Cautery targets a discrete anterior vessel; packing applies broad pressure when the source is not accessible.

Posterior bleeding

Posterior branches of the sphenopalatine artery may be involved and require specialist control.

Coagulopathy

Anticoagulants and thrombocytopenia can prolong bleeding; management must balance haemostasis and thrombosis risk.

Recurrence

Dryness and local trauma perpetuate mucosal injury; prevention addresses both.
