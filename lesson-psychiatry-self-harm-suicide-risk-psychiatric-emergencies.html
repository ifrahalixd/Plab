Self-Harm, Suicide Risk Assessment & Psychiatric Emergencies

PLAB priority: Treat physical emergencies first, ask directly about suicide, and use a collaborative formulation rather than a risk score.

01. Scope and priorities

Self-harm means intentional self-poisoning or injury irrespective of apparent purpose. Every presentation needs compassionate medical assessment, psychosocial assessment and a collaborative safety plan; do not equate self-harm automatically with suicidal intent.

PLAB application: Treat injuries or poisoning first while arranging timely mental health assessment.

02. Immediate ABCDE assessment

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure; identify overdose, bleeding, head injury, hypoglycaemia, intoxication or delirium. Obtain observations, glucose, ECG and relevant investigations based on presentation.

PLAB application: Unstable patients need emergency medical care before psychiatric disposition.

03. Engagement and privacy

Introduce yourself, obtain consent where possible, use a private setting, listen without judgement and offer an interpreter when needed. Explain confidentiality and its safety limits.

PLAB application: Avoid blaming language and assumptions about motivation.

04. Direct suicide enquiry

Ask about thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, intent, plans, access to means, preparations, timing and recent actions. Ask about protective factors and reasons for living without assuming these eliminate risk.

PLAB application: Direct questioning does not plant suicidal ideas.

05. Current and past self-harm

Clarify method, timing, substances or doses, medical consequences, whether rescue was expected, previous attempts and escalation in frequency or lethality. Explore what happened before and after the act.

PLAB application: Do not use the method alone to determine intent.

06. Psychiatric and contextual factors

Assess depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, trauma, anxiety, substance use, recent losses, isolation, housing, domestic abuse and access to support. Review medicines and recent changes.

PLAB application: Consider command hallucinations, severe agitation and intoxication.

07. Mental state examination

Document appearance, behaviour, speech, mood, affect, thought form and content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. Look for delirium, mania, psychosis and severe depression.

PLAB application: Capacity is decision-specific and may fluctuate.

08. Risk formulation rather than scores

NICE NG225 advises against using risk assessment tools or low/medium/high risk categories to predict future suicide or decide discharge. Formulate immediate needs, changing factors, strengths and safety arrangements.

PLAB application: A low score must never justify discharge.

09. Psychosocial assessment

A trained mental health professional should explore needs, circumstances, intent, safeguarding, coping and follow-up. Offer assessment as soon as possible after medical stabilisation; do not withhold it because of intoxication alone.

PLAB application: Coordinate medical and mental health teams.

10. Safety planning

Develop a collaborative written plan: personal warning signs, coping strategies, supportive contacts, crisis services, steps to make the environment safer and follow-up. Discuss restricting access to medicines or other means.

PLAB application: Provide clear return precautions and an accessible copy.

11. Discharge and aftercare

Before discharge ensure psychosocial assessment, an appropriate management plan, involvement of relevant agencies and clear aftercare. If ongoing safety concerns exist, arrange initial aftercare within 48 hours where indicated by NICE.

PLAB application: Do not discharge solely because the patient denies current suicidal thoughts.

12. Agitation and violence

Use calm verbal de-escalation, personal space, a safe environment and trained support. Exclude hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, intoxication and delirium. Rapid tranquillisation is a last resort under local policy with monitoring.

PLAB application: Protect patient and staff; use least restrictive care.

13. Psychosis and command hallucinations

Assess hallucinations instructing self-harm, persecutory beliefs, disorganisation, ability to meet basic needs and risk to others. Seek urgent specialist review when safety cannot be maintained.

PLAB application: Avoid arguing with delusions; acknowledge distress.

14. Mental capacity and legal frameworks

In England and Wales, the Mental Capacity Act applies to specific decisions; presume capacity unless evidence suggests otherwise and assess understanding, retention, weighing and communication. Mental Health Act assessment may be required for detention; laws vary across UK nations.

PLAB application: Urgent legal decisions require senior and specialist support.

15. Special groups and safeguarding

Consider young people, pregnancy/postpartum, older adults, neurodivergent people and those facing abuse. Ask about dependent children and immediate safeguarding risks; adapt communication and involve carers appropriately.

PLAB application: Do not use age or diagnosis as a shortcut for risk.

16. PLAB 2 structured station

Start with empathy and immediate safety; ask directly about suicide; explore method, mental state and supports; summarise a collaborative plan; escalate appropriately; document and safety-net.

PLAB application: Explain next steps without promising secrecy or automatic admission.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Why scores fail

Suicide is difficult to predict at an individual level. A risk category can create false reassurance or unnecessary restriction; use a dynamic personalised formulation instead.

Intent is not identical to injury severity

Method, lethality and suicidal intent are related but not interchangeable. Explore what the person hoped would happen, their understanding and the circumstances.

Acute intoxication and assessment

Intoxication can alter cognition and increase immediate danger. Treat intoxication, ensure observation and provide psychosocial assessment as soon as meaningful engagement is possible.

Trauma-informed communication

Offer choice and control where possible, explain each step, avoid repeated unnecessary retelling, and recognise how previous trauma affects trust.

Least restrictive emergency care

De-escalation reduces harm. If restrictive measures become necessary, follow law, local policy and monitoring requirements, and review the need continuously.

Practical safety planning

A usable plan names concrete coping actions, trusted people, local crisis contacts and ways to reduce access to means; it should be revisited as circumstances change.
