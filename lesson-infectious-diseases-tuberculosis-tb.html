Tuberculosis (TB)

PLAB priority: Recognise pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB, implement airborne precautions, investigate appropriately, understand treatment safety and involve public health services.

01. Overview and transmission

Tuberculosis is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex. Pulmonary or laryngeal TB can spread through airborne particles when a person with infectious disease coughs, speaks or sings. Latent TB infection is not infectious; active TB may be infectious.

PLAB application: Ask about household contacts, travel, previous TB, crowded settings, immunosuppression and HIV.

02. Pulmonary TB presentation

Persistent cough, sputum, haemoptysis, fever, drenching night sweats, fatigue, weight loss and loss of appetite are typical. Examination may be normal or show crackles, lymphadenopathy or signs of advanced disease.

PLAB application: Think TB in prolonged cough with constitutional symptoms; do not require every classic feature.

03. Extrapulmonary TB

TB can affect lymph nodes, pleura, spine (Pott disease), joints, pericardium, abdomen, kidneys and meninges. Cervical lymphadenitis may be painless. TB meningitis can cause subacute headache, fever, confusion and cranial nerve palsies.

PLAB application: Back pain with neurological deficits or possible TB meningitis requires urgent specialist evaluation.

04. Latent versus active TB

Latent infection means immune sensitisation without clinical active disease; people have no TB symptoms and are not contagious. Active disease causes symptoms or radiological/microbiological evidence and needs multidrug treatment. IGRA and tuberculin skin testing detect immune response, not whether disease is active.

PLAB application: A positive IGRA does not diagnose active TB; exclude active disease before latent TB treatment.

05. Immediate assessment and infection control

Assess ABCDE and severity. In suspected infectious pulmonary TB, arrange appropriate airborne precautions, a well-ventilated or negative-pressure room where available, and suitable respirators for staff according to local policy. Notify the TB team and infection prevention service.

PLAB application: Do not delay isolation while waiting for sputum results when infectious pulmonary TB is suspected.

06. Chest radiography

Chest X-ray may show upper-zone infiltrates, cavities, nodules or miliary changes; presentations vary, especially with immunosuppression. Imaging supports suspicion but does not prove or exclude TB.

PLAB application: A normal or atypical chest X-ray does not completely exclude TB, especially in HIV.

07. Microbiological confirmation

Collect appropriate respiratory samples, often sputum, for acid-fast smear, mycobacterial culture and rapid molecular testing such as NAAT where available. Culture helps confirm disease and assess drug susceptibility; molecular assays can rapidly identify TB and some resistance.

PLAB application: Request samples before treatment when feasible, but do not postpone urgent treatment in severe suspected TB.

08. Drug susceptibility and resistant TB

Drug-resistant TB requires expert-led treatment guided by rapid resistance testing and full susceptibility results. Rifampicin resistance is a major warning sign; multidrug-resistant TB generally means resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin.

PLAB application: Never improvise a standard regimen for known or suspected resistant TB; involve specialist services.

09. Standard drug-susceptible TB treatment

A common regimen for uncomplicated drug-susceptible pulmonary TB is 2 months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol, followed by 4 months of isoniazid and rifampicin, subject to specialist assessment and local guidance. CNS or bone/joint disease may require different durations.

PLAB application: Know the 2HRZE/4HR framework but check susceptibility, interactions and site of disease.

10. Treatment adverse effects and monitoring

Isoniazid can cause hepatitis and peripheral neuropathy; pyridoxine is used when indicated. Rifampicin causes interactions and orange discoloration of body fluids. Pyrazinamide may cause hepatotoxicity and hyperuricaemia. Ethambutol can cause optic neuropathy: assess vision and new visual symptoms.

PLAB application: Warn patients to seek urgent assessment for jaundice, severe vomiting or new visual changes.

11. HIV and TB co-infection

HIV increases risk of active and disseminated TB. Offer HIV testing to people with TB. ART timing requires specialist coordination because of drug interactions and immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS); TB meningitis is a special situation.

PLAB application: Consider both TB and HIV when patients have prolonged systemic illness or atypical imaging.

12. TB meningitis and spinal TB emergencies

TB meningitis is potentially fatal and needs urgent hospital care, specialist antimicrobials and consideration of adjunctive corticosteroids. Spinal TB can cause cord compression, weakness or bladder dysfunction requiring urgent imaging and spinal review.

PLAB application: Never manage neurological red flags as routine outpatient TB.

13. Contact tracing and public health

TB is a notifiable disease in the UK. Notify appropriate public health teams through established pathways; contact investigation and testing are coordinated by TB services. Assess household and other close contacts, especially children and immunocompromised people.

PLAB application: In PLAB, include notification, contact tracing, adherence support and safeguarding.

14. Latent TB testing and preventive treatment

Targeted testing uses IGRA or tuberculin skin tests according to age and risk group. Exclude active TB before preventive therapy. Regimen selection considers age, liver health, pregnancy, drug interactions and current NICE/local protocols.

PLAB application: Do not treat a positive IGRA as a complete diagnosis without symptom assessment and appropriate imaging.

15. Pregnancy, children and vulnerable groups

Pregnant people, children and immunocompromised patients require specialist assessment. Children may have paucibacillary disease, so microbiological tests can be negative. Assess nutrition, adherence barriers, language and social circumstances sensitively.

PLAB application: Avoid stigma and provide interpreters and accessible follow-up arrangements.

16. PLAB 2: consultation and safety netting

Take a focused symptom and exposure history, explain why sputum testing and imaging are needed, discuss temporary infection precautions, and explain treatment length and follow-up in plain language. Escalate breathlessness, significant haemoptysis, confusion and neurological symptoms immediately.

PLAB application: Explain that TB is treatable and contact tracing is a routine protective public health measure.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Granuloma and latency

Cell-mediated immunity can contain mycobacteria within granulomas. Reactivation becomes more likely when immunity is impaired; a positive immune test does not establish active disease.

Why multiple drugs?

Mycobacteria exist in different metabolic states and resistance can emerge with inadequate therapy. Combination therapy reduces treatment failure and resistance.

Interpreting TB tests

Smear gives a rapid indication of acid-fast organisms but is less sensitive than culture and not species-specific. NAAT is faster than culture and can reveal selected resistance markers; culture remains important.

HIV changes TB presentation

Advanced immunosuppression can produce atypical chest radiographs and extrapulmonary or disseminated disease; clinicians must maintain suspicion despite nonclassic findings.

Drug safety and interactions

Rifampicin induces drug-metabolising enzymes and can reduce efficacy of many medications, including some contraceptives and antiretrovirals. Liver toxicity and visual symptoms need clear safety-netting.

Public health and patient-centred care

Prompt notification, contact tracing and supported adherence reduce spread. Explain precautions compassionately, avoid blame and tailor follow-up to social barriers.
