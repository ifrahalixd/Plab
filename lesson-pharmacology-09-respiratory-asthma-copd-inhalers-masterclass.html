Respiratory Pharmacology · Deep Dive

Molecular pathways

Beta-2 receptor activation stimulates Gs → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A, reducing smooth-muscle contractile activity. M3 receptors couple to Gq → phospholipase C → IP3/Ca2+ signalling; muscarinic blockade inhibits this bronchoconstrictor pathway. Glucocorticoid receptor activation alters transcription of inflammatory mediators and enhances beta-2 receptor responsiveness.

Why combination treatment works

ICS treats inflammation and lowers asthma exacerbation risk; LABA improves bronchomotor tone and symptoms. Their combination targets distinct processes. In COPD, LAMA/LABA dual bronchodilation addresses vagal and adrenergic pathways, whereas ICS is reserved for selected exacerbation-prone phenotypes because benefit must be balanced against pneumonia risk.

Ventilation physiology and oxygen

In COPD exacerbation, excessive oxygen in susceptible patients can worsen hypercapnia through ventilation-perfusion effects and the Haldane effect; do not withhold oxygen from hypoxaemic patients. Use controlled oxygen and blood gas assessment. In acute asthma, worsening fatigue, silent chest or a normalising/rising carbon dioxide despite distress are dangerous signs.

Steroid pharmacology

Inhaled delivery achieves high local airway exposure with less systemic exposure than oral treatment. Device technique, deposition, dose and formulation affect effective exposure. Oral steroids rapidly deliver systemic anti-inflammatory treatment in exacerbations but repeated courses accumulate adverse effects.

Clinical reasoning: four decisions

1. Is this asthma, COPD, overlap or another diagnosis? 2. Is there an emergency requiring immediate stabilisation? 3. Is the regimen safe and evidence-based for this patient and device? 4. Has the patient demonstrated correct technique and received a clear action plan?

Respiratory Pharmacology · Clinical Masterclass

UK PLAB-focused, mechanism-first guide. Check current NICE/BNF and local emergency protocols before prescribing.

01 · Foundations: asthma versus COPD

Asthma is a variable inflammatory airway disease with fluctuating symptoms and variable expiratory airflow limitation. COPD causes persistent airflow obstruction, usually linked to smoking or other noxious exposures; diagnosis requires post-bronchodilator spirometry showing FEV1/FVC below 0.7 in the appropriate clinical context. Asthma and COPD may coexist; never assume all wheeze is asthma. Assess smoking, occupational triggers, atopy, exacerbation history, inhaler technique and adherence.

02 · Airway pharmacology and receptors

Beta-2 agonists stimulate Gs-coupled receptors, raising intracellular cAMP and relaxing airway smooth muscle. Muscarinic M3 antagonists inhibit vagal-mediated bronchoconstriction. Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) bind intracellular glucocorticoid receptors and suppress inflammatory transcription, reducing exacerbations; they are not immediate rescue bronchodilators. Leukotriene receptor antagonists inhibit cysteinyl-leukotriene signalling. Distinguish bronchodilation from long-term anti-inflammatory control.

03 · Short- and long-acting beta-2 agonists

Salbutamol is a short-acting beta-2 agonist (SABA) with rapid bronchodilator effect; excessive use signals poor control. Formoterol has a rapid onset and long duration, enabling use in certain ICS-formoterol reliever regimens. Salmeterol is long-acting but is not used as an acute reliever. Beta-2 agonist adverse effects include tremor, tachycardia and hypokalaemia, particularly at high doses. Never use LABA monotherapy in asthma.

PLAB prescribing checkpoint: verify the exact drug, device, indication, dose, contraindications and follow-up in the current BNF/NICE pathway.

04 · Inhaled corticosteroids and combination inhalers

Examples include beclometasone, budesonide and fluticasone. ICS reduce airway inflammation and asthma exacerbations. Use an ICS-containing regimen in asthma according to current guidance; SABA-only treatment is not the preferred approach. ICS adverse effects include dysphonia and oral candidiasis; review inhaler technique, rinse the mouth and consider a spacer for suitable metered-dose devices. High-dose ICS can cause systemic effects. ICS/LABA combination options include budesonide-formoterol and fluticasone-salmeterol; they are not interchangeable as relievers.

05 · Asthma management: prevention and reliever strategy

UK guidance increasingly favours anti-inflammatory reliever strategies, often low-dose ICS-formoterol as needed for eligible patients aged 12 and over, and maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART) when indicated. Regimen choice depends on age, severity, product licence and local pathway. Check that the prescribed inhaler is licensed for the intended reliever regimen. Evaluate symptoms, night waking, activity limitation, exacerbations, adherence and inhaler technique before stepping up. Offer a written personalised asthma action plan and arrange review.

06 · Acute asthma exacerbation

Assess ability to speak, respiratory rate, pulse, oxygen saturation, peak expiratory flow when feasible, exhaustion and altered consciousness. Severe or life-threatening features require emergency assessment and senior help. Give inhaled salbutamol, usually via oxygen-driven nebuliser in severe attacks or spacer in less severe attacks, oxygen titrated to appropriate targets, and early systemic corticosteroids according to local acute-asthma protocols. Add ipratropium in severe attacks; IV magnesium sulfate may be considered by senior clinicians in selected severe cases. Do not delay escalation for a normal or rising PaCO2 in a tiring patient.

07 · COPD maintenance therapy

Smoking cessation, vaccination, pulmonary rehabilitation and correct inhaler technique are essential. Short-acting bronchodilators provide symptom relief. Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA, e.g. tiotropium) and LABAs improve symptoms; LABA/LAMA combinations are commonly used when symptoms persist. ICS is not universally indicated in COPD and increases pneumonia risk in some patients. Consider ICS-containing triple therapy for selected patients with exacerbations and relevant clinical features, using NICE guidance and specialist advice.

08 · Acute COPD exacerbation

Look for increased dyspnoea, sputum volume and purulence, but consider pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, heart failure and pneumothorax. Provide short-acting bronchodilators, controlled oxygen if hypoxaemic (often target SpO2 88–92% in patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure pending blood gases), a short oral corticosteroid course when indicated and antibiotics only when clinical criteria support bacterial infection. Obtain blood gases when indicated and consider non-invasive ventilation for persistent hypercapnic acidosis under specialist protocols.

09 · Antimuscarinics, leukotriene antagonists and theophylline

Ipratropium is a short-acting muscarinic antagonist (SAMA); tiotropium is a LAMA. Common effects include dry mouth; use caution with glaucoma risk and urinary retention. Montelukast is an oral leukotriene receptor antagonist used in selected asthma patients, not as rescue treatment; counsel about potential neuropsychiatric adverse effects and seek review if symptoms emerge. Theophylline has a narrow therapeutic index, numerous interactions and toxicity risks, so is reserved for selected situations with monitoring.

10 · Biologics and severe asthma

Patients with persistent uncontrolled asthma despite optimised treatment need specialist assessment for diagnosis, adherence, comorbidities and phenotype. Selected severe asthma treatments target IgE (omalizumab), IL-5/IL-5R (mepolizumab, benralizumab), IL-4R alpha (dupilumab) or TSLP (tezepelumab). Eligibility depends on biomarkers, exacerbation history, licence and NICE technology appraisal. These drugs are not emergency rescue treatments.

11 · Inhaler devices and patient technique

Pressurised metered-dose inhalers require coordinated slow inhalation, often improved by a compatible spacer. Dry-powder inhalers usually require a sufficiently forceful deep inhalation; they are not ideal for every patient. Breath-actuated and soft-mist devices have different techniques. Demonstrate, observe return demonstration, confirm dose counter use and check that the device matches the prescription. Avoid assuming two devices with the same drug name have equivalent strengths or instructions.

12 · Prescribing in pregnancy and comorbidity

Poorly controlled asthma carries maternal and fetal risk; do not stop essential controller therapy without review. ICS and usual bronchodilators are often continued with individualised assessment. Beta-blockers may precipitate bronchospasm in susceptible patients, especially non-selective agents; assess indication and alternatives. Review renal/hepatic disease, arrhythmia, osteoporosis, diabetes and concurrent medicines when selecting systemic corticosteroids and other respiratory drugs.

13 · Systemic corticosteroids: benefits and harms

Prednisolone is used for selected exacerbations, not routine long-term symptom relief. Important harms include hyperglycaemia, infection risk, mood change, hypertension, fluid retention, osteoporosis and adrenal suppression with repeated or prolonged exposure. Long-term oral steroids require specialist review, risk reduction and appropriate tapering assessment; a short course does not always require tapering. Avoid indiscriminate repeated courses without investigating inadequate control.

14 · Monitoring and safety-netting

Record symptom control, reliever use, exacerbations, hospital attendance, smoking status, spirometry/peak flow where appropriate, and device technique. Monitor ICS-related oral candidiasis, pneumonia risk with COPD ICS, and beta-agonist adverse effects. Provide clear red flags: severe breathlessness, inability to speak, cyanosis, confusion, poor response to reliever or worsening despite action plan warrant urgent emergency care.

15 · Clinical case 1: frequent salbutamol use

A 23-year-old with asthma uses salbutamol every day and wakes coughing twice weekly. This is uncontrolled asthma, not a reason to keep increasing SABA alone. Assess triggers, adherence, inhaler technique and exacerbation risk, then initiate or adjust an appropriate ICS-containing regimen per current UK pathway and provide a written action plan.

16 · Clinical case 2: COPD with exacerbations

A 70-year-old smoker has confirmed COPD, daily breathlessness and two treated exacerbations this year. Check inhaler technique, smoking cessation support, vaccinations and pulmonary rehabilitation. Review long-acting bronchodilation and whether ICS-containing treatment is appropriate based on exacerbation pattern and individual risk; do not prescribe ICS solely because COPD is present.

17 · Clinical case 3: acute severe wheeze

A 30-year-old with asthma can speak only in words, is tachypnoeic and has low peak flow. Call for emergency help, start protocol-directed oxygen and rapid inhaled bronchodilator treatment, give systemic corticosteroids early and monitor closely. A silent chest, exhaustion or altered consciousness signals impending respiratory failure.

18 · Clinical case 4: inhaler counselling

A 55-year-old has persistent symptoms despite a new dry-powder inhaler. Ask them to demonstrate their technique; they exhale into the device and inhale too gently. Correct the technique, check inspiratory ability and consider an alternative device if needed before automatically escalating the dose.

19 · PLAB 2 OSCE: shared decision-making

Introduce yourself, confirm diagnosis and concerns, ask about reliever use and adherence, explain the purpose of controller versus reliever medication, demonstrate inhaler technique, check understanding with teach-back, discuss common adverse effects, provide an action plan and explain when to seek urgent help.

20 · Exam traps and practical pearls

LABA without ICS is unsafe in asthma. Not all ICS/LABA products are suitable as relievers. A normal oxygen saturation does not exclude a severe asthma attack. COPD oxygen may need controlled targets with blood gases. Antibiotics are not routine for asthma exacerbations. Always reassess inhaler technique and adherence before increasing treatment.

    NICE / BNF · Respiratory clinical pathways

    Educational decision aids only. Verify up-to-date UK guidance and local emergency protocols.

    Asthma controller

    Confirm diagnosis and assess symptoms → check technique/adherence → choose an age-appropriate ICS-containing treatment and licensed reliever strategy → action plan → review.

    Acute severe asthma

    Recognise severe/life-threatening features → call emergency team → oxygen and inhaled bronchodilators → systemic steroids → add ipratropium in severe cases → monitor and escalate.

    COPD maintenance

    Confirm spirometry → smoking cessation, vaccines and rehab → individualise long-acting bronchodilators → consider ICS only for selected exacerbation patterns → review.

    COPD exacerbation

    Assess severity and alternative diagnoses → controlled oxygen when needed and blood gases → short-acting bronchodilators → steroids if indicated → selective antibiotics → NIV if persistent hypercapnic acidosis.
