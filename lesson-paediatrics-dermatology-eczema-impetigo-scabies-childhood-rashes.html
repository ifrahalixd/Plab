Paediatric Dermatology: Eczema, Impetigo, Scabies & Childhood Rashes

Emergency red flags: toxic child with non-blanching rash; anaphylaxis; painful punched-out erosions in eczema; rapidly spreading painful infection; mucosal blistering or skin detachment.

Assessing a child with a rash

Start with ABCDE, observations, hydration and general appearance. Ask about fever, onset, itch, pain, contacts, travel, immunisations, medication and mucosal involvement. Examine distribution, blanching, vesicles, crusting, scaling and purpura.

PLAB application: A toxic child with a non-blanching rash needs emergency sepsis assessment.

Atopic eczema: recognition

Atopic dermatitis causes chronic relapsing itchy, dry inflamed skin. Infants often have facial and extensor involvement; older children often have flexural disease. Skin colour changes erythema appearance; look for texture and excoriation.

PLAB application: Itch and sleep disturbance are central to severity assessment.

Eczema management

Use liberal leave-on emollients regularly, soap substitutes, avoidance of irritants and topical corticosteroids of appropriate potency for active inflammation according to site and age. Explain fingertip units and a written flare plan.

PLAB application: Do not withhold appropriate topical steroids through exaggerated fear; avoid potent steroids on face without specialist guidance.

Infected eczema and eczema herpeticum

Bacterial infection may cause weeping, pustules, crusting and worsening eczema; not every flare requires antibiotics. Eczema herpeticum presents with painful monomorphic punched-out erosions, fever or malaise and can threaten vision.

PLAB application: Suspected eczema herpeticum needs same-day specialist assessment and systemic antiviral treatment.

Impetigo

Impetigo is a contagious superficial bacterial infection, commonly with honey-coloured crusts. Localised non-bullous disease may be treated with topical antiseptic according to NICE guidance; widespread or bullous disease often needs oral antibiotics.

PLAB application: Advise hygiene, avoiding sharing towels and exclusion from school until no longer infectious under current guidance.

Cellulitis and erysipelas

Cellulitis produces spreading warmth, tenderness and swelling, sometimes fever. Mark borders, assess systemic illness and consider alternative diagnoses. Facial or periorbital infection, rapidly progressive pain or toxicity needs urgent escalation.

PLAB application: Pain out of proportion, shock or rapid progression suggests necrotising infection.

Scabies

Scabies causes intense itch, often worse at night, with papules and burrows; typical sites include finger webs and wrists, while infants may have palms, soles and scalp involvement. Treat close contacts simultaneously and follow preparation-specific instructions.

PLAB application: Persistent itch for several weeks after treatment does not automatically mean treatment failure.

Head lice and tinea capitis

Head lice are diagnosed by finding live lice; use wet combing or appropriate treatment. Tinea capitis causes patchy hair loss, scaling and sometimes a boggy kerion; scalp infection generally needs systemic antifungal therapy after appropriate testing.

PLAB application: Topical antifungal shampoo alone is inadequate for tinea capitis.

Urticaria and angioedema

Urticaria consists of transient itchy wheals that move or disappear within 24 hours; viral illness is a common childhood trigger. Non-sedating antihistamines may help. Assess airway, breathing and circulation if swelling or systemic symptoms occur.

PLAB application: Urticaria with breathing difficulty, hypotension or airway symptoms suggests anaphylaxis.

Viral exanthems

Common viral rashes accompany fever or respiratory symptoms. Assess for measles exposure and vaccination, varicella vesicles, hand-foot-mouth disease and roseola. Morphology alone is not always diagnostic.

PLAB application: Suspected measles needs infection-control precautions and public-health notification.

Scarlet fever

Group A streptococcal scarlet fever often causes fever, sore throat, sandpaper-textured rash and strawberry tongue. Assess hydration and severity, use guideline-based antibiotics and advise exclusion per UKHSA guidance.

PLAB application: Consider alternative serious diagnoses when a child is systemically unwell.

Chickenpox and complications

Varicella typically produces itchy vesicles in crops at different stages. Give supportive advice, avoid aspirin, and recognise bacterial superinfection, dehydration, neurological complications and risk in immunocompromised children.

PLAB application: Discuss high-risk exposure or severe disease promptly with specialists.

Kawasaki disease

Consider Kawasaki disease in prolonged fever with bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, red cracked lips, oral changes, extremity swelling or peeling, and rash. Incomplete presentations occur, especially in infants.

PLAB application: Persistent fever for five days or longer should prompt consideration of Kawasaki disease and urgent assessment.

IgA vasculitis

IgA vasculitis causes palpable purpura usually on legs and buttocks, abdominal pain and arthralgia; renal disease may appear later. Check blood pressure and urinalysis, arrange follow-up for renal monitoring.

PLAB application: Severe abdominal pain may indicate intussusception; check for renal involvement.

Non-blanching rash and meningococcal disease

Petechiae and purpura can have multiple causes, but a child with fever, toxicity, poor perfusion or rapidly spreading non-blanching lesions needs immediate emergency assessment. Absence of rash does not exclude meningococcal sepsis.

PLAB application: Do not delay resuscitation and urgent antibiotics while waiting for rash progression.

PLAB 2: explaining skin treatment to parents

Ask what parents think causes the rash and explore treatment concerns. Demonstrate emollient and topical steroid application, clarify contagious conditions, give written safety-netting for fever, spreading redness, dehydration or painful blisters.

PLAB application: Use teach-back to confirm a parent can describe when to seek urgent care.

PLAB 2 — Eczema counselling station

Assess impact on sleep and family, explain the chronic relapsing course, demonstrate emollients and topical steroids, explore steroid worries and give a clear flare and infection safety-net plan.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Paediatric Dermatology

Eczema severity

Assess itch, sleep loss, affected area, infection and psychosocial impact. Treatment is stepped according to severity and body site.

Topical corticosteroid counselling

Choose potency by severity and site, apply to active areas as prescribed, and continue emollients. Review inadequate response and adherence before escalation.

Distinguishing bacterial infection from flare

Weeping or crusting can occur without infection. Consider spreading erythema, pustules, systemic symptoms and response to usual eczema treatment.

Rash morphology

Macules are flat; papules raised; vesicles fluid-filled; wheals transient; purpura non-blanching. Distribution and systemic findings guide diagnosis.

Drug reactions and severe skin emergencies

Fever, mucosal erosions, blistering or epidermal detachment after a new drug raises concern for Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis and requires emergency referral.

Skin infections and safeguarding

Consider household contacts, treatment access, recurrent infection and neglect only within a sensitive, evidence-based holistic assessment.
