Metabolic Emergencies: DKA, HHS, Hypoglycaemia & Electrolyte Disturbances

Emergency red flags: reduced consciousness, seizures, severe acidosis, shock, ECG changes or severe potassium disturbance require immediate ABCDE assessment and senior escalation. Adult and paediatric DKA protocols differ.

Metabolic emergencies: ABCDE

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure; obtain bedside glucose, ketones, ECG, venous blood gas, electrolytes and renal function. Escalate instability immediately.

PLAB application: Treat life-threatening abnormalities while investigating the cause.

Recognising diabetic ketoacidosis

DKA involves hyperglycaemia or sometimes near-normal glucose, ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis. Symptoms include polyuria, thirst, vomiting, abdominal pain and Kussmaul breathing.

PLAB application: Check ketones and acid–base status, not glucose alone.

DKA diagnostic approach

In adults, UK JBDS criteria generally use glucose >11 mmol/L or known diabetes, blood ketones ≥3 mmol/L or significant ketonuria, and venous bicarbonate <15 mmol/L and/or pH <7.3. Local criteria govern practice.

PLAB application: A patient taking SGLT2 inhibitors may develop euglycaemic DKA.

DKA immediate treatment

Use adult DKA protocol: monitored isotonic IV fluid, fixed-rate IV insulin where indicated, potassium replacement according to laboratory results, and investigation of precipitating illness.

PLAB application: Check potassium before and throughout insulin treatment.

DKA monitoring and resolution

Monitor glucose, capillary ketones, blood gas, potassium, fluid balance and clinical status. Add glucose-containing fluid when indicated while insulin continues to clear ketones.

PLAB application: Do not stop insulin solely because glucose normalises.

DKA precipitating factors

Common triggers include infection, missed insulin, new diabetes, myocardial infarction, steroids and SGLT2 inhibitors. Check for sepsis and other acute illness without assuming infection.

PLAB application: Treat the precipitant alongside metabolic correction.

Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

HHS typically features profound hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality and dehydration with little significant ketoacidosis; it often develops over days in older adults with type 2 diabetes.

PLAB application: Neurological changes and severe dehydration are common.

HHS treatment principles

Prioritise careful fluid replacement and monitoring of osmolality, sodium and cardiovascular status. Insulin is not automatically started at presentation unless significant ketonaemia or other indications exist.

PLAB application: Rapid osmotic shifts can cause harm; follow JBDS HHS guidance.

Hypoglycaemia recognition

Sweating, tremor, hunger, palpitations, confusion, seizures and coma can occur. Check capillary glucose immediately; assess ability to swallow safely.

PLAB application: A low glucose is a reversible cause of altered consciousness.

Hypoglycaemia treatment

If conscious and safe to swallow, give rapid-acting oral glucose and recheck. If unconscious or unable to swallow, use IV glucose or IM glucagon according to local guidance, then reassess and give longer-acting carbohydrate when safe.

PLAB application: Never give oral glucose to an unconscious patient.

Hyperkalaemia: recognition

High potassium can cause weakness and dangerous arrhythmias; ECG may show peaked T waves, broad QRS or bradyarrhythmias, but a normal ECG does not exclude risk.

PLAB application: Severe hyperkalaemia requires urgent treatment even without ECG changes.

Hyperkalaemia: emergency treatment

Protect the myocardium with IV calcium when indicated; shift potassium intracellularly using insulin with glucose and consider nebulised salbutamol; remove potassium and treat the cause. Repeat potassium and glucose monitoring.

PLAB application: IV calcium stabilises the heart but does not remove potassium.

Hypokalaemia

Low potassium can cause weakness, ileus and arrhythmias; ECG may show flattened T waves and U waves. Replace potassium by appropriate route and check magnesium, renal function and ECG.

PLAB application: Avoid rapid unmonitored IV potassium administration.

Hyponatraemia and seizures

Severe symptomatic hyponatraemia may cause headache, confusion, seizures or coma. Seek urgent senior help and use controlled hypertonic saline according to protocol while monitoring sodium closely.

PLAB application: Overly rapid sodium correction risks osmotic demyelination.

Hypernatraemia and calcium disturbances

Hypernatraemia often reflects water deficit and requires careful assessment and controlled correction. Severe hypercalcaemia causes dehydration, confusion and arrhythmias; symptomatic hypocalcaemia can cause tetany, seizures or QT prolongation.

PLAB application: Identify the cause and correct electrolyte disturbances at a safe rate.

PLAB 2: emergency handover and safety

Explain the diagnosis, urgent blood tests, IV treatment and monitoring; check medication history and sick-day knowledge. Use SBAR and document reassessment, escalation and safe discharge criteria.

PLAB application: Use separate paediatric DKA pathways for children and adolescents.

PLAB 2 — confused patient with diabetes

Call for help; use ABCDE, bedside glucose and ketones, ECG, venous blood gas and electrolyte testing. Treat immediately reversible problems, initiate the appropriate adult or paediatric pathway, reassess and communicate with SBAR.

Deep Dive — Metabolic Emergencies

DKA versus HHS

DKA is dominated by ketosis and acidosis; HHS by hyperosmolality and profound dehydration. Mixed presentations occur, and treatment follows the predominant physiology and specialist protocols.

Euglycaemic DKA

SGLT2 inhibitors, pregnancy or reduced carbohydrate intake can contribute to significant ketonaemia and acidosis without marked hyperglycaemia. Always measure ketones when clinically suspicious.

Potassium during DKA

Serum potassium may initially appear normal or high despite total-body depletion. Insulin drives potassium into cells, so serial electrolyte monitoring and protocol-guided replacement are essential.

Hypoglycaemia recurrence

Sulfonylureas and long-acting insulin can cause recurrent hypoglycaemia; observation and additional treatment may be required even after initial recovery.

Hyperkalaemia ECG pitfalls

A normal ECG cannot rule out severe hyperkalaemia. Management depends on potassium concentration, clinical condition, ECG and the local emergency algorithm.

Safe sodium correction

Neurological symptoms may require urgent hypertonic saline, but overall sodium correction must be controlled to avoid osmotic demyelination, especially in chronic hyponatraemia.
