Abdominal mass: finding the origin before naming the disease

An abdominal mass is an abnormal focal swelling detected by the patient or by examination. It may arise from the skin or abdominal wall, a distended organ, an enlarged viscus, a cyst, a tumour, an aneurysm, or stool. Unlike generalised distension, a mass usually has a localisable border or point of origin. The challenge is to determine where it arises, whether it is dangerous now and what investigation or referral is most appropriate.

1. Initial triage: stable mass or dangerous presentation?

Start with ABCDE and vital signs if the patient is unwell, especially if there is acute pain, collapse, hypotension, fever, guarding, vomiting or a painful irreducible groin lump. A pulsatile expansile abdominal mass with severe abdominal/back pain or shock suggests an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA); absence of a palpable aneurysm does not exclude rupture. Avoid delaying vascular resuscitation for routine tests in an unstable patient. A painful irreducible hernia with vomiting, peritonism or systemic features may signify strangulation. Sudden unilateral pelvic pain with vomiting may represent ovarian torsion; amenorrhoea, pain or vaginal bleeding raises concern for ectopic pregnancy. These need emergency referral rather than a routine outpatient ultrasound.

Emergency red flags: syncope or shock; abrupt severe abdominal/back pain; peritoneal rigidity; rapid enlargement; suspected bowel obstruction; absent pulses with ischaemic symptoms; fever/sepsis; acute pelvic pain with pregnancy possibility; and painful irreducible hernia. An unexplained hard or enlarging mass with weight loss or anaemia also needs expedited evaluation, even if the patient is stable.

Orientation schematic only: structures overlap. Pelvic/bladder/uterine masses may arise centrally and extend upward; retroperitoneal lesions often have indistinct borders.

Abdominal wall vs intra-abdominal: ask the patient to lift their head or tense the abdominal muscles (Carnett-style manoeuvre). An abdominal-wall swelling may remain palpable or become more obvious when muscles tense, while many deeper masses become harder to feel. This is supportive, not definitive. Evaluate for hernia with standing and cough impulse when safe; do not forcibly reduce a painful, irreducible or potentially strangulated hernia.

4. The abdominal mass examination: precise language matters

  1. Consent, exposure, comfort: inspect from chest to pubis with dignity; ask about pain before palpation.
  2. Inspect: site, contour, surgical scars, veins, visible peristalsis, movement with respiration, skin changes, cough/standing effect.
  3. Palpate away from pain: location, size in centimetres, shape, margins, surface (smooth/nodular), consistency (soft/firm/hard), tenderness, warmth, mobility, fixation, fluctuation, pulsatility and relation to respiration.
  4. Percuss: dullness vs resonance; assess surrounding organomegaly or ascites. Ballot a suspected renal mass when appropriate; distinguish an enlarged spleen (often moves with respiration, may have notch) from other LUQ masses.
  5. Auscultate: bowel sounds when clinically relevant; a bruit may be present over vascular lesions but is not reliably sensitive. Do not use vigorous palpation in a suspected unstable aneurysm.
  6. Complete the examination: groin hernial orifices, supraclavicular nodes, lower limbs, rectal or pelvic examination only with indication, explanation, consent and chaperone as appropriate; test pregnancy where relevant.
Key distinction: a pulsatile mass can merely transmit aortic pulsations. An expansile aneurysm pushes the examiner's fingers outward; still, neither absence nor presence of a clinical sign alone safely excludes or diagnoses AAA. Ultrasound/CT is required in the appropriate clinical setting.

6. Investigation: ultrasound, CT and cancer pathways

Begin with tests that change management: FBC (anaemia, blood cell abnormalities), CRP if infection suspected, electrolytes/creatinine, liver tests as indicated, urinalysis and pregnancy test when relevant. No blood test alone classifies an abdominal mass, and routine CA125/CEA/CA19-9 panels are not appropriate screening tools for all lumps.

Ultrasound is often first line for abdominal/pelvic lumps, characterising fluid versus solid lesions, organ of origin, hepatosplenomegaly, bladder retention and suspected AAA in stable patients. Contrast CT abdomen/pelvis is often needed for suspected bowel mass, deep lesion, complicated infection, staging or an uncertain ultrasound, when appropriate for stability, renal function and pregnancy. CT angiography is relevant to vascular emergency or suspected aneurysm rupture in a sufficiently stable patient, directed by vascular teams.

NICE NG12 (current guidance): In patients with female reproductive organs, a clinically identified pelvic/abdominal mass or ascites not clearly due to uterine fibroids warrants referral on a suspected ovarian cancer pathway. For abdominal mass potentially suggesting colorectal cancer, NICE recommends offering quantitative FIT as part of assessment, with safety-netting and referral based on clinical findings; a clinically detected rectal mass is a separate suspected-cancer referral consideration. Do not use a negative FIT to dismiss a clinically suspicious palpable mass.

If a patient has a palpable enlarged spleen without explanation, NICE recommends considering a suspected non-Hodgkin lymphoma referral, especially with systemic symptoms. An upper-abdominal mass with obstructive jaundice warrants urgent investigation for biliary or pancreatic pathology. Imaging strategy and referral should be adapted to the whole presentation, not one isolated risk criterion.

7. Immediate management and safe communication

A. Unstable, painful, expanding or peritonitic mass

ABCDE, urgent senior/vascular/surgical assessment, IV access and monitored resuscitation; treat shock and sepsis according to context. Do not delay emergency transfer or surgery for a routine outpatient scan. For a possible ruptured AAA, follow emergency vascular and major haemorrhage pathways.

B. Stable abdominal or pelvic mass

Document the physical findings precisely, arrange targeted laboratory and imaging tests, explain possibilities without prematurely diagnosing cancer, and ensure an accountable referral/follow-up plan. For a mass meeting suspected-cancer criteria, make the referral promptly and communicate urgency sensitively. Include results tracking and safety-netting.

C. Likely benign abdominal wall mass or hernia

If uncomplicated and low risk, discuss observation versus elective treatment and review if it enlarges or changes. For a hernia, warn about pain, irreducibility, vomiting or skin changes as reasons for urgent evaluation. A new or growing soft-tissue mass that is deep, fixed, large or otherwise suspicious merits appropriate soft-tissue assessment rather than assumption of lipoma.

8. Worked clinical cases

Case 1 — The shock patient with back pain

A 74-year-old smoker reports sudden abdominal and back pain, becomes pale and hypotensive, and has an expansile-feeling abdominal mass. Prioritise: suspected ruptured AAA; activate emergency vascular/major haemorrhage care, ABCDE and coordinated resuscitation. A missing palpable mass would not reassure you. Avoid a time-consuming routine work-up that delays definitive treatment.


Case 2 — A pelvic mass with early satiety

A 61-year-old with ovaries has early satiety, abdominal fullness and a palpable pelvic mass. Reasoning: ovarian pathology including malignancy must be excluded. Action: suspected ovarian cancer pathway referral per NG12 (unless the mass is obviously fibroids), explain uncertainty, arrange tests within local pathway and ensure results are followed up.


Case 3 — The LUQ lump and B symptoms

A 43-year-old has a LUQ mass, drenching night sweats and fatigue. The mass descends with inspiration, suggesting splenomegaly. Action: FBC, film, LFT and appropriate ultrasound and haematology review; unexplained splenomegaly can meet urgent lymphoma referral consideration under NG12. Avoid contact sports or abdominal trauma until significant splenic enlargement is assessed.


Case 4 — Not every suprapubic mass is a tumour

An 82-year-old taking anticholinergic medication has lower abdominal discomfort, poor urine flow and a smooth tender suprapubic swelling. Reasoning: acute urinary retention. Action: assess and confirm bladder fullness with bedside scan if available; catheterise under appropriate policy, record output and monitor for post-obstructive problems. Reassess the cause of retention and arrange follow-up.

9. PLAB 2 station: explaining an unexplained lump

Task: A 58-year-old noticed a firm abdominal lump three weeks ago and fears cancer. Take a focused history, explain your plan and address concerns.
  1. Introduce, confirm identity and ask what the patient is worried about. Assess severity, pain, growth and red flags first.
  2. Ask systemic symptoms, bowel and urinary changes, bleeding, reproductive history where relevant, family history and previous operations.
  3. Explain an examination using consent and a chaperone where needed. Describe lump characteristics in clear language.
  4. Explain that causes include hernias, enlarged organs, cysts and sometimes tumours; do not reassure falsely or diagnose without tests.
  5. Arrange targeted blood tests/imaging and the appropriate urgent referral if indicated; explain expected next steps and follow-up responsibility.
  6. Safety-net: urgent help for collapse, sudden severe pain, rapid growth, vomiting, inability to pass stool or urine, fever or irreducible tender hernia.

Example: “I can understand why finding a lump is worrying. There are several possible causes, and the examination and scan will help us work out where it comes from. Because it is new and firm, I would like to investigate it promptly rather than guess.”

Mechanism and integrated reasoning

Deep Dive: why the examination works

1. Embryology and peritoneal anatomy

Intraperitoneal organs (such as much of the liver and spleen) are attached through mesenteric/peritoneal structures; a mass within them may move with respiration as the diaphragm descends. Retroperitoneal kidney or pancreatic lesions are often harder to define and may be palpable as deep flank or upper abdominal fullness. Organ mobility varies with disease, body habitus and fixation, so these signs are clues rather than definitive tests.

2. Percussion, ballotability and visceral enlargement

Gas-filled bowel gives a resonant percussion note; fluid or solid tissues are usually dull. A markedly full bladder may produce central suprapubic dullness. Renal masses can sometimes be balloted between anterior and posterior hands; large kidneys may be felt from the loin. A splenic edge can have a notch and typically extends inferomedially; significant enlargement raises risks of rupture with trauma. A palpable liver edge alone is not necessarily pathological: body habitus and diaphragmatic position matter.

3. Pulsation versus expansion

The abdominal aorta lies anterior to the vertebral bodies. A mass just anterior to it can transmit pulsation without being an aneurysm. True aneurysmal expansion produces lateral movement of the examiner’s fingertips, but physical examination is insensitive, particularly in obesity. Clinical instability and sudden pain override an equivocal examination; emergent vascular assessment is essential.

4. Solid, cystic and inflammatory masses

A cyst is fluid-filled, often smooth and fluctuant; a solid mass may be firm or irregular. However, clinical fluctuation is unreliable deep in the abdomen. Ultrasound uses echo characteristics, septations and Doppler vascularity to identify organ of origin. CT evaluates enhancement, fat planes, local spread and adjacent structures. Inflammatory phlegmon/abscess can resemble a neoplasm, and imaging with clinical findings helps avoid unsafe biopsy or drainage decisions.

5. Cancer biology is not diagnosed by palpation

Malignant masses may be hard, irregular, fixed or accompanied by cachexia or lymphadenopathy, but benign lesions can also be firm and cancers may be soft or asymptomatic. Histology is often needed where diagnosis remains uncertain, planned by the relevant specialist to avoid biopsy of vascular lesions and to preserve surgical planes. Tumour markers should not be used as untargeted screening in a palpable abdominal mass.

6. Hypersplenism and portal hypertension

Portal hypertension increases splenic venous pressure and may lead to congestion and splenomegaly. An enlarged spleen can sequester platelets, leucocytes and erythrocytes, causing cytopenias. Haematological malignancy, infection and autoimmune disorders are alternative causes. FBC, blood film, liver tests and imaging help target subsequent investigations.

7. The bladder as a 'mass'

Urine produced by the kidneys accumulates in the bladder. Outflow obstruction or impaired detrusor contraction can cause marked enlargement with suprapubic dullness and discomfort. Causes include prostate enlargement, constipation, anticholinergic medications and neurological disease. Bedside bladder scanning helps distinguish retention from other pelvic pathology. Management includes appropriate decompression and monitoring renal function, residual volume and post-obstructive diuresis when relevant.

8. Population-specific interpretation

Pregnancy: evaluate pregnancy possibility before radiation-based imaging when feasible, but do not delay emergency care. Children: an unexplained abdominal mass requires careful paediatric evaluation; large renal masses should not be repeatedly manipulated. Older adults: consider malignancy and AAA, but also common reversible causes such as retention or faecal loading. Patients with ovaries: avoid attributing a persistent new pelvic mass to IBS without the proper examination and cancer pathway.
