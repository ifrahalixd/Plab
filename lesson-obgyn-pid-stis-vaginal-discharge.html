Pelvic Inflammatory Disease, STIs & Vaginal Discharge

Red flags: Severe pelvic pain, collapse, peritonism, pregnancy-associated pain, sepsis or suspected tubo-ovarian abscess require urgent assessment.

Clinical priorities

Vaginal discharge can be physiological, infective or associated with cervicitis or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). First assess pregnancy possibility, haemodynamic stability, severe pain, fever and safeguarding.

PLAB application: Do not assume all discharge is an STI.

Focused history

Ask about colour, consistency, odour, itching, dysuria, dyspareunia, postcoital bleeding, pelvic pain, fever, last menstrual period, contraception, pregnancy risk, recent antibiotics, new partners and previous STIs. Ask permission before sensitive questions.

PLAB application: Use non-judgemental language and offer confidentiality.

Examination and consent

Record observations and abdominal findings. With consent, offer external and speculum examination when indicated; consider bimanual examination for pelvic tenderness, cervical motion tenderness or adnexal tenderness. Explain chaperone options.

PLAB application: A severely unwell patient needs urgent escalation, not a delayed routine examination.

Physiological discharge

Normal discharge varies through the menstrual cycle and is usually non-offensive without significant itching, pelvic pain or bleeding. Reassure after excluding concerning causes.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary antimicrobials.

Bacterial vaginosis

A shift in vaginal microbiota may cause thin homogeneous grey-white discharge and fishy odour, usually without marked vulval inflammation. Vaginal pH is often above 4.5; microscopy or validated testing may help.

PLAB application: Treat symptomatic cases using current BASHH/local guidance; BV is not classified as a conventional STI.

Vulvovaginal candidiasis

Typically causes intense vulval itching, soreness, erythema and thick white discharge; vaginal pH is usually normal. Diabetes, pregnancy and recent antibiotics may predispose.

PLAB application: Topical azoles are preferred in pregnancy; avoid oral fluconazole in pregnancy.

Trichomoniasis

Trichomonas vaginalis is sexually transmitted and can cause malodorous yellow-green or frothy discharge, irritation, dysuria and sometimes a strawberry cervix. NAAT is a sensitive diagnostic test.

PLAB application: Arrange partner treatment and advise abstinence until treatment is completed as instructed.

Chlamydia

Chlamydia trachomatis is often asymptomatic but may cause cervicitis, postcoital bleeding, dysuria, pelvic pain and PID. Use appropriate NAAT sampling, including relevant exposed sites.

PLAB application: Offer partner notification, testing for other STIs and treatment following current guidance.

Gonorrhoea

Neisseria gonorrhoeae may cause mucopurulent cervicitis, dysuria or PID and can be asymptomatic. Obtain NAAT and, where feasible, culture for susceptibility before treatment.

PLAB application: Antimicrobial resistance matters; follow current BASHH regimen and test-of-cure advice.

PID: diagnosis

PID is infection/inflammation of the upper genital tract. Symptoms include bilateral lower abdominal pain, deep dyspareunia, abnormal bleeding or discharge and fever; cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness support diagnosis. Clinical findings may be subtle.

PLAB application: Treat empirically when PID is suspected; negative swabs do not exclude it.

PID: investigations

Perform pregnancy test when relevant, STI NAAT, urinalysis and other tests guided by severity. Consider FBC, CRP and ultrasound for diagnostic uncertainty, severe illness or tubo-ovarian abscess; ultrasound may be normal in uncomplicated PID.

PLAB application: Exclude ectopic pregnancy and surgical causes of acute abdomen.

PID: treatment

Start broad-spectrum antibiotics promptly under current BASHH/local protocols, covering gonorrhoea, chlamydia and anaerobes. Provide analgesia, counselling, partner management and review if symptoms fail to improve within 72 hours.

PLAB application: Admit if pregnancy, severe illness, peritonitis, suspected abscess, inability to tolerate oral treatment or diagnostic uncertainty.

PID: complications

Untreated or recurrent PID can cause tubal-factor infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain and tubo-ovarian abscess. Early treatment reduces but does not eliminate these risks.

PLAB application: Explain complications without implying that infertility is inevitable.

Other diagnoses

Consider genital herpes with painful vesicles or ulcers, cervicitis, retained foreign body, irritant dermatitis, atrophic vaginitis, urinary infection, appendicitis, ovarian torsion and ectopic pregnancy.

PLAB application: Severe unilateral pain, collapse or peritonism warrants urgent assessment.

STI screening and prevention

Offer HIV and syphilis testing and site-specific STI testing based on exposure and patient choice. Discuss condoms, vaccination where relevant, partner notification, confidentiality and safeguarding.

PLAB application: Offer a sexual-health service referral where appropriate.

Safety-netting and follow-up

Advise urgent care for worsening abdominal pain, fever, vomiting, fainting, pregnancy-related pain or heavy bleeding. For PID, ensure review if no improvement within 72 hours and follow local retesting and partner-management guidance.

PLAB application: Check understanding using teach-back and document the plan.

PLAB 2 — Vaginal discharge and pelvic pain consultation

Introduce yourself; confirm identity and consent; take a sensitive, non-judgemental history including symptoms, pregnancy possibility, sexual exposure and safeguarding. Explain proposed examination and chaperone. Discuss differential diagnoses, confidential STI testing, empirical PID treatment if indicated, partner notification and clear emergency safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Why PID is treated clinically

No single symptom, swab or scan reliably excludes upper genital tract infection. Low threshold for empiric treatment reduces risk of delayed therapy.

BASHH and local protocols

STI antibiotic recommendations change as resistance patterns evolve, especially gonorrhoea. Confirm doses, duration, allergies and pregnancy status against current UK guidance.

Pregnancy and pelvic pain

Ectopic pregnancy is a must-not-miss diagnosis. Pregnancy changes antimicrobial choices and often the threshold for hospital referral.

Microbiology and vaginal pH

BV and trichomoniasis often raise vaginal pH; candidiasis usually does not. pH alone is not diagnostic and interpretation requires symptoms and appropriate tests.

Partner notification and confidentiality

Explain the purpose of notifying partners and offer sexual-health support. Confidentiality has limited safeguarding exceptions that require careful professional judgement.

Long-term outcomes

PID can lead to tubal damage and chronic pain, but risk varies. Avoid deterministic statements about future fertility and offer support and follow-up.
