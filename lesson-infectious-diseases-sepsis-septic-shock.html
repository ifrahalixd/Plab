Sepsis & Septic Shock

PLAB priority: Recognise deterioration early, perform ABCDE, call for help, give timely antibiotics and resuscitation according to current UK protocols, and reassess frequently.

01. Recognise sepsis

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Septic shock is a subset with profound circulatory and metabolic abnormalities.

PLAB application: Suspect sepsis in a deteriorating patient with possible infection, even without fever.

02. Initial ABCDE assessment

Assess airway, respiratory rate, oxygenation, circulation, blood pressure, capillary refill, consciousness, temperature and glucose. Call for senior help early.

PLAB application: Treat immediate life threats while investigating the source.

03. UK risk stratification

Use current NICE NG51 age-appropriate risk criteria and NEWS2 in adults in acute hospital settings, alongside clinical judgement. NEWS2 does not replace assessment.

PLAB application: Altered mental status, hypotension, tachypnoea and poor perfusion are concerning.

04. Sepsis versus septic shock

Sepsis requires infection-related organ dysfunction. Septic shock clinically involves persistent hypotension needing vasopressors to maintain MAP at least 65 mmHg and lactate above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation.

PLAB application: This Sepsis-3 definition is not a reason to delay escalation while awaiting lactate.

05. Common infection sources

Consider pneumonia, urinary tract infection, intra-abdominal infection, skin/soft tissue infection, meningitis, line infection and postoperative infection.

PLAB application: Ask about recent surgery, devices, immunosuppression and travel.

06. Investigations

Send blood cultures before antimicrobials when feasible without delaying treatment; measure lactate, FBC, renal function, electrolytes, CRP and relevant microbiology. Obtain imaging guided by source.

PLAB application: Repeat lactate when elevated and reassess organ function.

07. Antibiotic timing

Give IV broad-spectrum antibiotics urgently for patients at high risk or with septic shock; follow NICE timing, local antimicrobial guidelines and microbiology advice. Other risk groups require timely assessment and treatment.

PLAB application: Never delay indicated antibiotics for cultures or imaging.

08. Oxygen and respiratory support

Give oxygen for hypoxaemia, titrating to an appropriate target; seek urgent support for respiratory failure. Do not assume every patient requires maximal oxygen.

PLAB application: Consider COPD or other risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure when choosing targets.

09. IV fluids and reassessment

For hypotension or poor perfusion, give isotonic crystalloid in carefully assessed boluses according to current local/NICE protocols. Reassess after each bolus for response and fluid overload.

PLAB application: Use caution with heart failure, renal failure or frailty.

10. Vasopressors and critical care

If shock persists despite appropriate fluids, escalate to critical care for vasopressors, commonly noradrenaline, and invasive monitoring as indicated.

PLAB application: Do not repeatedly give fluids without reassessment in refractory shock.

11. Source control

Drain abscesses, remove infected lines when appropriate, relieve urinary obstruction and arrange surgery for perforation or necrotising infection.

PLAB application: Antibiotics alone may fail without source control.

12. Organ dysfunction

Monitor urine output, creatinine, mental status, platelets, oxygenation and liver function. Acute kidney injury, encephalopathy and respiratory failure may signal progression.

PLAB application: Use urine output trends and clinical assessment, not a single number alone.

13. Special populations

Older adults may have no fever; immunosuppressed people can deteriorate rapidly. Pregnancy, children and neonates need specific pathways.

PLAB application: Use the correct age- and pregnancy-specific NICE guidance.

14. Differential diagnoses

Consider pulmonary embolism, cardiogenic shock, haemorrhage, anaphylaxis, pancreatitis and adrenal crisis. These can mimic or coexist with sepsis.

PLAB application: Keep a broad differential during ABCDE.

15. Monitoring and handover

Repeat observations, NEWS2, perfusion, lactate when appropriate, fluids, antibiotic times and response. Escalate using SBAR.

PLAB application: Document time of recognition and actions taken.

16. PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that a serious infection can affect blood pressure and organs, requiring urgent tests, antibiotics, fluids and close monitoring. Check understanding and involve family with consent.

PLAB application: Communicate urgency clearly without promising outcomes.

Deep Dive

Sepsis-3 physiology

Dysregulated inflammatory, endothelial and coagulation responses can impair microcirculation and organ function.

Lactate interpretation

Lactate can rise with hypoperfusion, adrenergic stress, seizures and other causes; trend it with examination and perfusion.

Fluids versus overload

Crystalloids can improve preload but may worsen pulmonary oedema. Assess fluid responsiveness and monitor after each bolus.

Antibiotic stewardship

Empirical choice depends on suspected source, allergy, local resistance and severity; narrow therapy when microbiology becomes available.

Vasopressors

Noradrenaline supports vascular tone in persistent shock; management requires monitored critical care and correction of the cause.

Sepsis mimics

Cardiogenic, obstructive, haemorrhagic and distributive shock can overlap; repeat assessment when the response is unexpected.
