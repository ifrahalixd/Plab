Gynaecological Cancers: Cervical, Endometrial & Ovarian Cancer

Red flag: Postmenopausal bleeding, a suspicious cervix, persistent abdominal distension or ascites require timely assessment. Unstable bleeding requires emergency care.

Overview and clinical priorities

Cervical, endometrial and ovarian malignancies have different presentations and referral pathways. Assess stability, bleeding, pain, family history, examination findings and patient preferences.

PLAB application: Persistent or unexplained symptoms require assessment even after a normal screening result.

Cervical cancer: aetiology

Persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly types 16 and 18, causes most cervical cancers. Smoking and immunosuppression increase risk. HPV vaccination reduces risk but does not replace screening.

PLAB application: Vaccinated patients still follow the applicable NHS cervical screening programme.

Cervical cancer: symptoms

Postcoital bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, persistent unusual discharge, dyspareunia and pelvic pain can occur. Early disease may be asymptomatic. Examine the cervix with consent and a chaperone when appropriate.

PLAB application: A suspicious-looking cervix warrants urgent specialist referral; do not wait for a screening test.

Cervical screening versus diagnostic assessment

NHS cervical screening is designed for asymptomatic people with a cervix and uses HPV-based testing; it is not a diagnostic test for cancer in someone with concerning symptoms. Screening intervals and eligibility vary across UK nations and may change.

PLAB application: Do not offer a routine smear as the investigation for postcoital bleeding.

Cervical cancer: diagnosis and treatment

Specialist colposcopy-directed biopsy establishes histology. Staging may include pelvic MRI and other imaging. Management can include excision or surgery for selected early stages and chemoradiotherapy for more advanced disease.

PLAB application: Explain that biopsy confirms diagnosis and treatment depends on stage and fertility goals.

Endometrial cancer: risks

Risk increases with age, obesity, prolonged unopposed oestrogen exposure, anovulation such as PCOS, tamoxifen use and Lynch syndrome. Some patients have no recognised risk factors.

PLAB application: Ask about HRT, menstrual history, metabolic risk and relevant family cancers.

Endometrial cancer: presentation

Postmenopausal bleeding is a classic symptom. Premenopausal patients may present with persistent irregular or heavy bleeding. Benign causes are common but malignancy must be considered.

PLAB application: Unexplained postmenopausal bleeding needs prompt assessment and referral under local/NICE pathways.

Endometrial cancer: investigation

Transvaginal ultrasound assesses the endometrium; endometrial sampling confirms histology when indicated. A thin endometrium is reassuring in selected contexts but persistent or recurrent bleeding still requires review.

PLAB application: Never dismiss persistent bleeding solely because an initial ultrasound is reassuring.

Endometrial cancer: management

Management commonly involves hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and risk-adapted staging. Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy or hormonal options depend on tumour characteristics and stage.

PLAB application: Discuss fertility, menopause consequences and specialist multidisciplinary decisions.

Ovarian cancer: risk and pathology

Ovarian cancers are heterogeneous; epithelial tumours are common. Risk increases with age and inherited variants such as BRCA1/2 or Lynch-associated syndromes. A family history may prompt genetics assessment.

PLAB application: Ask about breast, ovarian, colorectal and endometrial cancer in relatives.

Ovarian cancer: symptoms

Persistent abdominal bloating or distension, early satiety, pelvic/abdominal pain and urinary urgency or frequency are important symptoms, particularly if new and frequent. Ascites and a pelvic mass may occur.

PLAB application: Do not attribute persistent bloating automatically to IBS.

Ovarian cancer: primary care tests

NICE NG12 recommends serum CA125 for patients with symptoms suggesting ovarian cancer; if CA125 is 35 IU/mL or greater, arrange ultrasound of abdomen and pelvis. Consider age, menopausal status, symptoms and local pathways.

PLAB application: CA125 is not specific and a normal result does not always exclude cancer.

Ovarian cancer: specialist assessment

Specialists use ultrasound, imaging, tumour markers and tissue diagnosis where appropriate. Treatment often combines cytoreductive surgery and platinum-based chemotherapy; targeted maintenance therapy may be used according to tumour genetics and response.

PLAB application: Explain that final treatment planning is undertaken by the gynaecological oncology MDT.

Red flags and urgent referral

Suspicious cervical appearance, unexplained postmenopausal bleeding, concerning pelvic mass or ascites, and persistent ovarian-cancer symptoms merit urgent evaluation. Significant bleeding, severe pain or haemodynamic compromise require emergency assessment.

PLAB application: Follow current NICE NG12 and local suspected-cancer referral criteria.

Communicating possible cancer

Ask what the patient understands and fears. Explain that tests are needed to identify the cause, avoid prematurely confirming cancer, give clear referral timing and safety-net. Offer an interpreter and chaperone as needed.

PLAB application: Use empathetic, non-alarmist language and check understanding.

Prevention, follow-up and survivorship

HPV vaccination and cervical screening reduce cervical cancer burden. Encourage healthy lifestyle measures without implying blame. After treatment, discuss surveillance, menopausal symptoms, sexual wellbeing, fertility, psychological support and hereditary-risk assessment.

PLAB application: Offer clear written follow-up and red-flag advice.

PLAB 2 — Possible gynaecological cancer consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent, explore bleeding/bloating and associated symptoms, family history and concerns. Explain investigations and urgent referral sensitively without asserting a diagnosis. Offer chaperone, safety-net and follow-up.

Deep Dive

Why HPV matters

High-risk HPV persistence can cause cervical intraepithelial neoplasia and eventually invasive carcinoma. Vaccination is preventive; abnormal symptoms still require diagnostic evaluation.

Endometrial thickness nuance

Ultrasound thickness thresholds are interpreted in the context of bleeding, HRT and local guidance. Persistent bleeding may require biopsy even after a reassuring scan.

CA125 limitations

CA125 can rise in endometriosis, fibroids and other benign or inflammatory conditions; not every ovarian cancer produces elevated CA125. Clinical concern remains important.

Inherited cancer syndromes

BRCA-associated hereditary breast–ovarian cancer and Lynch syndrome affect cancer risks and can warrant specialist genetic counselling.

PLAB 2 examination and communication

Obtain consent for abdominal and pelvic examination, offer a chaperone, assess bleeding severity and anaemia, use clear language and acknowledge uncertainty.

Emergency versus suspected cancer

Shock, collapse, severe bleeding or acute abdomen require immediate emergency management rather than routine outpatient referral.
