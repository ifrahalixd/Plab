Osteoarthritis, arthroplasty and postoperative complications

Urgent red flags: acutely hot painful prosthetic joint, wound discharge with systemic illness, sudden painful swollen leg with breathlessness, dislocated hip prosthesis, new foot drop or neurovascular compromise. Escalate urgently.

1 · Osteoarthritis (OA): presentation

OA is a clinical syndrome of joint pain with activity and functional limitation. Common sites: knees, hips, hands and first carpometacarpal joint. Symptoms: mechanical pain, brief morning stiffness (usually ≤30 minutes), crepitus, bony enlargement and reduced range of motion. Hip OA can refer pain to groin, thigh or knee. Red, acutely swollen joints, prolonged stiffness or systemic features warrant consideration of inflammatory or infective causes.

2 · Diagnosis and differential

NICE NG226 supports clinical diagnosis in adults aged 45 or over with activity-related joint pain and no morning stiffness or stiffness lasting 30 minutes or less. Routine imaging is not required for typical OA. X-rays may show joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts, but radiographic severity may not correlate with pain. Consider rheumatoid arthritis, gout, septic arthritis, fracture, malignancy and referred pain if atypical.

3 · Core non-operative treatment

Offer tailored therapeutic exercise (local muscle strengthening and general aerobic activity) and weight management when relevant; explain that exercise may initially cause discomfort but improves function. Support education, pacing, walking aids and physiotherapy. NICE recommends topical NSAID for knee OA and consideration for other joints; if oral NSAID is needed, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time with gastroprotection and assess renal, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risks. Do not routinely offer paracetamol or weak opioids; avoid strong opioids. Consider intra-articular corticosteroid injection for short-term relief when appropriate. Do not offer arthroscopic lavage/debridement for uncomplicated OA.

4 · When to refer for joint replacement

Consider referral for hip, knee or shoulder replacement when pain, stiffness, reduced function or progressive deformity substantially affect quality of life and non-surgical treatment is ineffective or unsuitable. Referral should be based on clinical assessment and shared decision-making rather than arbitrary numerical score, BMI, age or smoking status alone. Discuss expected gains, rehabilitation, complications and patient preferences.

5 · Hip and knee arthroplasty

Total hip replacement replaces acetabular and femoral articulating surfaces; hemiarthroplasty replaces femoral head only and is usually considered for selected hip fractures, not routine OA. Knee replacement may be total or unicompartmental depending on disease pattern and suitability. Preoperative assessment includes comorbidity optimisation, anaesthetic review, medication reconciliation, infection assessment, consent and rehabilitation planning.

6 · Early postoperative care

Monitor observations, pain, wound, haemoglobin where indicated, mobility, hydration, delirium risk and distal neurovascular function. Encourage early mobilisation with physiotherapy. VTE prevention combines individualised mechanical and pharmacological measures according to NICE NG89 and bleeding risk. Follow local protocols for perioperative antibiotics and thromboprophylaxis; avoid assuming a single regimen fits all patients.

7 · Prosthetic joint infection (PJI)

Early PJI may present with persistent wound drainage, increasing pain, erythema, swelling or fever; chronic PJI may cause pain and loosening without fever. Obtain urgent orthopaedic assessment, inflammatory markers and blood cultures if febrile; aspiration and cultures should be coordinated with the specialist team. In a stable patient, avoid starting antibiotics before appropriate sampling where feasible; in sepsis, give antibiotics promptly after cultures if this does not delay treatment. Management may require debridement with implant retention in selected early infections or staged revision in chronic infection.

8 · Mechanical and thromboembolic complications

Hip dislocation: sudden severe pain, deformity and inability to bear weight after arthroplasty; document neurovascular status, radiograph and arrange urgent specialist reduction. Periprosthetic fracture: pain/deformity after fall; image and refer urgently. Loosening/wear: progressive pain or instability; specialist imaging and revision consideration. DVT/PE: new unilateral swelling, pleuritic pain, dyspnoea or hypoxia; assess promptly using VTE pathways. Nerve injury: new foot drop after hip surgery needs immediate examination and escalation.

9 · Postoperative deterioration

For fever, hypotension, tachycardia, hypoxia, confusion or reduced urine output, perform ABCDE, assess sepsis, bleeding, PE, myocardial events and other causes. Follow NICE sepsis guidance and local escalation pathways. Postoperative anaemia or haematoma may cause hypotension and require urgent assessment. Early pain out of proportion with swelling should raise concern for compartment syndrome in relevant procedures.

10 · Rehabilitation, follow-up and safety-net

Explain wound care, rehabilitation exercises, mobility aids, VTE precautions and follow-up. Advise urgent assessment for wound discharge, fever with worsening joint pain, sudden inability to weight-bear, severe calf swelling, chest pain or breathlessness. Shared decisions should address realistic functional goals and individual risks.

Decision pathways and rapid comparison

Suspected osteoarthritis

Activity-related pain + age ≥45 + stiffness ≤30 minutes → clinical diagnosis when typical → exercise and weight management → topical NSAID where appropriate → reassess function → consider arthroplasty referral if quality of life remains significantly affected.

OA versus inflammatory arthritis

OA: mechanical pain, short stiffness, bony enlargement. Inflammatory arthritis: prolonged stiffness, warm swollen joints, systemic features; assess alternative diagnoses.

Joint replacement referral

Persistent symptoms substantially impairing quality of life despite appropriate non-operative options → shared decision and orthopaedic assessment; do not exclude solely by age or BMI.

Hot painful prosthetic joint

ABCDE if unwell → urgent orthopaedic review → blood tests/cultures and coordinated joint aspiration → antibiotics urgently if sepsis; otherwise seek cultures before antibiotics when feasible → operative strategy decided by specialists.

Sudden pain after hip replacement

Check neurovascular status → X-ray → consider dislocation or periprosthetic fracture → urgent orthopaedic reduction/fixation pathway; do not attempt unsupported manipulation.

Swollen leg or dyspnoea after surgery

Consider DVT/PE → immediate clinical assessment and NICE VTE pathway → imaging and anticoagulation decisions based on bleeding risk and clinical context.

Early postoperative deterioration

ABCDE → assess bleeding, infection/sepsis, PE, myocardial causes, medications and delirium → prompt senior escalation and targeted management.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Progressive knee pain

A 66-year-old has pain on walking, crepitus and 10 minutes of morning stiffness without redness or fever. Decision: typical knee OA; diagnose clinically, offer tailored exercise, weight management where appropriate and topical NSAID.

Case 2 — Hip pain despite treatment

A 71-year-old has severe hip OA affecting sleep and daily activities despite structured exercise and suitable analgesia. Decision: discuss shared decision-making and referral for joint replacement assessment; do not impose arbitrary age or BMI exclusion.

Case 3 — Draining wound after arthroplasty

Two weeks after knee replacement, a patient has worsening pain, wound drainage and fever. Decision: suspect PJI and sepsis; urgent orthopaedic review, blood cultures and appropriate sampling, prompt antibiotics if systemically unwell.

Case 4 — Sudden hip pain after twisting

Six weeks after total hip replacement, a patient suddenly cannot bear weight and has a deformed painful leg. Decision: suspect prosthetic hip dislocation; check neurovascular status, obtain radiographs and arrange urgent specialist reduction.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Counselling about knee replacement

Station: A 68-year-old with severe knee OA asks whether surgery is the next step.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm concerns and establish the patient's goals and current function.
  2. Ask about pain, morning stiffness, sleep, mobility, falls, previous treatment and red flags for infection or inflammatory disease.
  3. Explain OA in accessible language and clarify that treatment decisions depend on symptoms and quality of life, not X-ray appearance alone.
  4. Review tailored exercise, weight management, walking aids and suitable analgesia, including NSAID risks.
  5. Explain knee replacement may reduce pain and improve function when non-surgical care is ineffective or unsuitable, but results and recovery vary.
  6. Discuss surgery, anaesthesia, rehabilitation, wound infection, prosthetic infection, DVT/PE, stiffness, persistent pain and possible revision.
  7. Discuss shared decision-making and referral without excluding solely on age or BMI; address expectations and preferences.
  8. Safety-net for a hot swollen joint, fever, acute loss of function or new neurological symptoms; check understanding and offer written information.

Examiner priorities: patient-centred explanation, conservative management, referral indications, balanced consent and safety-netting.

Rapid revision — 12 essentials

  1. OA commonly causes activity-related pain and morning stiffness ≤30 minutes.
  2. Typical OA in adults aged ≥45 can be diagnosed clinically without routine imaging.
  3. Therapeutic exercise and weight management are core treatments.
  4. Topical NSAID is first-line pharmacological option for knee OA when suitable.
  5. Oral NSAIDs require individual risk review and gastroprotection.
  6. Do not routinely offer arthroscopic lavage/debridement for uncomplicated OA.
  7. Refer for arthroplasty when symptoms significantly impair quality of life despite appropriate care.
  8. Do not exclude joint replacement referral solely by age or BMI.
  9. Early mobilisation and individualised VTE prophylaxis are key after arthroplasty.
  10. Hot painful prosthetic joint or wound discharge requires urgent PJI assessment.
  11. Sudden severe pain after hip replacement may indicate dislocation or fracture.
  12. Postoperative dyspnoea, chest pain or unilateral leg swelling requires urgent VTE assessment.
