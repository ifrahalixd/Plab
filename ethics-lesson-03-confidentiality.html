Confidentiality and information sharing

Learning outcomes: Protect identifiable patient information; recognise lawful disclosure with and without consent; distinguish direct care from other uses; handle relatives, safeguarding and public-interest concerns; document proportionate decisions.

1. Why confidentiality matters

Confidentiality is a professional and legal duty, grounded in trust. Information learned in a professional role must be protected, including after death. It includes spoken, written, photographic and electronic information. Avoid discussing identifiable patients in public areas or on unsecured channels.

2. Key frameworks

GMC Good medical practice 2024 paragraph 22 requires confidentiality, including after death. GMC's detailed Confidentiality: good practice in handling patient information guides disclosures. The UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 regulate personal data processing; the common law duty of confidentiality is a distinct consideration. A UK GDPR lawful basis does not by itself remove the common law confidentiality duty.

3. Consent and direct care

Share relevant information with the team involved in direct care on an appropriate basis, explaining to patients how information is used and respecting applicable objections. Implied consent may support sharing for direct care where its conditions are met; do not assume it applies to research, marketing or unrelated third parties. Share only what is needed, with authorised people and secure systems.

4. Disclosing without consent

Disclosure may be justified or required by law, to protect a patient or others from serious harm, or for safeguarding. Check whether consent can safely be sought, whether there is a legal obligation or sufficiently strong public interest, and whether the proposed disclosure is necessary and proportionate. Seek senior, Caldicott Guardian or information-governance advice when practicable; do not delay urgent protective action.

5. Relatives, police and employers

Relatives and partners have no automatic right to information. Ask the patient what may be shared and with whom. Police requests are not automatically authority to disclose: verify the legal basis and urgency. Employers, schools and insurers generally require appropriate consent or another valid legal justification.

6. Special circumstances

For children and young people, consider competence, best interests, safeguarding and the young person's confidentiality. A person lacking capacity may require necessary information sharing for their care and best interests, consistent with applicable law. Confidentiality continues after death, subject to lawful exceptions.

High-yield distinction

Capacity to consent to treatment ≠ consent to disclose information. A patient's treatment decision and information-sharing preferences are separate questions. A public-interest disclosure should be limited to the minimum relevant information.

Confidentiality decision pathway

Step 1 · Identify the request

Who wants the information, why, and exactly what information? Is the recipient part of the direct-care team?

Step 2 · Assess the patient's wishes

Can you explain the disclosure and seek informed consent? Check existing preferences and any objections.

Step 3 · Identify the authority

Consider consent, relevant direct-care sharing arrangements, a statutory requirement, safeguarding duties, or an overriding public interest. Check data protection requirements separately.

Step 4 · Balance harm and necessity

Is there a credible risk of serious harm? Can it be managed without disclosure? Is the disclosure necessary, proportionate and directed to the right person?

Step 5 · Share securely and record

Disclose the minimum necessary, use secure channels, inform the patient where appropriate and safe, and document the rationale, recipient and information shared.

Four clinical reasoning cases

Case 1 · A spouse calls the ward

A patient's husband asks for her HIV test result. She has not authorised disclosure.

Best approach: Do not disclose. Verify identity, seek the patient's preferences, and offer general support without revealing confidential information.

Case 2 · Serious threat to another person

A patient makes a credible, specific threat of serious violence. The risk cannot be managed by consent-based steps alone.

Best approach: Escalate urgently, assess the risk, and consider proportionate public-interest disclosure to appropriate authorities. Document the reasoning.

Case 3 · Child safeguarding concern

A 13-year-old discloses ongoing abuse and asks you to keep it secret.

Best approach: Explain the limits of confidentiality, prioritise safety and share necessary information through safeguarding procedures. Do not promise absolute secrecy.

Case 4 · Police request

An officer phones asking for the records of a patient involved in an investigation but provides no legal authority or urgent safety justification.

Best approach: Do not release records merely because the requester is police. Verify identity and legal grounds, seek information-governance advice, and disclose only if justified.

PLAB 2 simulated station · Confidentiality

Station: A patient's partner asks you to reveal the patient's newly diagnosed STI result. The patient has asked you not to share it. Explain your approach to the partner and discuss what you would do next.

Suggested structure

  1. Introduce yourself, verify who is speaking and their relationship to the patient.
  2. Listen empathetically without confirming confidential facts.
  3. Explain that you cannot share another person's private medical information without appropriate authority.
  4. Encourage direct discussion with the patient; offer appropriate general sexual-health advice and support.
  5. Assess any serious risk to others, and seek specialist/senior advice on lawful and proportionate disclosure if indicated.
  6. Document the encounter and any disclosure decision.
