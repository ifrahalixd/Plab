Delirium, Dementia & Cognitive Disorders

PLAB priority: Acute confusion is delirium until assessed; identify reversible causes, recognise hypoactive delirium and distinguish dementia subtypes.

01. Core distinction

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention and awareness, often with altered cognition. Dementia is a usually progressive decline in cognition that affects independence. Delirium may occur on top of dementia.

PLAB application: Ask family or carers about baseline cognition and exact time of onset.

02. Delirium presentation

Look for inattention, altered alertness, disorganised thinking, disturbed sleep–wake cycle and fluctuating symptoms. Hyperactive, hypoactive and mixed presentations occur. Hypoactive delirium is easily missed.

PLAB application: A quiet withdrawn older patient can be delirious.

03. Delirium triggers

Common precipitants include infection, dehydration, pain, constipation, urinary retention, hypoxia, medications, withdrawal, metabolic disturbance and unfamiliar surroundings. Multiple factors often coexist.

PLAB application: Investigate causes without assuming every positive urine dipstick means UTI.

04. Immediate assessment

Use ABCDE for acute illness, check capillary glucose and observations, review medications and assess pain, hydration, oxygenation and neurological signs. Look for sepsis, stroke and other emergencies.

PLAB application: Sudden focal deficit or reduced consciousness demands urgent escalation.

05. 4AT screening

The 4AT assesses alertness, abbreviated mental test, attention and acute change or fluctuation. A score of 4 or more suggests possible delirium, but clinical assessment remains essential.

PLAB application: Use 4AT as a screening aid, not a replacement for diagnosis.

06. Investigations

Tailor FBC, U&Es, renal function, calcium, glucose, CRP, cultures, ECG and imaging to findings. Consider drug effects and withdrawal. CT brain is not routine unless indicated by trauma, focal neurology or another concern.

PLAB application: Avoid indiscriminate tests; investigate the likely reversible causes.

07. Delirium management

Treat underlying causes, provide fluids/nutrition, orientation, glasses/hearing aids, familiar carers, sleep hygiene, mobilisation and pain relief. Avoid unnecessary catheterisation and restraints.

PLAB application: Non-pharmacological care is first-line.

08. Agitation and medication

If a patient is distressed or risks harm, first use verbal and non-verbal de-escalation. Short-term low-dose haloperidol may be considered only when necessary, after assessing contraindications and risks; use particular caution in Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia.

PLAB application: Check ECG/QT risk and local guidance; never routinely sedate delirium.

09. Dementia assessment

Obtain a history from patient and informant, assess memory and other cognitive domains, daily functioning, mood, medications, sensory deficits and reversible contributors. Use a validated cognitive tool.

PLAB application: A single screening score cannot establish the dementia subtype.

10. Alzheimer disease

Typically gradual episodic memory decline followed by broader cognitive and functional problems. NICE recommends specialist diagnostic assessment when dementia is suspected after initial workup.

PLAB application: Consider donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine for mild–moderate Alzheimer disease after appropriate assessment.

11. Vascular dementia

Often associated with cerebrovascular disease, executive dysfunction and sometimes stepwise deterioration; presentations vary. Manage vascular risk factors for general health.

PLAB application: Look for prior stroke, gait changes and vascular risk factors.

12. Lewy body dementia

Core features include fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder and spontaneous parkinsonism. Severe sensitivity to antipsychotics may occur.

PLAB application: Avoid reflexively prescribing antipsychotics for hallucinations.

13. Frontotemporal dementia

Early behavioural/personality change or language dysfunction may dominate, while memory can be relatively preserved initially. Often presents at a younger age than typical Alzheimer disease.

PLAB application: Ask about disinhibition, loss of empathy, compulsions and language changes.

14. Mild cognitive impairment

Objective cognitive decline without substantial loss of independent daily function. It may remain stable, improve or progress. Evaluate contributing factors and monitor.

PLAB application: Do not label every memory complaint as dementia.

15. Capacity, safeguarding and support

Assess decision-specific capacity under the Mental Capacity Act framework where applicable; presume capacity unless shown otherwise. Discuss driving, medication safety, falls, carer strain and advance planning sensitively.

PLAB application: In England and Wales, a dementia diagnosis alone does not mean incapacity.

16. PLAB 2 consultation

Introduce yourself, obtain consent, check hearing/vision, ask open questions, perform focused mental state and cognitive assessment, seek collateral history and explain investigations, referrals and safety-netting clearly.

PLAB application: Explain acute confusion as a medical symptom requiring prompt assessment.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Attention vs memory

Delirium prominently disrupts attention, so poor memory test performance during acute illness may not represent established dementia. Reassess cognition after delirium resolves.

Why hypoactive delirium is missed

A patient may appear sleepy or disengaged rather than agitated. Repeated observation and collateral history improve recognition.

Inflammation and vulnerability

Acute illness, systemic inflammation, metabolic disruption and medications can precipitate delirium, particularly in brains with reduced cognitive reserve.

Cholinergic and dopaminergic pathways

Alzheimer disease involves cholinergic deficits; Lewy body disease combines cognitive and movement features. Antipsychotic sensitivity in Lewy body disease influences prescribing.

Diagnostic overlap

Depression, sensory loss, sleep problems, medication effects and delirium can mimic or worsen dementia symptoms; careful longitudinal history helps.

Ethics in cognitive disorders

Assess capacity for the actual decision, support communication, consider best interests only when capacity is lacking, and protect confidentiality while appropriately involving carers.
