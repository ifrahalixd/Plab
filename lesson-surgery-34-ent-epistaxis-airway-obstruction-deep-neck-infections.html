ENT Surgical Emergencies: Epistaxis, Airway Obstruction & Deep Neck Infections

Surgery · Lesson 34 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

ENT emergencies: recognise the airway first

Immediate danger: stridor, drooling, muffled voice, rapidly progressive neck swelling, inability to swallow secretions, severe respiratory distress or haemodynamic instability. Call senior ENT, anaesthetics and resuscitation teams immediately. Avoid distressing or instrumenting a threatened airway without expert support.

1 · Initial assessment

ABCDE, vital signs, oxygen as indicated, monitoring, IV access and analgesia. Assess voice, work of breathing, stridor, swallowing, trismus, neck swelling and sepsis. Keep NBM when airway intervention or surgery may be needed. Ask about trauma, foreign bodies, infection, anticoagulants, immunosuppression and recent dental procedures. Prepare a difficult-airway plan with anaesthetics and ENT; do not delay escalation for imaging.

2 · Epistaxis: first aid

Sit patient upright and leaning forward, pinch the soft cartilaginous part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes, spit blood out. Assess haemodynamic stability and airway, especially with brisk posterior bleeding. Review antithrombotic medicines and bleeding disorders; check FBC/coagulation and crossmatch if severe. Consider topical vasoconstrictor/local anaesthetic per local protocol.

3 · Persistent epistaxis

If anterior bleeding point seen, consider silver nitrate cautery by trained clinician, avoiding bilateral opposing septal cautery. If not controlled, use appropriate nasal packing and ENT review. Posterior bleeding may cause blood in the oropharynx, large-volume haemorrhage and require admission, posterior packing, endoscopic arterial control or embolisation. Manage shock and consider transfusion when indicated. Anticoagulant reversal is based on severity and specialist guidance, not automatic for all nosebleeds.

4 · Peritonsillar abscess (quinsy)

Severe unilateral sore throat, fever, trismus, muffled “hot-potato” voice, uvular deviation and unilateral peritonsillar swelling. Urgent ENT assessment; fluids, analgesia, appropriate antibiotics and drainage (needle aspiration or incision) when indicated. Assess airway and ability to swallow; admission may be needed. Do not assume uvular deviation is always present.

5 · Deep neck space infections

Retropharyngeal/parapharyngeal abscess: fever, neck pain/stiffness, torticollis, dysphagia, drooling and possible stridor. Contrast-enhanced CT neck is useful once airway is secure and patient stable. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering likely oral flora, cultures where possible, urgent ENT and anaesthetic review; drainage for collections/clinical deterioration. Monitor for mediastinitis, sepsis and vascular complications.

6 · Ludwig angina

Rapidly spreading bilateral submandibular cellulitis, often from dental infection, with firm swelling, raised tongue, drooling, dysphagia and threatened airway. Call anaesthetics, ENT/maxillofacial surgery urgently; secure airway in a controlled expert setting, IV antibiotics and surgical source control. Do not lay flat or delay for CT if airway is threatened.

7 · Epiglottitis / supraglottitis

Severe sore throat disproportionate to oropharyngeal findings, dysphagia, drooling, muffled voice and stridor. Keep patient calm and upright; urgent anaesthetic/ENT airway management. Do not force throat examination in a distressed child with suspected epiglottitis. Start IV antibiotics per protocol after airway planning; intensive monitoring may be required.

8 · Foreign-body airway obstruction

Sudden choking, ineffective cough, inability to speak or breathe. Follow age-appropriate resuscitation algorithms (back blows/chest thrusts in infants; back blows/abdominal thrusts in older children/adults as appropriate). If unconscious, start CPR and call emergency help. Partial obstruction with effective cough: encourage coughing, monitor and arrange assessment. Suspected aspirated foreign body may require rigid bronchoscopy.

9 · Post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage

Any fresh bleeding after tonsillectomy is an emergency. Sit forward, suction available, NBM, urgent ENT and anaesthetic help, IV access, FBC, coagulation and crossmatch; resuscitate and prepare for theatre. Even a small sentinel bleed can precede catastrophic haemorrhage. Avoid repeated traumatic examination.

10 · Other urgent ENT conditions

Septal haematoma after nasal trauma requires prompt drainage to prevent cartilage necrosis. Mastoiditis presents with postauricular erythema/swelling and displaced pinna; urgent ENT, IV antibiotics and possible drainage. Malignant otitis externa (necrotising otitis externa) in diabetes/immunocompromise causes severe otalgia and granulation tissue; urgent specialist assessment. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss requires urgent ENT/audiology assessment and timely treatment, not routine wax management.

11 · Imaging and safe disposition

Use imaging only when it changes management and does not compromise airway safety. Admit for airway risk, sepsis, uncontrolled bleeding, dehydration or need for intervention. Give clear safety-netting for breathing difficulty, recurrent bleeding, inability to swallow, worsening swelling and systemic deterioration.

Emergency decision pathways

Stridor / drooling / progressive neck swelling

ABCDE → call anaesthetics + ENT ± maxillofacial team → keep upright and calm → controlled airway plan → IV antibiotics/source control where infection suspected → imaging only after stabilisation.

Active epistaxis

Lean forward + pinch soft nose 10–15 min → assess airway/circulation → inspect and cauterise visible anterior point if appropriate → packing if persistent → urgent ENT for posterior/severe bleeding, shock or failed measures.

Unilateral sore throat + trismus + muffled voice

Suspect quinsy → assess airway and hydration → ENT → antibiotics + drainage when indicated.

Bleeding after tonsillectomy

Urgent ENT and anaesthetics → NBM + IV access + bloods/crossmatch → resuscitate and prepare for possible operative haemostasis.

Nasal trauma + septal swelling

Suspect septal haematoma → same-day ENT drainage; do not discharge as uncomplicated fracture.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Persistent nosebleed

A 72-year-old taking apixaban has ongoing epistaxis after 15 minutes of correct compression and feels dizzy. Priority: ABCDE, haemodynamic assessment, IV access and blood tests as indicated, inspect and treat bleeding site, ENT escalation if uncontrolled. Review anticoagulation with specialist advice.

Case 2 — Hot-potato voice

A 24-year-old has unilateral sore throat, trismus and uvular deviation. Diagnosis: peritonsillar abscess. Plan: urgent ENT review, analgesia, hydration, antibiotics and drainage as indicated.

Case 3 — Swollen floor of mouth

A 48-year-old with dental infection has rapidly progressive submandibular swelling, drooling and difficulty speaking. Diagnosis: Ludwig angina. Priority: emergency airway team and surgical source control; do not delay for CT.

Case 4 — Blood after tonsillectomy

A child returns 6 days after tonsillectomy with fresh oral bleeding, currently alert. Priority: treat as potentially life-threatening, urgent ENT/anaesthetic review, NBM, IV access, prepare for resuscitation/theatre.

PLAB 2 OSCE — emergency epistaxis

Station: A 65-year-old patient presents with heavy nosebleed and is taking anticoagulants.

  1. Introduce yourself, ask for assistance, assess ABCDE, vital signs and signs of shock.
  2. Seat upright leaning forward; instruct continuous pressure over soft nose for 10–15 minutes.
  3. Ask about duration, blood loss, swallowing blood, trauma, previous bleeds, medicines and bleeding disorders.
  4. Arrange IV access, blood tests/crossmatch if clinically indicated; analgesia and topical measures per local policy.
  5. Inspect once bleeding is controlled; explain cautery or packing, and ENT involvement if bleeding persists.
  6. Explain that severe or posterior bleeding may need admission or an operation; review anticoagulation with appropriate team.
  7. Document response, reassess and safety-net clearly.

Exam pitfall: do not tilt the head backwards; this increases swallowing/aspiration of blood.

High-yield revision

  1. Stridor, drooling or rapid neck swelling: call airway experts immediately.
  2. Epistaxis first aid: lean forward and pinch soft nose for 10–15 minutes.
  3. Posterior epistaxis may be severe and require packing, surgery or embolisation.
  4. Quinsy: unilateral throat pain, trismus, muffled voice; antibiotics and drainage.
  5. Ludwig angina: dental source, raised tongue, bilateral submandibular swelling, airway risk.
  6. Epiglottitis: keep calm, avoid distressing examination, urgent airway plan.
  7. Deep neck abscess: CT with contrast only when safe, IV antibiotics and specialist drainage.
  8. Any fresh post-tonsillectomy bleeding needs emergency ENT assessment.
  9. Septal haematoma must be drained urgently.
  10. Mastoiditis: postauricular swelling and displaced pinna.
  11. Severe otalgia in diabetic patient may indicate necrotising otitis externa.
  12. Never delay airway management for radiology.
