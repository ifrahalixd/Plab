Anaemia: Iron, Vitamin B12 & Folate Deficiency

PLAB safety: Assess instability and bleeding urgently; neurological B12 deficiency requires prompt treatment.

01. Approach to anaemia

Anaemia is haemoglobin below the laboratory reference range. Ask about fatigue, dyspnoea, palpitations, bleeding, diet, pregnancy, medications and chronic illness. Assess severity and haemodynamic stability.

PLAB focus: Urgent assessment for chest pain, syncope, active bleeding or instability.

02. Classify by MCV

Microcytic: iron deficiency, thalassaemia, chronic inflammation. Normocytic: bleeding, kidney disease, inflammation or haemolysis. Macrocytic: B12/folate deficiency, alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism or drugs.

PLAB focus: MCV guides, but does not replace, cause investigation.

03. Initial investigations

FBC and film, reticulocyte count, ferritin and iron studies when indicated; B12, folate, renal function, CRP, thyroid tests and haemolysis markers according to context.

PLAB focus: Check previous results and avoid indiscriminate testing.

04. Iron deficiency pathophysiology

Low iron stores impair haem synthesis. Common causes include menstrual or gastrointestinal blood loss, inadequate intake, pregnancy and malabsorption such as coeliac disease.

PLAB focus: Identify the cause rather than only replacing iron.

05. Iron deficiency presentation

Fatigue, pallor, exertional breathlessness, pica, restless legs, glossitis and koilonychia can occur. Microcytosis and raised RDW are common but not universal.

PLAB focus: Early iron deficiency can be normocytic.

06. Iron investigations

Low ferritin strongly supports iron deficiency, but inflammation may elevate ferritin. Transferrin saturation and CRP can help when results are equivocal.

PLAB focus: Interpret ferritin with inflammatory context.

07. Investigating blood loss

Ask about heavy menstrual bleeding, GI symptoms, NSAIDs, dietary changes and family history. Adults with unexplained iron-deficiency anaemia may need GI investigation according to age, sex, symptoms and local referral guidance.

PLAB focus: Do not attribute iron deficiency to diet without assessing red flags.

08. Iron deficiency treatment

Treat the underlying cause and offer oral iron where suitable; a once-daily ferrous salt is commonly used, with alternate-day dosing considered for intolerance. Discuss GI side effects and follow-up haemoglobin. IV iron is used for selected intolerance, malabsorption or rapid repletion needs.

PLAB focus: Review response and continue replacement to replenish stores.

09. Vitamin B12 deficiency

B12 is needed for DNA synthesis and neurological function. Causes include autoimmune gastritis/pernicious anaemia, ileal disease or resection, vegan diets and some medicines.

PLAB focus: Neurological damage may occur without anaemia.

10. B12 symptoms and tests

Macrocytosis, glossitis, paraesthesia, gait imbalance, impaired vibration sense and cognitive symptoms may occur. Use local B12 testing pathways; assess anti-intrinsic factor antibodies when autoimmune gastritis suspected.

PLAB focus: A negative intrinsic factor antibody test does not exclude autoimmune gastritis.

11. Treating B12 deficiency

Replace B12 promptly, especially if neurological symptoms. Intramuscular hydroxocobalamin is commonly used when malabsorption or neurological involvement is suspected; oral therapy may suit selected patients. Follow current NICE NG239 and local regimens.

PLAB focus: Do not delay treatment for severe neurological features.

12. Folate deficiency

Folate deficiency causes impaired DNA synthesis and megaloblastic anaemia; risks include poor intake, alcohol, pregnancy, malabsorption and certain medicines. Neurological symptoms are not typical of isolated folate deficiency.

PLAB focus: Check B12 status before folic acid treatment.

13. Treating folate deficiency

Oral folic acid is generally used once B12 deficiency has been excluded or treated. Address dietary and underlying causes; consider medication interactions and pregnancy-specific needs.

PLAB focus: Folate can improve blood counts while untreated B12 neurological injury progresses.

14. Blood film and reticulocytes

Microcytic hypochromic cells suggest iron deficiency; macro-ovalocytes and hypersegmented neutrophils suggest megaloblastic anaemia. High reticulocytes suggest blood loss or haemolysis; low reticulocytes indicate underproduction.

PLAB focus: Mixed deficiencies may yield a near-normal MCV.

15. Transfusion and emergencies

Transfusion is guided by symptoms, active bleeding, haemodynamics and clinical context, not haemoglobin alone. Consider urgent care for severe symptomatic anaemia; follow local transfusion policy and consent requirements.

PLAB focus: Avoid automatic transfusion for stable iron deficiency.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and follow-up

Explain the result in plain language, explore fatigue and impact, ask about bleeding and diet, agree investigations and replacement, discuss side effects and safety-net for worsening breathlessness, chest pain, fainting or bleeding.

PLAB focus: Use shared decision-making and arrange repeat blood tests.

Deep Dive

Haem synthesis

Iron is incorporated into haem, enabling oxygen transport; low stores limit erythropoiesis.

MCV limitations

Mixed deficiencies, early disease and recent transfusion can obscure classical microcytosis or macrocytosis.

Ferritin and inflammation

Ferritin increases during inflammation, potentially concealing iron depletion; interpret with CRP and iron studies.

B12 neurological injury

B12 deficiency can cause peripheral neuropathy and subacute combined degeneration even without overt anaemia.

Autoimmune gastritis

Loss of gastric parietal cell function and intrinsic factor impairs B12 absorption; long-term replacement may be needed.

Safe replacement

Correct the underlying deficiency, reassess clinical response and avoid folate-only therapy in unrecognised B12 deficiency.
