Diabetes Mellitus — Type 1 & Type 2

Emergency: Suspected DKA, HHS, severe hypoglycaemia or a critically unwell person with diabetes requires immediate assessment and escalation.

What is diabetes?

Diabetes mellitus is chronic hyperglycaemia due to impaired insulin secretion, insulin action or both. Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune beta-cell destruction with absolute insulin deficiency; type 2 is insulin resistance with progressive beta-cell dysfunction.

PLAB application: Distinguish type 1 from type 2 without relying on age or body size alone.

Recognising presentation

Polyuria, polydipsia, fatigue, blurred vision, recurrent infections and weight loss are common. Type 1 may present rapidly with ketosis or DKA. Type 2 can be asymptomatic.

PLAB application: Vomiting, abdominal pain, deep breathing or confusion demand urgent ketone assessment.

Diagnostic thresholds

In a non-pregnant adult, HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%) supports diagnosis; fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L or random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L with classic symptoms are alternative criteria. Confirm asymptomatic results with repeat testing.

PLAB application: Do not delay treatment for suspected type 1 diabetes or metabolic emergency.

When HbA1c is unreliable

HbA1c may mislead with haemoglobinopathies, altered red-cell turnover, recent transfusion, pregnancy and some acute presentations. Use plasma glucose-based tests as appropriate.

PLAB application: A normal HbA1c does not exclude rapidly evolving type 1 diabetes.

Type 1 diabetes assessment

Check capillary glucose and blood ketones when unwell; assess dehydration, weight loss, vomiting and acidosis. Urgent specialist assessment is required for suspected new type 1 diabetes. Autoantibodies and C-peptide may aid classification in selected cases.

PLAB application: Do not manage suspected new type 1 diabetes with tablets alone.

Type 2 diabetes assessment

Take cardiovascular, renal, family and medication history. Record BP, weight, BMI, smoking, foot symptoms and eye symptoms. Check renal function, urine albumin:creatinine ratio and lipids.

PLAB application: Assess cardiovascular and kidney disease to guide treatment choices.

Lifestyle and education

Agree achievable nutrition, physical activity, weight management, sleep and smoking-cessation goals. Discuss culturally appropriate dietary changes without blame. Offer structured diabetes education and shared decisions.

PLAB application: Use specific, sustainable changes and assess health literacy.

Type 1 treatment

Insulin is essential, usually a basal–bolus regimen or pump where appropriate. Teach glucose monitoring, carbohydrate counting, injection technique, hypo treatment, ketone testing and sick-day rules.

PLAB application: Never omit basal insulin in type 1 diabetes even when not eating.

Type 2 glucose-lowering therapy

Individualise treatment under current NICE NG28, taking account of HbA1c, symptoms, renal function, cardiovascular risk, weight and preferences. Metformin is frequently used; SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists and other agents have specific indications and cautions.

PLAB application: Do not assume every patient follows one fixed drug sequence; check updated NICE/BNF.

HbA1c targets and monitoring

Agree an individual HbA1c target considering hypoglycaemia risk, frailty, comorbidity and treatment. Review response and adherence, and intensify when appropriate. Continuous glucose monitoring is standard or recommended for many people with type 1 diabetes under NICE guidance.

PLAB application: Avoid over-aggressive targets in people vulnerable to hypoglycaemia.

Hypoglycaemia

Symptoms include sweating, tremor, hunger, palpitations, confusion and seizures. If conscious and able to swallow, give fast-acting carbohydrate and recheck glucose; if unable to swallow, follow emergency protocols for IV glucose or glucagon.

PLAB application: Check glucose immediately in altered consciousness and do not give oral intake to an unconscious patient.

Diabetic ketoacidosis

DKA involves ketonaemia, metabolic acidosis and usually hyperglycaemia; euglycaemic DKA can occur, especially with SGLT2 inhibitors. Treat in hospital with IV fluids, fixed-rate IV insulin, potassium monitoring and treatment of the trigger using local protocol.

PLAB application: DKA is an emergency; ketones and acid-base status matter, not glucose alone.

Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

HHS typically affects type 2 diabetes and features severe hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality and profound dehydration with minimal significant ketosis. Careful fluid replacement and close electrolyte/osmolality monitoring are crucial.

PLAB application: Distinguish HHS from DKA; management differs.

Microvascular complications

Retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy develop with chronic hyperglycaemia. Arrange retinal screening, renal tests including ACR, and foot assessment. Address glucose and BP control and cardiovascular risks.

PLAB application: A painless foot ulcer may indicate neuropathy; examine urgently for infection/ischaemia.

Macrovascular risk and prevention

Diabetes raises risk of myocardial infarction, stroke and peripheral arterial disease. Manage blood pressure, lipids, smoking and kidney disease; consider statin treatment according to individual cardiovascular risk and UK guidance.

PLAB application: Treat diabetes as a cardiovascular and renal risk condition, not only a glucose problem.

Sick days, driving and safety-netting

During illness, monitor glucose more frequently and ketones where indicated, maintain hydration and seek individualised advice on medication adjustment. People using insulin must follow DVLA rules for driving. Urgently seek help for vomiting, high ketones, confusion, dehydration or persistent hypoglycaemia.

PLAB application: Type 1 patients must continue basal insulin during illness and seek urgent help for ketones or DKA symptoms.

PLAB 2 OSCE: New diabetes diagnosis and counselling

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and ideas, concerns and expectations.
  2. Ask about osmotic symptoms, weight change, infections, medicines, family history and DKA warning signs.
  3. Explain type 1 versus type 2 in clear language and check understanding.
  4. Explain investigations, treatment, glucose monitoring, complications screening and shared lifestyle goals.
  5. Discuss hypoglycaemia, sick-day rules, ketone testing where appropriate, driving and urgent red flags.
  6. Summarise, invite questions and arrange follow-up.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why type 1 causes DKA

Without insulin, lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis increase, causing ketone accumulation and high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis.

Why HbA1c can mislead

HbA1c reflects glycation over red-cell lifespan; shortened or altered lifespan may distort results and acute onset may precede HbA1c elevation.

Insulin and potassium

Total-body potassium may be depleted in DKA even when initial serum potassium is normal or high; insulin shifts potassium intracellularly.

HHS versus DKA

HHS usually has more extreme hyperosmolality and dehydration with limited ketosis; DKA is defined by ketones and acidosis. Mixed states occur.

Cardiorenal risk in type 2

Glucose control alone is insufficient: kidney function, BP, lipids, smoking and selected glucose-lowering agents influence long-term outcomes.

Patient-centred PLAB 2 counselling

Use teach-back, address concerns, discuss hypo recognition, driving where relevant, medication safety, sick-day rules and follow-up.
