Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs) & Pyelonephritis

PLAB priority: Differentiate lower UTI from pyelonephritis, identify sepsis and infected obstruction, and prescribe antibiotics according to UK guidance.

01. Clinical approach

UTI is infection involving the urinary tract. Distinguish lower UTI (cystitis), upper UTI (pyelonephritis), complicated infection and asymptomatic bacteriuria. Assess severity, pregnancy, age, urinary tract abnormalities and previous resistant organisms.

PLAB application: Ask about fever, flank pain, vomiting, pregnancy and sepsis symptoms before choosing treatment.

02. Symptoms of lower UTI

Dysuria, new frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort and sometimes visible haematuria are typical. Vaginal discharge or irritation raises the possibility of vaginitis or an STI. Cloudy or smelly urine alone is not diagnostic.

PLAB application: Use the symptom pattern and local diagnostic pathway; do not diagnose UTI from urine colour alone.

03. Acute pyelonephritis

Upper UTI commonly presents with fever, rigors, flank or loin pain, costovertebral-angle tenderness, nausea and vomiting; urinary symptoms may be absent. Assess dehydration and systemic deterioration.

PLAB application: Fever with loin pain warrants assessment for pyelonephritis and possible admission.

04. Red flags and sepsis

Assess ABCDE, NEWS2 where appropriate, vital signs, mental state, capillary refill, urine output and lactate when indicated. Hypotension, confusion, tachypnoea, oliguria and poor perfusion require urgent escalation.

PLAB application: Do not delay sepsis care while awaiting a urine result.

05. Who has complicated infection?

Risk factors include obstruction, stones, urinary instrumentation, catheter, structural abnormality, immunosuppression, pregnancy and severe systemic illness. Male UTI requires consideration of prostatitis and other underlying causes.

PLAB application: A septic patient with an obstructed infected kidney needs urgent drainage as well as antibiotics.

06. Urine sampling and testing

Use a clean-catch midstream specimen where feasible; sample a catheter via its sampling port, not the drainage bag. Dipstick interpretation depends on age, symptoms and context. Send urine culture for pyelonephritis, pregnancy, men, recurrent or complicated infection and treatment failure according to guidance.

PLAB application: Collect culture before antibiotics when feasible, but do not delay urgent treatment.

07. Interpreting dipsticks and culture

Nitrites suggest nitrate-reducing bacteria; leukocyte esterase suggests inflammation, not necessarily infection. Negative nitrites do not rule out UTI. Bacteriuria without symptoms often represents colonisation, especially with catheters.

PLAB application: Treat the patient and syndrome, not a positive dipstick in isolation.

08. Asymptomatic bacteriuria

Bacteria in urine without urinary symptoms generally do not need antibiotics, including in older adults and catheterised patients. Important exceptions include pregnancy and selected invasive urological procedures.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary antibiotic treatment in asymptomatic non-pregnant adults.

09. Lower UTI treatment

Offer self-care advice and analgesia. Antibiotic choice depends on pregnancy, sex, kidney function, local resistance and NICE guidance. Nitrofurantoin is commonly used for uncomplicated lower UTI when suitable; it is not appropriate for pyelonephritis because tissue penetration is inadequate.

PLAB application: Check eGFR, contraindications and local antibiotic guidance before prescribing.

10. Pyelonephritis treatment

Obtain urine culture and start an antibiotic with renal tissue activity. Assess ability to tolerate oral medication; vomiting, dehydration, pregnancy, sepsis or high-risk comorbidity may require hospital assessment and IV antibiotics. Review culture results and response within 48 hours.

PLAB application: Do not use nitrofurantoin or fosfomycin for upper UTI.

11. UTI in pregnancy

Pregnancy increases concern for ascending infection and complications. Obtain urine culture, treat symptomatic bacteriuria and screen/treat asymptomatic bacteriuria according to antenatal care. Suspected pyelonephritis in pregnancy requires urgent hospital assessment.

PLAB application: Check pregnancy status before selecting antibiotics and interpreting risk.

12. Catheter-associated UTI

Long-term catheters frequently cause bacteriuria without infection. Treat only when compatible symptoms or systemic signs are present, not for cloudy urine alone. Consider catheter change or removal if indicated, and obtain a suitable specimen before antibiotics.

PLAB application: Catheter bacteriuria is not automatically catheter-associated UTI.

13. Recurrent UTI

Recurrent UTI is commonly defined as at least 2 episodes in 6 months or 3 in 12 months. Confirm episodes, review culture results, hydration, sexual and menopausal factors, and consider vaginal oestrogen in appropriate postmenopausal patients. Prophylaxis is individualised.

PLAB application: Check for red flags, haematuria and structural causes before long-term prevention plans.

14. Obstruction, stones and renal abscess

Infected obstruction can progress rapidly to septic shock. Suspect it with severe flank pain, known stones, anuria, AKI or poor response. Arrange urgent imaging and urology review; decompression is source control.

PLAB application: An obstructed infected collecting system is a urological emergency.

15. Differential diagnoses

Consider urethritis/STIs, vaginitis, prostatitis, renal colic, appendicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, interstitial cystitis and malignancy when symptoms are atypical. Persistent haematuria needs appropriate assessment after infection resolves.

PLAB application: Do not overlook ectopic pregnancy in patients with pelvic pain and pregnancy possibility.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain diagnosis and testing, medicine adherence, hydration as appropriate, pain relief, side effects and antimicrobial stewardship. Seek urgent help for fever/rigors, flank pain, vomiting, pregnancy with symptoms, worsening illness or confusion. Reassess if symptoms fail to improve within 48 hours of treatment.

PLAB application: Use plain language, shared decisions and clear return precautions.

Deep Dive

Why nitrofurantoin is limited

Nitrofurantoin concentrates in urine, making it useful for susceptible lower UTI, but its renal parenchymal concentrations are inadequate for pyelonephritis.

Pyuria versus infection

White blood cells in urine indicate inflammation; catheterisation, contamination and non-bacterial disorders can produce pyuria without a treatable UTI.

Obstruction and septic shock

A blocked infected collecting system can maintain bacterial load despite antibiotics. Drainage is essential source control.

Antibiotic stewardship

Use local resistance data, renal function, pregnancy status and culture susceptibilities; avoid antibiotics for asymptomatic bacteriuria except defined situations.

Pregnancy physiology

Urinary stasis and physiological changes increase susceptibility to ascending infection; maternal and fetal risks change the threshold for assessment.

Why older-adult UTI diagnosis is challenging

Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common. Delirium has many causes; do not attribute non-specific deterioration to UTI solely on a positive dipstick.
