Postpartum Haemorrhage, Retained Placenta & Obstetric Emergencies

Emergency warning: Heavy bleeding, maternal collapse or suspected shock after birth requires immediate emergency obstetric assessment.

Definitions and timing

Primary postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) is blood loss of at least 500 mL within 24 hours after birth; major PPH is at least 1000 mL. Secondary PPH is abnormal or excessive bleeding from 24 hours to 12 weeks after birth.

PLAB application: Treat the patient, not just the estimated volume: shock can occur before an accurate measurement.

The four Ts

Tone (uterine atony), Trauma (genital tract injury), Tissue (retained placenta or products), and Thrombin (coagulopathy) organise the causes of PPH.

PLAB application: Atony is common, but multiple causes can coexist.

Risk factors

Previous PPH, multiple pregnancy, polyhydramnios, prolonged or augmented labour, uterine fibroids, operative delivery, placenta praevia/accreta and maternal anaemia increase risk. PPH can also occur without risk factors.

PLAB application: Never dismiss haemorrhage because pregnancy was low risk.

Recognition and initial assessment

Call the obstetric emergency team. Perform ABCDE; measure pulse, BP, oxygen saturation, consciousness and ongoing blood loss; assess uterine tone, placenta completeness and perineum. Quantify cumulative blood loss when possible.

PLAB application: Tachycardia, pallor, altered mental state and hypotension may indicate significant shock.

Immediate resuscitation

Activate major obstetric haemorrhage protocol when appropriate; obtain large-bore IV access, send FBC, coagulation including fibrinogen, group and crossmatch; start warmed fluids judiciously and give blood products guided by bleeding and local protocol. Prevent hypothermia.

PLAB application: Resuscitation and definitive bleeding control must happen in parallel.

Uterine atony

A soft, boggy uterus suggests atony. Use uterine massage and appropriate uterotonics under the local obstetric protocol, assess for retained tissue and empty the bladder. Escalate rapidly if bleeding persists.

PLAB application: Do not delay escalation while repeatedly trying ineffective measures.

Uterotonic medicines

Oxytocin is commonly first-line. Other options include ergometrine, carboprost and misoprostol, depending on context and contraindications. Ergometrine is generally avoided in hypertension; carboprost requires caution or avoidance in asthma.

PLAB application: Check contraindications, local dosing and senior advice.

Tranexamic acid

For clinically diagnosed PPH, give IV tranexamic acid as soon as possible and within 3 hours of birth in line with protocol; it complements, not replaces, uterotonics and source control.

PLAB application: Do not wait for severe shock before considering TXA in PPH.

Trauma

A firm contracted uterus with ongoing bright-red bleeding suggests genital tract trauma. Inspect for cervical, vaginal and perineal tears; consider uterine rupture or inversion if severe bleeding and collapse.

PLAB application: Definitive management may require theatre and surgical repair.

Retained placenta

A placenta not delivered within the expected third-stage timeframe requires assessment. NICE uses 30 minutes after active management or 60 minutes after physiological management to define a prolonged third stage. Evaluate bleeding, maternal status and need for manual removal.

PLAB application: Do not use forceful cord traction; obtain consent and provide adequate anaesthesia for manual removal.

Placenta accreta spectrum

Abnormally adherent placentation can cause catastrophic bleeding when separation is attempted, especially with placenta praevia and previous caesarean delivery.

PLAB application: Suspected accreta requires senior multidisciplinary planning; avoid forcible removal.

Coagulopathy and blood products

Severe haemorrhage can produce or worsen coagulopathy. Monitor platelets, PT/APTT and fibrinogen; replace components according to major haemorrhage protocol and laboratory guidance.

PLAB application: A low fibrinogen in obstetric bleeding is particularly concerning.

Mechanical and surgical escalation

Persistent PPH may require intrauterine balloon tamponade, examination under anaesthesia, compression sutures, vessel ligation, interventional radiology or hysterectomy.

PLAB application: Life-saving hysterectomy may be necessary if other measures fail.

Uterine inversion and rupture

Uterine inversion may present with shock, haemorrhage and a uterine mass; urgent skilled replacement is needed. Rupture can cause pain, fetal compromise, haemorrhage and maternal collapse, especially with uterine scars.

PLAB application: Both are time-critical emergencies requiring immediate senior obstetric support.

Secondary postpartum haemorrhage

Consider retained products, endometritis and other causes. Assess vitals, fever, uterine tenderness, offensive lochia and bleeding; investigate with blood tests and ultrasound when indicated.

PLAB application: Heavy bleeding, sepsis or instability requires emergency admission.

Recovery, debrief and prevention

After haemorrhage, monitor haemoglobin and symptoms, treat iron deficiency or anaemia, document events, discuss future pregnancy risks and provide a compassionate debrief and psychological support.

PLAB application: Explain what happened without blame, and provide clear return precautions.

PLAB 2 — Emergency PPH handover and counselling

Assess ABCDE and communicate urgently using SBAR. Explain the suspected bleeding cause, resuscitation, medicines, blood transfusion and possible procedures in plain language. Once stable, invite questions, debrief and plan follow-up.

Deep Dive

Four Ts in clinical practice

Assess the uterus for tone; inspect for trauma; check placental completeness; assess clotting. More than one cause can be present.

TXA timing

Early IV tranexamic acid within 3 hours of birth is recommended for clinically diagnosed PPH. Follow local dosing protocols and avoid delay.

Uterotonic cautions

Oxytocin is frequently first-line; ergometrine is problematic with hypertension and carboprost with asthma. Drug choice depends on local protocols and clinical factors.

Massive haemorrhage and fibrinogen

Pregnancy normally raises fibrinogen. Falling or low fibrinogen during major haemorrhage is an important warning sign; use lab-guided blood component therapy.

Placenta accreta and retained placenta

Previous caesarean and placenta praevia increase accreta risk. Avoid forcible placental separation when suspected; obtain specialist help.

PLAB 2 communication

Use calm explanations, confirm understanding, obtain consent where feasible, communicate uncertainty, and arrange post-event debrief and follow-up.
