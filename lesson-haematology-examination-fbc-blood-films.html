Haematological Examination, Full Blood Count Interpretation & Blood Films

PLAB priority: Interpret FBC findings alongside symptoms and clinical stability; do not miss neutropenic sepsis, TTP or acute leukaemia.

01. Clinical approach

Start with patient stability, presenting symptoms, prior results and trends. The FBC is a clue, not a diagnosis.

PLAB application: Escalate immediately for unstable patients, active major bleeding or suspected sepsis.

02. Focused history

Ask about fatigue, dyspnoea, bleeding, bruising, infections, fever, weight loss, night sweats, bone pain, diet, drugs, alcohol, family and travel history.

PLAB application: Ask about anticoagulants, chemotherapy and transfusions.

03. Consent and preparation

Wash hands, introduce yourself, confirm identity, explain the examination, obtain consent and offer a chaperone when appropriate.

PLAB application: Position patient comfortably with adequate exposure and dignity.

04. General inspection

Assess pallor, jaundice, cachexia, breathlessness, bruising, petechiae, lymphadenopathy and medical devices.

PLAB application: Record observations and examine the patient, not just the laboratory values.

05. Hands, nails and arms

Look for koilonychia, palmar pallor, bruises, petechiae and signs of venepuncture. Check capillary refill where relevant.

PLAB application: Koilonychia can accompany chronic iron deficiency but is not diagnostic.

06. Face and mouth

Inspect conjunctivae for pallor, sclerae for icterus, mouth for ulcers, glossitis, gum bleeding and infections.

PLAB application: Glossitis can occur in nutritional deficiencies.

07. Lymph nodes

Palpate cervical, supraclavicular, axillary and inguinal nodes with consent; document size, tenderness, consistency and mobility.

PLAB application: Persistent unexplained lymphadenopathy requires appropriate assessment.

08. Abdominal examination

Inspect and palpate gently for hepatomegaly and splenomegaly; check for tenderness and abdominal masses.

PLAB application: Splenomegaly may accompany haemolysis, infection or haematological malignancy.

09. Completing the OSCE

Thank patient, offer to cover, wash hands, summarise positives and negatives. Suggest FBC, film, reticulocytes, haematinics and targeted tests.

PLAB application: Mention specialist review or urgent escalation if red flags exist.

10. Reading the FBC systematically

Review Hb, MCV, MCH, WBC differential, neutrophils and platelets, then compare prior results and reference ranges.

PLAB application: Check whether one, two or three cell lines are affected.

11. Microcytic anaemia

Low MCV suggests iron deficiency or thalassaemia; chronic inflammation can also be microcytic. Ferritin and iron studies guide evaluation.

PLAB application: Do not assume every microcytic anaemia is iron deficiency.

12. Normocytic anaemia

Consider acute blood loss, chronic disease, CKD, haemolysis and early nutritional deficiency. Reticulocyte count helps separate production from loss/destruction.

PLAB application: Assess kidney function and haemolysis markers where indicated.

13. Macrocytic anaemia

Causes include B12/folate deficiency, alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, drugs and marrow disease.

PLAB application: Neurological findings raise concern for B12 deficiency.

14. White cell abnormalities

Neutrophilia may reflect infection or inflammation; neutropenia may reflect drugs, infection or marrow disease. Lymphocytosis may be reactive or clonal.

PLAB application: Fever in an immunosuppressed neutropenic patient is an emergency.

15. Platelet abnormalities

Thrombocytopenia can cause petechiae or bleeding; thrombocytosis may be reactive or clonal. Exclude artefactual platelet clumping when indicated.

PLAB application: Assess bleeding severity and blood film before assuming ITP.

16. Blood film patterns

Target cells: thalassaemia/liver disease; spherocytes: haemolysis; schistocytes: microangiopathy; sickle cells: HbS disease; blasts: possible leukaemia.

PLAB application: Schistocytes with thrombocytopenia or blasts with illness need urgent assessment.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Blood-cell production

Bone marrow produces erythrocytes, granulocytes and platelets from haematopoietic stem cells. Trends across all three lines help localise problems.

MCV limitations

Mixed iron and B12 deficiency may produce a near-normal MCV. Use the film and RDW alongside the clinical picture.

Reticulocyte reasoning

An inappropriately low reticulocyte response suggests inadequate production; elevated reticulocytes suggest compensation after haemolysis or bleeding.

Morphology and mechanism

Schistocytes reflect mechanical fragmentation; spherocytes lose membrane surface area; target cells have excess membrane relative to volume.

White-cell differentials

Absolute counts matter more than percentages alone. Reactive and malignant processes can overlap and require clinical context.

OSCE explanation

Explain that a blood count measures oxygen-carrying cells, infection-fighting cells and platelets, and that a film helps examine cell appearance.
