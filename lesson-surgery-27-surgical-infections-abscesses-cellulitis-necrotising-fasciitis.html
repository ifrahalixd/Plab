Surgical Infections, Abscesses, Cellulitis & Necrotising Fasciitis

Surgery · Lesson 27 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Surgical infections: recognising severity and achieving source control

Time-critical: suspected necrotising soft-tissue infection, rapidly progressing infection, septic shock or infection with neurovascular compromise needs immediate senior surgical assessment, resuscitation and empiric IV antibiotics. Do not delay theatre for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

1 · Structured first assessment

Assess ABCDE, NEWS2, temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, mental state and urine output. Ask onset, progression, preceding trauma/surgery, bite, injection, diabetes, immunosuppression, vascular disease, recent antibiotics, MRSA history and allergies. Examine distribution, warmth, erythema, swelling, fluctuance, tenderness, crepitus, bullae, skin necrosis, lymphangitis and distal neurovascular status. Mark erythema margins and reassess. Infection can be severe without fever, particularly in older or immunocompromised patients.

2 · Cellulitis versus erysipelas

Cellulitis: infection of deeper dermis/subcutaneous tissue, poorly demarcated warm tender erythema, commonly lower limb; streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus are frequent pathogens. Erysipelas: more superficial infection with raised, sharply demarcated erythema, commonly streptococcal. Assess entry sites, oedema, tinea pedis and recurrence risk. Mimics include DVT, venous eczema, contact dermatitis and gout. Mild uncomplicated cellulitis is generally treated with oral antibiotics according to NICE NG141/local policy; severe systemic illness, rapidly spreading infection or inability to take oral medication warrants urgent assessment and potentially IV therapy.

3 · Cutaneous abscess

Localized pus usually causes a tender, fluctuant swelling; fever may be absent. Incision and drainage is the primary definitive treatment for a drainable abscess, with analgesia/anaesthesia, appropriate sampling in selected cases and wound aftercare. Antibiotics are added based on systemic illness, surrounding cellulitis, high-risk location, immunosuppression or local policy; antibiotics alone rarely replace drainage of a substantial collection. Consider ultrasound if a deep collection is uncertain. Facial, hand, perianal and deep-space abscesses merit specialist assessment.

4 · Surgical site infection (SSI)

Superficial incisional SSI involves skin/subcutaneous tissue; deep incisional SSI involves fascia/muscle; organ-space SSI involves an operated anatomical space. Clues include new wound pain, erythema, warmth, purulent discharge, dehiscence, fever or systemic deterioration. Examine for a collection, obtain samples when appropriate, assess sepsis and seek surgical review. Treatment may require opening/drainage, debridement, removal of infected material when indicated and targeted antimicrobials. Consider anastomotic leak or intra-abdominal abscess in postoperative patients with unexplained sepsis.

5 · Necrotising fasciitis and necrotising soft-tissue infection

Rapidly progressive deep infection with fascial necrosis. Early red flags: severe pain disproportionate to skin findings, rapid progression, marked systemic toxicity, oedema beyond erythema and tenderness. Later signs include dusky skin, haemorrhagic bullae, anaesthesia, crepitus and shock; their absence does not exclude disease. Risk factors include diabetes, immunosuppression, trauma and recent surgery, but healthy people can be affected. Group A streptococcus, polymicrobial infection and other pathogens are possible. Immediate management: ABCDE, senior surgical/critical care involvement, blood cultures if this does not delay antibiotics, urgent broad-spectrum IV antibiotics according to local policy and urgent surgical exploration/debridement. Repeat debridement may be needed. CT can help when diagnosis uncertain and patient stable, but never delay surgery in a strongly suspected case. LRINEC is not sufficiently sensitive to rule out necrotising infection.

6 · Fournier gangrene

Necrotising infection of perineum/genital region. Symptoms include severe perineal pain, swelling, discoloration, crepitus and systemic toxicity. Assess diabetes, urinary/perianal sources and immunosuppression. This is a surgical emergency: resuscitate, give broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, urgent general surgical/urological review and extensive debridement; consider ICU and reconstructive planning.

7 · Sepsis and septic shock

Suspected infection plus acute organ dysfunction requires urgent sepsis assessment. Check lactate/VBG, FBC, U&E, renal function, CRP, cultures where appropriate and source-specific imaging. Follow current NICE sepsis guidance and local escalation pathways. Give oxygen only when indicated, IV fluids guided by perfusion/comorbidity, prompt antibiotics for high-risk sepsis, monitor urine output and reassess frequently. Septic shock may require critical-care vasopressors. Source control (drainage, debridement or removing infected devices) is essential and should not be postponed by transient physiological improvement.

8 · Other important surgical infections

Infected diabetic foot: assess perfusion, neuropathy, depth, bone involvement and systemic illness; urgent multidisciplinary diabetic-foot/vascular review for severe infection, gangrene or ischaemia. Septic arthritis: hot swollen painful joint with reduced range of movement requires urgent orthopaedic assessment and aspiration where feasible before antibiotics if safe; do not delay treatment in sepsis. Infected prosthesis: urgent orthopaedic review, microbiology and source control. Animal/human bites: irrigate, assess tendon/joint injury, tetanus and rabies risk where relevant; follow NICE NG184 for prophylaxis/treatment.

9 · Antibiotic stewardship and microbiology

Use severity, anatomical site, likely organisms, allergies, renal function, prior cultures and local resistance guidance to select therapy. Obtain cultures when indicated without delaying life-saving treatment. Review IV antibiotics at 48 hours where clinically appropriate, narrow to results and use recommended course lengths. Avoid routine swabs of uninfected wounds. Consider C. difficile risk and antimicrobial adverse effects.

10 · Safety net and follow-up

For uncomplicated cellulitis, advise that redness can initially persist despite treatment; seek urgent review for rapid spread, increasing pain, new fever, confusion, dizziness, blistering, skin darkening or failure to improve within 2–3 days. Elevate affected limb when practical, manage skin breaks and underlying oedema, and document review plans. Never discharge a patient with suspected necrotising infection.

Decision pathways and comparison charts

Soft-tissue infection triage

ABCDE + vitals → assess rapid progression, pain out of proportion, bullae/necrosis, systemic toxicity → any red flag: immediate surgical review, sepsis treatment and source control. Otherwise distinguish cellulitis from localized abscess and treat appropriately.

Cellulitis versus abscess

Cellulitis: diffuse warm erythema without focal pus → antibiotics per NICE/local guidance. Abscess: focal fluctuant collection → incision and drainage, plus antibiotics when indicated.

Necrotising fasciitis — do not miss

Disproportionate pain + rapidly worsening swelling ± systemic toxicity → resuscitate, urgent surgery and IV broad-spectrum antibiotics. No single blood test or normal early skin appearance safely rules it out.

Postoperative fever and wound changes

Examine incision and patient → assess superficial/deep/organ-space infection → consider collection or anastomotic leak → urgent senior review, imaging if stable, drainage/debridement as indicated.

Sepsis management

Recognise organ dysfunction → escalate and monitor → appropriate cultures, lactate and investigations → prompt antibiotics in high-risk cases → tailored fluids and urgent source control.

Antibiotic principles

Follow current local/NICE guidance; account for allergy, renal function, severity, MRSA risk and microbiology; review and narrow therapy when possible.

Four PLAB-style clinical cases

Case 1 — Spreading leg redness

A 54-year-old has a warm tender lower leg, poorly defined erythema, no fluctuant focus, normal observations and no systemic symptoms. Best approach: assess for mimics, skin break and severity; treat uncomplicated cellulitis with appropriate oral antibiotics and safety-netting under NICE/local guidance.

Case 2 — Painful fluctuant lump

A 28-year-old has a 3 cm fluctuant axillary swelling and surrounding erythema. Best approach: assess systemic illness and anatomical risks; arrange incision and drainage, with antibiotics if clinically indicated.

Case 3 — Pain out of proportion

A 62-year-old with diabetes has severe thigh pain, subtle skin redness, rapidly increasing swelling, tachycardia and hypotension. Best approach: suspect necrotising fasciitis; resuscitate, give urgent IV antibiotics, contact surgeons/ICU and proceed to urgent exploration. Do not wait for CT or LRINEC.

Case 4 — Postoperative deterioration

Five days after bowel surgery, a patient becomes febrile, tachycardic and hypotensive with increasing abdominal pain and wound discharge. Best approach: sepsis resuscitation and immediate senior review; consider deep SSI, intra-abdominal abscess or anastomotic leak and arrange urgent source control.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Rapidly progressive leg infection

Station: A 60-year-old with diabetes presents with severe leg pain and spreading redness over six hours.

  1. Introduce yourself, obtain consent, establish timing, rate of progression and severity of pain.
  2. Ask about fever/rigors, confusion, dizziness, trauma, recent surgery, wounds, diabetes, immune suppression, allergies and previous antibiotics.
  3. Assess ABCDE and full observations; identify hypotension, tachycardia and altered mental state.
  4. Inspect both legs and skin for erythema, swelling, bullae, discoloration, necrosis or crepitus; assess tenderness and distal perfusion. Avoid delaying escalation for a complete examination.
  5. State concern for necrotising infection if pain is disproportionate or deterioration rapid; request immediate senior surgical and critical-care help.
  6. Arrange IV access, appropriate blood tests including lactate, cultures when feasible, analgesia, prompt broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and guided fluid resuscitation.
  7. Explain that emergency surgery to remove infected tissue may be necessary and that imaging must not delay theatre when suspicion is high.
  8. Communicate clearly, reassess frequently, document and hand over using SBAR.

Examiner priorities: recognise life-threatening infection early, prioritise resuscitation and surgical source control, avoid false reassurance from normal early skin or blood tests.

Rapid revision — 12 essentials

  1. Cellulitis causes diffuse tender warm erythema; consider DVT and venous eczema as mimics.
  2. Erysipelas often has raised, sharply demarcated margins.
  3. A drainable abscess usually needs incision and drainage.
  4. Antibiotics supplement drainage when systemic illness or other risk factors warrant.
  5. Postoperative infection may be superficial, deep incisional or organ-space.
  6. Postoperative sepsis can signal an anastomotic leak or deep collection.
  7. Pain out of proportion and rapid progression are early necrotising fasciitis clues.
  8. Late bullae, skin necrosis or crepitus need not be present.
  9. Normal LRINEC or imaging does not reliably exclude necrotising infection.
  10. Urgent debridement and broad-spectrum IV antibiotics are central to necrotising infection treatment.
  11. Fournier gangrene is a perineal necrotising surgical emergency.
  12. Sepsis care includes early recognition, appropriate antibiotics, reassessment and source control.
