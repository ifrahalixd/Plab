Vasculitis & Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)

PLAB priority: Recognise suspected giant cell arteritis immediately, protect vision and identify organ-threatening vasculitis.

01. Overview

Vasculitis is inflammation of blood-vessel walls, causing narrowing, occlusion, aneurysm or bleeding. Classify by vessel size and organ involvement.

PLAB application: Consider vasculitis in multisystem illness with unexplained inflammation and ischaemic symptoms.

02. Vessel-size classification

Large-vessel: giant cell arteritis and Takayasu arteritis. Medium-vessel: polyarteritis nodosa and Kawasaki disease. Small-vessel: ANCA-associated vasculitis and immune-complex vasculitis.

PLAB application: Use the affected organs and vessel size to narrow the differential.

03. Giant cell arteritis

GCA usually affects people aged 50 or over; features include new temporal headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, constitutional symptoms and visual disturbance.

PLAB application: New headache plus jaw claudication or visual symptoms in an older adult is an emergency.

04. GCA visual red flags

Transient monocular blindness, diplopia or permanent vision loss may reflect ocular ischaemia. Irreversible blindness can develop rapidly.

PLAB application: Arrange same-day emergency specialist assessment; do not delay treatment for tests.

05. GCA immediate treatment

When GCA is strongly suspected, initiate high-dose glucocorticoids promptly under urgent clinical guidance; visual symptoms may require IV therapy and immediate ophthalmology involvement.

PLAB application: Never wait for temporal artery ultrasound or biopsy before starting indicated steroids.

06. Investigating GCA

Obtain ESR, CRP, FBC and baseline blood tests, but normal inflammatory markers do not completely exclude GCA. Temporal artery ultrasound and/or biopsy support diagnosis; imaging should not delay treatment.

PLAB application: Confirm through a fast-track specialist pathway while protecting vision.

07. Polymyalgia rheumatica association

GCA can coexist with polymyalgia rheumatica: bilateral shoulder or hip girdle pain and marked morning stiffness. PMR without cranial symptoms does not itself establish GCA.

PLAB application: Ask patients with PMR about headache, jaw claudication and visual change.

08. Takayasu arteritis

Typically presents in younger patients with constitutional symptoms, limb claudication, pulse asymmetry, blood-pressure differences and arterial bruits.

PLAB application: Check bilateral arm pulses and blood pressure when large-vessel disease is suspected.

09. ANCA-associated vasculitis

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis can cause sinus, ear, lung and renal disease; microscopic polyangiitis often affects kidneys and lungs; eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis is linked to asthma and eosinophilia.

PLAB application: Pulmonary haemorrhage with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis is life-threatening.

10. Renal and pulmonary warning signs

Haematuria, proteinuria, rising creatinine, haemoptysis, hypoxia or new infiltrates suggest organ-threatening vasculitis.

PLAB application: Check urinalysis, renal function and respiratory status promptly.

11. Immune-complex and medium-vessel disease

IgA vasculitis causes palpable purpura, arthralgia, abdominal pain and sometimes nephritis. Polyarteritis nodosa may cause neuropathy, abdominal ischaemia and hypertension but classically spares glomeruli.

PLAB application: Palpable purpura should trigger assessment for systemic involvement.

12. Diagnostic workup

History and examination guide CBC, ESR/CRP, renal function, urine dip and microscopy, ANCA, complement, hepatitis serology, imaging and tissue biopsy when appropriate.

PLAB application: ANCA supports selected diagnoses but is not diagnostic alone; exclude infection mimics.

13. Treatment principles

Organ-threatening systemic vasculitis requires urgent specialist-directed immunosuppression. Regimens vary by diagnosis and severity, and may include glucocorticoids with rituximab or cyclophosphamide.

PLAB application: Avoid initiating complex regimens without specialist assessment and infection screening.

14. Monitoring and complications

Monitor relapse, renal and pulmonary damage, cardiovascular risk, steroid adverse effects, infection and osteoporosis. Steroid-sparing agents may be used for GCA under specialist guidance.

PLAB application: Provide bone protection and vaccination planning where appropriate.

15. Differential diagnosis

Consider infection including endocarditis, malignancy, thromboembolism, atherosclerotic disease, connective-tissue disease and drug reactions.

PLAB application: Fever and raised CRP do not automatically mean vasculitis.

16. PLAB 2 approach

Ask about new headache, visual change, jaw pain, rash, sinus symptoms, haemoptysis, neuropathy and urinary change; assess vital signs and organ function. Explain urgent referral and safety-netting.

PLAB application: For sudden visual symptoms or severe breathlessness, arrange emergency care rather than routine follow-up.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why GCA causes blindness

Granulomatous inflammation narrows branches of the carotid circulation, including vessels supplying the optic nerve. Ischaemia may cause irreversible visual loss.

Temporal artery ultrasound

A non-compressible hypoechoic halo can support GCA diagnosis; operator skill and early timing matter. Imaging does not replace urgent treatment.

ANCA patterns

PR3-ANCA often occurs with GPA and MPO-ANCA with MPA, but patterns overlap and negative ANCA does not exclude disease.

Pulmonary–renal syndrome

Capillaritis can damage alveoli and glomeruli simultaneously, leading to alveolar haemorrhage and rapidly progressive nephritis.

Large versus small vessels

Large-vessel disease causes claudication, pulse deficits and cranial ischaemia; small-vessel disease more often causes purpura, glomerulonephritis or alveolar haemorrhage.

Balancing immunosuppression

Rapid control of organ-threatening inflammation must be balanced against infection, bone loss, metabolic and cardiovascular complications.
