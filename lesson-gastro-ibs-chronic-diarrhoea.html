IBS & Chronic Diarrhoea

Safety: Rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, anaemia or persistent nocturnal diarrhoea requires further assessment.

IBS overview

Irritable bowel syndrome is a disorder of gut–brain interaction characterised by recurrent abdominal pain associated with altered bowel habits, without structural disease explaining symptoms.

PLAB application: Positive diagnosis is possible when the symptom pattern fits and red flags are absent.

Rome IV and NICE criteria

Rome IV describes recurrent abdominal pain averaging at least one day per week in the last three months, related to defecation and/or stool frequency/form change, with symptom onset at least six months before diagnosis. NICE CG61 uses a different pragmatic primary-care symptom framework.

PLAB application: Do not confuse Rome IV research criteria with the NICE approach used in UK primary care.

IBS subtypes

IBS-C is constipation predominant, IBS-D diarrhoea predominant, IBS-M mixed, and IBS-U unclassified. Bristol stool chart helps characterise bowel habit.

PLAB application: Treatment is symptom- and subtype-directed.

History

Ask about pain, stool consistency/frequency, urgency, bloating, mucus, dietary triggers, medicines, stress, travel, family history and effect on daily life.

PLAB application: Ask specifically about nocturnal diarrhoea and unintentional weight loss.

Red flags

Rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia, abdominal/rectal mass, persistent change in bowel habit and concerning family history require further assessment.

PLAB application: Follow current NICE NG12 and local colorectal referral pathways rather than assuming IBS.

Initial investigations

In suspected IBS, NICE recommends full blood count, inflammatory markers (ESR or plasma viscosity, or CRP) and coeliac serology to exclude common alternatives.

PLAB application: Faecal calprotectin can help distinguish inflammatory disease from functional symptoms when indicated by local pathways.

Coeliac disease

Coeliac disease can cause chronic diarrhoea, bloating, weight loss and iron-deficiency anaemia. Test tissue transglutaminase IgA with total IgA while the patient is eating gluten.

PLAB application: Do not advise a gluten-free diet before diagnostic testing.

Chronic diarrhoea differential

Consider infection, IBD, coeliac disease, medication effects (metformin, laxatives, antibiotics), bile acid diarrhoea, microscopic colitis, malabsorption and endocrine causes.

PLAB application: Blood, nocturnal symptoms, fever or weight loss should prompt organic-disease evaluation.

Stool tests

Faecal calprotectin supports assessment for intestinal inflammation; stool culture or parasite testing is guided by travel, exposure, duration and epidemiology. FIT may be indicated under colorectal cancer pathways.

PLAB application: A normal calprotectin does not replace clinical assessment or exclude every cause.

First-line IBS management

Provide a clear explanation, regular meals, hydration, physical activity, sleep and individualised dietary advice. Review caffeine, alcohol, fizzy drinks and triggers.

PLAB application: Avoid overly restrictive diets without dietitian support.

Low-FODMAP diet

If first-line advice fails, a dietitian-supervised low-FODMAP trial with structured reintroduction may improve symptoms in some patients.

PLAB application: Long-term blanket food exclusion is not the goal.

IBS-C management

Consider soluble fibre such as ispaghula; avoid excess insoluble bran if it worsens symptoms. Laxatives may be needed; NICE advises against lactulose for IBS.

PLAB application: Tailor treatment to stool pattern and pain.

IBS-D management

Loperamide can be used for troublesome diarrhoea, titrated to stool consistency; assess other causes if symptoms are atypical or persistent.

PLAB application: Do not use antimotility drugs in bloody diarrhoea with systemic illness without evaluation.

Pain and psychological therapies

Antispasmodics can be considered for cramping; low-dose tricyclic antidepressants may be considered second-line with monitoring and shared decision-making. Psychological therapies can help refractory IBS.

PLAB application: Explain gut–brain interaction without implying symptoms are imaginary.

Safety-net and follow-up

Review response, quality of life, dietary adequacy, medication harms and emerging alarm features. Refer when diagnosis is uncertain, symptoms are severe or treatment fails.

PLAB application: New bleeding, anaemia, weight loss or nocturnal symptoms warrant reassessment.

PLAB 2 counselling

Validate the impact of symptoms, explain the diagnosis positively, discuss targeted tests, personalised treatment and safety-netting.

PLAB application: Use shared decision-making and avoid stigmatising language.

PLAB 2: IBS counselling OSCE

  1. Ask about pain, bowel habit, duration, diet, medications and quality of life.
  2. Screen for bleeding, weight loss, anaemia, family history and nocturnal symptoms.
  3. Explain gut–brain interaction and the rationale for limited initial investigations.
  4. Discuss subtype-specific dietary and medical treatments with shared decisions.
  5. Arrange review and safety-net for new alarm features.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Visceral hypersensitivity

Altered perception of intestinal distension can amplify pain despite no visible structural lesion.

Gut–brain interaction

Bidirectional neural, immune and endocrine signalling contributes to symptom variability and stress sensitivity.

Microbiome

Microbial patterns may influence fermentation and symptoms, but routine microbiome testing is not established IBS care.

Why calprotectin helps

Neutrophil-derived calprotectin rises with gut inflammation; interpret results with age, medicines and clinical context.

Bile acid diarrhoea

Excess bile acids reaching the colon stimulate secretion and motility, causing watery diarrhoea.

Psychological therapies

CBT and gut-directed approaches can reduce symptom burden without implying the condition is psychological only.
