Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs)

PLAB priority: Use a confidential sexual history, site-specific testing, recognise PID and other emergencies, and address partner notification and prevention.

01. Clinical approach

STIs may be asymptomatic. Take a confidential, non-judgemental sexual history, assess symptoms and exposure sites, offer appropriate testing and discuss safeguarding, pregnancy and partner notification.

PLAB application: Ask about vaginal, oral and anal exposures to select swab sites; never assume site from identity.

02. Sexual history and consent

Explain confidentiality and its limits, obtain consent, and use inclusive language. Ask about symptoms, last exposure, partners, barrier protection, contraception, pregnancy possibility, previous STIs, vaccination, HIV prevention and coercion.

PLAB application: Acknowledge embarrassment and explain why questions guide safe care.

03. Red flags and emergencies

Severe pelvic or abdominal pain, pregnancy with pain or bleeding, fever or sepsis, testicular pain, neurological or ocular symptoms, and sexual assault need urgent pathways. Exclude ectopic pregnancy and torsion where relevant.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency assessment for routine STI results.

04. Chlamydia

Chlamydia trachomatis often causes no symptoms; it may cause cervicitis, urethritis, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), epididymitis and infertility. Diagnose with NAAT on first-catch urine or appropriate vaginal/other site swabs.

PLAB application: Follow current BASHH treatment and pregnancy guidance; ensure partner management.

05. Gonorrhoea

Neisseria gonorrhoeae can cause urethral discharge, dysuria, cervicitis, rectal or pharyngeal infection and disseminated disease. Use site-specific NAAT and obtain culture for susceptibility when possible because resistance is a major concern.

PLAB application: Treatment is guided by current BASHH resistance recommendations; arrange test of cure as indicated.

06. Syphilis

Treponema pallidum causes primary painless chancre, secondary rash (including palms/soles), latent infection and possible late neurological or cardiovascular disease. Diagnose with treponemal and non-treponemal serology; interpret in clinical context.

PLAB application: Urgently assess ocular, otic or neurological symptoms; pregnancy requires specialist care.

07. Genital herpes

HSV-1 or HSV-2 causes painful vesicles or ulcers, sometimes dysuria and systemic symptoms. Swab fresh lesions for HSV PCR. Antivirals shorten episodes and suppress recurrences; discuss transmission and asymptomatic shedding.

PLAB application: Avoid implying that a negative lesion swab excludes infection when lesions have healed.

08. HPV and genital warts

Human papillomavirus causes anogenital warts and is linked to cervical, anal and other cancers. Diagnosis of typical warts is clinical. Vaccination and cervical screening reduce preventable disease; wart treatments do not eradicate latent HPV.

PLAB application: Offer vaccination advice and ensure eligible screening rather than using HPV tests to diagnose visible warts.

09. Trichomoniasis

Trichomonas vaginalis may cause vaginal discharge, vulval irritation or urethral symptoms; many infections are asymptomatic. NAAT is preferred where available. Treat with recommended nitroimidazole regimens and manage partners.

PLAB application: Consider differential diagnoses including bacterial vaginosis and candidiasis, which are not necessarily STIs.

10. Pelvic inflammatory disease

PID is upper genital tract inflammation often associated with chlamydia, gonorrhoea and other organisms. Suspect with lower abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness or adnexal tenderness; pregnancy test and assess for ectopic pregnancy.

PLAB application: Start empiric treatment promptly when clinically suspected; severe illness, pregnancy or abscess may require admission.

11. Epididymitis and urethritis

Urethritis causes dysuria or discharge; epididymitis causes scrotal pain and swelling. Evaluate age, exposure and urinary pathogens, but sudden severe testicular pain mandates urgent torsion assessment.

PLAB application: Send appropriate NAAT and cultures; antibiotic choice depends on likely organisms and guidelines.

12. HIV, hepatitis and combined screening

STI assessment is an opportunity to offer HIV and syphilis testing, assess hepatitis B vaccination, consider hepatitis C risk and offer broader tests based on exposure. Explain window periods and retesting where needed.

PLAB application: Offer HIV PrEP assessment for ongoing risk and urgent PEP assessment after significant recent exposure.

13. Post-exposure and sexual assault

HIV PEP is time-sensitive: assess as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours after a qualifying exposure. Consider emergency contraception, hepatitis B prophylaxis, STI testing, safeguarding and forensic referral after assault.

PLAB application: Use trauma-informed care; do not pressure someone into examination or reporting.

14. Partner notification and abstinence

Explain partner notification sensitively, using sexual health services for confidential support. Avoid sex until the infection-specific recommended interval has passed and partners have been treated where appropriate.

PLAB application: Give written safety-net advice, follow-up and reinfection prevention.

15. Pregnancy and neonatal implications

Untreated syphilis can cause congenital infection; chlamydia and gonorrhoea can affect pregnancy and newborns. Drug selection and follow-up differ during pregnancy. Offer prompt specialist-directed screening and treatment.

PLAB application: Check pregnancy possibility before prescribing and avoid assuming routine adult regimens apply.

16. PLAB 2 OSCE framework

Introduce yourself, confirm privacy and consent, explore symptoms and sexual exposure without judgement, assess red flags and safeguarding, explain tests and confidentiality, discuss treatment, partners, prevention and follow-up.

PLAB application: Use plain language, check understanding and offer a chaperone for intimate examination.

Deep Dive

Anatomical site matters

Urine or genital testing does not reliably detect pharyngeal and rectal infections. A consensual, specific exposure history informs site-specific NAAT.

NAAT versus culture

NAAT is sensitive for chlamydia and gonorrhoea; gonococcal culture remains important for antimicrobial susceptibility and treatment failures.

Syphilis serological interpretation

Treponemal tests can remain positive after treatment; non-treponemal titres assist staging and response monitoring. Clinical history and prior treatment matter.

PID and fertility

Ascending genital tract inflammation may cause tubal scarring, infertility and ectopic pregnancy. Early empiric therapy is important when clinical suspicion is significant.

Antimicrobial stewardship

Gonorrhoea resistance patterns change. Use current UK BASHH guidance and local susceptibility results; avoid memorising an outdated universal regimen.

Prevention and public health

Vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B, condoms, PrEP, timely PEP, screening and confidential partner notification complement individual treatment.
