Emergency Procedures: Airway Management, IV Access, Blood Gases, ECG & Defibrillation

Safety first: Procedural skills must be performed within training and local policy. An unstable airway, cardiac arrest or shock needs immediate team escalation.

Procedural safety and preparation

Begin with ABCDE and call for senior support. Explain the procedure, obtain consent where feasible, check identity, allergies, equipment, monitoring and infection control. Use a checklist and have a backup plan.

PLAB application: Treat immediate threats before nonessential procedures.

Airway assessment

Assess speech, stridor, gurgling, facial trauma, secretions and consciousness. Predict difficult airway using anatomy, physiology and clinical context. Prepare suction, oxygen, bag-mask equipment and specialist help.

PLAB application: An airway that is patent now can deteriorate.

Basic airway manoeuvres

Use head tilt–chin lift when appropriate; use jaw thrust with manual in-line stabilisation if cervical spine injury is suspected. Clear visible obstruction, suction secretions and reassess ventilation.

PLAB application: Do not perform blind finger sweeps.

Airway adjuncts

Oropharyngeal airways may help an unconscious patient without a gag reflex; nasopharyngeal airways may be inappropriate in suspected basal skull fracture or severe facial trauma. Choose size correctly and monitor response.

PLAB application: Adjuncts do not replace definitive airway management.

Bag-mask ventilation

Use a good mask seal, airway positioning and two-person technique when needed. Give enough ventilation for visible chest rise and avoid excessive pressure or rate. Use capnography when available and appropriate.

PLAB application: Poor seal and obstruction are common reversible problems.

Advanced airway and intubation

Endotracheal intubation and rapid sequence induction are high-risk interventions for trained clinicians with an agreed difficult-airway plan. Optimise oxygenation, haemodynamics and monitoring; confirm tube placement with continuous waveform capnography.

PLAB application: Do not attempt advanced airway procedures outside competence.

Emergency oxygen and monitoring

Attach pulse oximetry, ECG and blood-pressure monitoring. Titrate oxygen to the clinical indication: commonly 94–98% in acutely ill adults, or 88–92% in those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure pending blood gases and clinical review.

PLAB application: Treat the patient, not a single saturation reading.

Peripheral IV cannulation

Choose an appropriate site and cannula size; use aseptic non-touch technique, confirm patency, flush and secure the line. Document attempts and complications. Escalate difficult access promptly rather than repeatedly attempting without a plan.

PLAB application: A patent IV line is crucial for time-critical treatment.

Intraosseous access

When emergency vascular access cannot be obtained rapidly, trained clinicians may use intraosseous access according to local resuscitation guidance. Confirm placement and monitor for extravasation or compartment syndrome.

PLAB application: Do not delay resuscitation while struggling for IV access.

Venous and arterial blood gases

Venous gases often assist with pH, bicarbonate and lactate trends; arterial sampling is useful for precise oxygenation and carbon dioxide assessment. Interpret results with clinical context, inspired oxygen and timing.

PLAB application: A venous PO2 does not assess arterial oxygenation.

Acid–base interpretation

Review pH, PaCO2, bicarbonate, lactate and anion gap when relevant. Identify respiratory versus metabolic disturbance and possible mixed disorders. Check glucose and ketones when DKA is suspected.

PLAB application: Treat the underlying cause rather than the number alone.

12-lead ECG acquisition

Use correct limb and chest lead placement, reduce artefact and record time and symptoms. Obtain an ECG promptly in chest pain, syncope, palpitations or suspected electrolyte disturbance; repeat if symptoms persist.

PLAB application: A normal first ECG does not exclude ACS.

Systematic ECG interpretation

Check rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, QRS morphology, ST segments and T waves. Identify immediately dangerous patterns including STEMI, broad-complex tachycardia, severe bradycardia and hyperkalaemic changes.

PLAB application: Interpret ECG alongside stability and clinical presentation.

Defibrillation in cardiac arrest

Confirm unresponsiveness and absent normal breathing, summon help, start CPR and attach a defibrillator. VF and pulseless VT are shockable; deliver shocks according to current Resuscitation Council UK ALS guidance with minimal CPR interruption.

PLAB application: Clear everyone before shock delivery and resume CPR immediately.

Synchronised cardioversion versus pacing

Unstable tachyarrhythmia with a pulse may need synchronised cardioversion; severe symptomatic bradycardia may require pacing. These require appropriate monitoring, sedation when feasible and trained team support.

PLAB application: Do not confuse unsynchronised defibrillation with synchronised cardioversion.

PLAB 2 procedure communication and handover

State indication, risks, consent, safety checks and aftercare; use a chaperone where appropriate. Reassess ABCDE, document observations and procedural outcome, and communicate escalation using SBAR.

PLAB application: Recognise limits, ask for help early and close the loop.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Emergency procedure station

Introduce yourself, assess ABCDE, identify indication and contraindications, explain and consent when possible, request appropriate assistance, prepare and monitor, describe safe steps without attempting procedures beyond competence, reassess and hand over using SBAR.

Deep Dive — Emergency Procedures

Airway decision-making

Basic manoeuvres, suction, adjuncts and bag-mask ventilation are foundational. Advanced airway interventions need trained staff, a failed-airway plan, physiological optimisation and waveform capnography.

Oxygen and ventilation

Oxygen targets differ by risk of hypercapnic failure. Pulse oximetry cannot measure ventilation or reliably detect carbon monoxide poisoning. Blood gases and clinical examination help guide escalation.

Vascular access escalation

Choose IV size for intended therapy and patient condition. Difficult access may require ultrasound guidance or emergency intraosseous access by trained personnel. Monitor for extravasation.

Blood gas pitfalls

Interpret venous versus arterial samples correctly. Venous PO2 cannot substitute for arterial oxygenation; a normal lactate does not exclude serious illness. Consider mixed acid–base disorders.

Defibrillation and rhythm management

VF and pulseless VT are shockable cardiac arrest rhythms; PEA and asystole are not. A tachycardia with a pulse requires assessment of instability and potentially synchronised cardioversion, not the pulseless arrest algorithm.

Safe procedural documentation

Record indications, consent or emergency justification, equipment and monitoring, attempts, complications, results, reassessment and senior involvement. Use structured SBAR communication.
