Abdominal distension: the complete approach

Abdominal distension is an objective increase in abdominal girth, whereas bloating is usually a subjective sensation of fullness or pressure. They often coexist but are not interchangeable. Your first job is to distinguish a benign fluctuating complaint from obstruction, peritonitis, infection, ascites or malignancy.

60-second clinical summary

Start with ABCDE and observations if acutely unwell. Ask: When did it start? Pain? Vomiting? Passing flatus and stool? Fever? Weight loss? Periods/pregnancy? Liver disease? Previous operations? Examine for peritonism, hernias, shifting dullness, masses and bowel sounds (which alone cannot rule out obstruction). Consider the traditional six Fs: flatus, faeces, fat, fluid, fetus, fatal growth — a memory aid, not a complete differential. Severe pain, persistent vomiting, obstipation, shock or rigidity demand urgent surgical assessment.

1. The physiology: why does the abdomen enlarge?

Gasswallowed air, fermentation, impaired transit
Stoolconstipation, faecal loading
Fluidascites, fluid collections
Masstumour, cyst, organ enlargement
Pregnancy / body habitusuterus, adipose tissue

Gas: the gastrointestinal lumen normally contains air from swallowing and microbial fermentation. Distension develops when gas production, movement or clearance changes; functional bloating can occur without major objective volume increase because of visceral hypersensitivity and altered abdomino-phrenic responses.

Stool: reduced transit, inadequate hydration, medications (opioids, antimuscarinics, iron), hypothyroidism and neurologic disease may cause significant loading. Always ask about paradoxical overflow diarrhoea in faecal impaction.

Fluid: portal hypertension increases splanchnic hydrostatic pressure; renal sodium retention compounds ascites. Malignancy, heart failure and infection can also produce peritoneal fluid. Pitting oedema, jaundice, stigmata of chronic liver disease and a fluid wave suggest systemic or portal disease.

Mechanical obstruction: an intraluminal or extrinsic blockage leads to proximal accumulation of fluid and swallowed gas. Bowel dilatation, vomiting, third spacing and electrolyte derangement follow. Strangulation adds vascular compromise and can progress to infarction and perforation.

Immediate red flags

Haemodynamic instability or shock; rigid tender abdomen; severe continuous pain; bilious/faeculent persistent vomiting; obstipation with a rapidly increasing distension; painful irreducible hernia; fever/sepsis; gastrointestinal bleeding; or symptoms suggesting ectopic pregnancy. Escalate urgently — do not label these patients as having “simple bloating”.

3. Examination: systematic and diagnostic

  1. General and observations: assess appearance, hydration, nutrition, pallor, jaundice, temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiration, oxygen saturation. Use ABCDE when unstable.
  2. Inspection: symmetry, scars, umbilicus, visible veins, striae, hernias, visible peristalsis, generalised versus localised bulge.
  3. Auscultation: bowel sounds may be increased, absent or normal; they are neither sufficiently sensitive nor specific to exclude obstruction.
  4. Percussion: tympany supports gas; flank dullness and shifting dullness support a large fluid collection. Small ascites may be missed.
  5. Palpation: begin gently away from pain; assess tenderness, rebound/guarding, rigidity, masses, enlarged liver/spleen, aortic pulsation and hernial orifices.
  6. Targeted examination: rectal or pelvic examination only when indicated, with consent, appropriate chaperone and clear explanation. Consider bladder scan if retention.
Shifting dullnessFlanks are dull when supine; the dullness shifts with repositioning as free peritoneal fluid moves. Moderate-volume fluid needed; sensitivity varies.
Fluid thrillMay occur with marked ascites but is less reliable, especially with obesity. Ultrasound is better for confirming small collections.
Succussion splashA persistent splash several hours after eating may suggest gastric retention or outlet obstruction, but is not independently diagnostic.
PeritonismInvoluntary guarding or rigidity with systemic illness implies urgent evaluation for perforation, ischaemia or severe inflammation.

Do not use a plain abdominal X-ray as an automatic substitute for CT in an unwell person with suspected complicated obstruction. The test depends on stability, age, pregnancy status and local pathways. Investigation should not delay emergency resuscitation or senior review.

6. Initial management according to severity

A. Suspected bowel obstruction or acute abdomen

Use ABCDE, request urgent surgical help, keep nil by mouth when indicated, gain IV access, provide appropriate analgesia and antiemetics, correct fluid/electrolyte deficits with reassessment, and consider nasogastric decompression for persistent vomiting or significant obstruction under team guidance. Monitor urine output and serial abdominal examinations. Treat sepsis promptly; antibiotics depend on suspected infection, ischaemia or perforation and local protocols. The decision for conservative, endoscopic or operative management depends on cause, ischaemia/peritonism and patient physiology.

B. Ascites

Assess for underlying liver disease, malignancy, heart and kidney disease; confirm with ultrasound where appropriate and arrange diagnostic paracentesis for new-onset ascites. A serum–ascites albumin gradient (SAAG) of at least 11 g/L supports portal-hypertensive physiology, but does not identify the cause alone. If a patient with cirrhosis and ascites has fever, abdominal pain, encephalopathy, renal worsening or unexplained deterioration, urgent sampling for spontaneous bacterial peritonitis is necessary; do not delay indicated antimicrobials for a prolonged workup.

C. Persistent abdominal distension with possible ovarian cancer

For patients with female reproductive organs, especially aged 50 or over, persistent or frequent distension (particularly >12 times per month), early satiety, pelvic pain or urinary frequency warrants ovarian-cancer assessment. NICE NG12 was updated in 2026: in patients aged 40 or over with suggestive persistent symptoms, measure serum CA125 and use age-specific thresholds for urgent ultrasound; in those aged 39 or under, consider urgent ultrasound rather than relying on CA125 alone. An abdominal/pelvic mass or ascites on examination warrants suspected-cancer pathway referral. A reassuring initial result does not end follow-up if symptoms persist.

Do not memorise the old universal “CA125 ≥35 for everyone” rule. NICE 2026 recommends age-specific thresholds. Check the live NG12 recommendations before clinical use.

D. Uncomplicated constipation or functional bloating

After excluding red flags, address contributing drugs, fluid/fibre intake (adjust to tolerance), mobility, toileting and a suitable laxative regimen where indicated. Consider coeliac or other testing when history supports it. IBS and functional bloating need explanation, follow-up and safety-netting; do not dismiss progressive or persistent new symptoms, especially with early satiety, bleeding, anaemia, weight loss or new onset in older adults.

7. Worked clinical cases

Case 1 — Previous surgery + obstruction

A 67-year-old with prior laparotomy develops colicky pain, bilious vomiting and no flatus. The abdomen is tympanitic and distended; pulse 116. Reasoning: adhesional small-bowel obstruction is likely, but strangulation cannot be excluded. Action: ABCDE, IV access, analgesia, fluid and electrolyte assessment, urgent surgery and CT in a sufficiently stable patient. Escalate immediately if continuous pain, peritonism or shock develops.


Case 2 — New ascites

A 54-year-old with alcohol-related cirrhosis notices increasing abdominal girth and ankle swelling. Shifting dullness is present. Reasoning: portal-hypertensive ascites is plausible but not proven. Action: bloods, ultrasound, diagnostic paracentesis (SAAG, protein, cell count and cultures where appropriate), then specialist-guided sodium, diuretic and complication management. Fever or confusion raises immediate concern for SBP.


Case 3 — Persistent “bloating” is not IBS by default

A 62-year-old has daily progressive distension, early satiety and increased urinary frequency. Reasoning: ovarian cancer is an important differential. Action: focused examination, primary-care testing under updated NICE NG12 and urgent ultrasound/referral as indicated. Follow up persistent symptoms even with an initial normal result.


Case 4 — Postoperative ileus or mechanical blockage?

Three days after hip surgery, a patient receiving morphine develops a distended abdomen, nausea and reduced bowel movements. Reasoning: opioid-induced constipation or ileus is plausible; do not assume benignity with severe pain, vomiting, fever or peritonism. Action: assess observations, medication, electrolytes, rectal loading and clinical severity; imaging/surgical review if obstruction or complications are possible.

8. PLAB 2: eight-minute station

Candidate task: A 55-year-old patient reports “My stomach has been getting bigger for weeks.” Take a focused history, explain concerns and outline your management.
  1. Introduce yourself, check identity, obtain consent and ask what concerns the patient most.
  2. Clarify time course, pain, vomiting, stool/flatus, fever, appetite, weight loss, early satiety, bleeding, urinary/menstrual/pregnancy symptoms and relevant medical or surgical history.
  3. Ask medication, alcohol, family history, diet and functional impact. Assess red flags.
  4. Explain that distension has different possible causes — gas/stool, fluid or a mass — and that tests depend on findings.
  5. Offer observations, abdominal examination with consent, blood tests, pregnancy test if relevant, ultrasound/CT if indicated and specialist referral when needed.
  6. Safety-net clearly: worsening pain, repeated vomiting, inability to pass wind/stool, fainting, bleeding or fever warrant urgent assessment.

Communication example: “There are several explanations for the swelling, many of which are treatable. Because it has been persistent, I don't want to assume it's only trapped wind. I'd like to examine you and arrange tests to understand the cause.”

Mechanisms & clinical integration

Deep Dive: from peritoneal physiology to differential diagnosis

1. Distension is not just too much gas

Patients with disorders of gut–brain interaction may experience a strong sensation of bloating without substantial excess gas volume. Visceral hypersensitivity amplifies discomfort from normal stretch; disordered abdominal wall/diaphragm responses can create visible distension. This explains why a normal CT does not mean a patient's discomfort is imaginary. Treat functional diagnoses positively after appropriate evaluation, and explain the physiology without dismissiveness.

2. Why obstruction causes third spacing

With luminal blockage, secreted gastrointestinal fluid and gas accumulate proximally, raising intraluminal pressure. Vomiting and sequestration of fluid can reduce circulating volume, causing tachycardia, acute kidney injury and metabolic disturbances. A closed-loop obstruction can impair venous outflow and then arterial perfusion; strangulation progresses to mucosal injury, necrosis, bacterial translocation and perforation. A normal serum lactate early on offers no reassurance when clinical suspicion is high.

4. Ascites: Starling forces, SAAG and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

Cirrhotic portal hypertension increases splanchnic pressure and promotes arterial vasodilation with neurohormonal sodium retention. Net fluid accumulation exceeds lymphatic drainage. Compare serum and ascitic albumin taken close together: SAAG = serum albumin − ascitic fluid albumin (both g/L). A SAAG ≥11 g/L supports portal hypertension (for example cirrhosis or cardiac ascites). Ascitic total protein can help separate causes but must be interpreted in context. A polymorphonuclear leukocyte count ≥250 cells/mm³ suggests SBP and requires urgent management in the appropriate clinical setting.

Worked calculation: Serum albumin = 29 g/L. Ascitic fluid albumin = 12 g/L. SAAG = 17 g/L, supporting portal-hypertensive physiology. It does not prove cirrhosis.

5. Diagnostic trade-offs: ultrasound versus CT

Ultrasound is dynamic, radiation-free and good for detecting free fluid and pelvic pathology; operator dependency and bowel gas can limit views. In a stable adult with suspected mechanical obstruction, contrast CT clarifies the level, transition point, cause and complications. A patient with possible bowel ischaemia may need CT angiography. Choose imaging according to acuity, pregnancy, renal considerations and available expertise, not a one-test-fits-all routine.

6. Ovarian malignancy and why timing matters

Ovarian cancer can present with nonspecific fullness, early satiety and urinary symptoms before severe pain. NICE NG12 updated the CA125 approach in 2026: older “35 IU/mL at every age” shortcuts are inaccurate. For aged 40+ with suggestive persistent symptoms, age-specific CA125 cut-offs guide urgent ultrasound; for aged ≤39, urgent ultrasound may be more useful than CA125 alone. A physical examination revealing ascites or an unexplained pelvic mass is a suspected-cancer referral indication. CA125 is not a standalone cancer diagnosis and may rise for benign reasons.

7. PLAB traps and common mistakes

  1. Calling all distension IBS without checking obstruction, malignancy and red flags.
  2. Thinking bowel sounds or a normal lactate excludes obstruction/ischaemia.
  3. Assuming passage of some stool excludes partial obstruction.
  4. Skipping pregnancy testing in a patient who could be pregnant.
  5. Starting diuretics for newly diagnosed ascites without an assessment for its cause and infection.
  6. Assuming a normal CA125 excludes ovarian cancer despite persistent concerning symptoms.
  7. Giving laxatives without assessing suspected obstruction.

8. Clinical reflection

Ask yourself: Is this patient acutely unstable? Is it objectively distended? Which of the five broad mechanisms fits? What is my most dangerous plausible diagnosis? What single next investigation changes management? How will I explain the plan and safety-net? These questions move you from memorisation to safe practice.
