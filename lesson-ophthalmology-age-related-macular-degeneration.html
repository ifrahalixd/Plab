Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

Urgent red flag: New central visual distortion or loss may be wet AMD. Refer promptly to a macular service.

What is AMD?

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) affects the macula, the central part of the retina responsible for fine detail, reading and face recognition. Peripheral vision is usually preserved.

PLAB application: Ask about distortion, central blur and difficulty reading.

Risk factors

Increasing age and smoking are major risk factors. Family history, cardiovascular risk and genetic susceptibility also contribute.

PLAB application: Smoking cessation is a key modifiable intervention.

Dry (non-neovascular) AMD

Dry AMD typically progresses gradually and involves drusen and retinal pigment epithelial changes. Advanced geographic atrophy can cause marked central vision loss.

PLAB application: Do not assume all gradual visual loss is cataract.

Wet (neovascular) AMD

Wet AMD involves abnormal choroidal neovascularisation, leakage or bleeding, and may cause rapid central visual deterioration.

PLAB application: New distortion or sudden central visual change requires urgent macular assessment.

Classic symptoms

Patients may report blurred central vision, straight lines appearing wavy (metamorphopsia), missing central areas or difficulty recognising faces.

PLAB application: Ask whether one or both eyes are affected.

Examination

Check visual acuity in each eye, pinhole if indicated, pupils, fundus and macular appearance. Drusen, pigment changes, haemorrhage or exudation may be seen.

PLAB application: A normal-looking basic fundoscopy does not exclude macular disease.

Amsler grid

An Amsler grid can help patients notice new distortion or missing lines, but it does not replace a full assessment.

PLAB application: Explain monocular self-monitoring and urgent reporting of changes.

Optical coherence tomography

OCT produces cross-sectional retinal images and helps identify intraretinal/subretinal fluid, drusen and atrophy.

PLAB application: OCT is central to evaluating suspected wet AMD.

Fluorescein angiography

Specialists may use fluorescein angiography when needed to characterise neovascular lesions or clarify diagnosis.

PLAB application: It is not always required before treatment.

Wet AMD referral

NICE NG82 recommends urgent referral to a macular service for suspected active wet AMD, normally within 1 working day; treatment should start promptly if indicated.

PLAB application: Do not delay urgent referral while waiting for community tests.

Wet AMD treatment

Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections suppress vascular leakage and can stabilise or improve vision. Treatment usually requires repeated visits and OCT monitoring.

PLAB application: Discuss adherence, realistic outcomes and injection-related warning signs.

Dry AMD management

There is no routine anti-VEGF treatment for dry AMD. Provide low-vision support, risk-factor advice and monitoring; management of geographic atrophy may depend on current local availability.

PLAB application: Do not prescribe intravitreal anti-VEGF for uncomplicated dry AMD.

AREDS supplements

Specific AREDS2 formulations may reduce progression risk in selected patients with intermediate AMD or advanced AMD in one eye. They do not cure AMD or prevent it in everyone.

PLAB application: Recommend specialist discussion; avoid suggesting supplements replace assessment.

Differential diagnoses

Consider cataract, diabetic macular oedema, central serous chorioretinopathy, macular hole, epiretinal membrane and retinal vascular disease.

PLAB application: New central visual symptoms require proper examination rather than diagnosis by age alone.

Safety-netting

Sudden central visual distortion or loss, a new dark patch or rapid deterioration needs urgent ophthalmology review. Pain or marked redness after intravitreal injection is an emergency.

PLAB application: Warn about endophthalmitis symptoms after injection.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explore visual impact, smoking, driving, reading, emotional wellbeing and home support. Explain dry versus wet AMD, the referral pathway and follow-up in plain language.

PLAB application: Offer low-vision and rehabilitation resources when appropriate.

PLAB 2 — AMD consultation

Introduce yourself, obtain consent and assess central vision symptoms in each eye. Ask about onset, distortion, smoking, functional impact and existing eye care. Explain dry versus wet AMD, urgent referral when appropriate, possible injections, follow-up and low-vision support. Safety-net new distortion, central scotoma and rapid decline.

Deep Dive

Macular anatomy

The fovea has densely packed cone photoreceptors, supporting high-acuity central vision. Damage produces central rather than peripheral symptoms.

Drusen and retinal pigment epithelium

Drusen are extracellular deposits associated with AMD. Pigment epithelial dysfunction contributes to photoreceptor damage.

Choroidal neovascularisation

Abnormal vessels can cross tissue barriers and leak fluid or blood. Anti-VEGF treatment suppresses this activity.

OCT interpretation

Fluid, pigment epithelial detachment and atrophy can be assessed on OCT; specialists integrate imaging with symptoms and acuity.

Risk and prevention

Smoking cessation is strongly encouraged. AREDS2 supplements have stage-specific evidence and should not be described as universal prevention.

Functional impact

Macular disease may impair reading and facial recognition while navigation with peripheral vision remains possible; low-vision rehabilitation is valuable.
