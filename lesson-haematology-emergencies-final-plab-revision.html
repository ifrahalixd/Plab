Haematology Emergencies & Final PLAB Revision

Emergency priority: Stabilise ABCDE, call for help early and initiate time-critical treatment while investigations proceed.

First five minutes: ABCDE

Identify airway compromise, hypoxia, shock, major haemorrhage and altered consciousness. Call for senior help, monitor observations, obtain IV access and send urgent bloods.

PLAB application: Treat the patient, not a laboratory number.

Neutropenic sepsis

Suspect in a patient receiving anticancer treatment who becomes unwell, even without a high fever. Obtain cultures if possible without delaying treatment; give empiric IV antibiotics urgently per local protocol.

PLAB application: Do not wait for neutrophil count confirmation.

Acute leukaemia

Fatigue, bleeding, infection, bruising and circulating blasts can signal acute leukaemia. Request urgent FBC, film, coagulation profile and haematology assessment.

PLAB application: A normal total WBC does not exclude leukaemia.

Acute promyelocytic leukaemia

APL may cause catastrophic coagulopathy and bleeding. Suspected APL requires immediate haematology involvement and prompt ATRA on specialist advice, plus blood product support.

PLAB application: Treat suspected APL as a time-critical emergency.

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

TTP often causes microangiopathic haemolysis and thrombocytopenia; renal and neurological findings vary. Send ADAMTS13 sample before treatment if feasible, but do not delay urgent specialist plasma exchange.

PLAB application: Normal PT/APTT may distinguish TTP from DIC.

Disseminated intravascular coagulation

DIC is secondary to severe illness such as sepsis, trauma, obstetric catastrophe or malignancy. Platelets and fibrinogen may fall, PT/APTT rise and D-dimer increase. Treat the cause and support haemostasis.

PLAB application: Search for and manage the trigger.

Major haemorrhage

Activate local major haemorrhage protocol, control bleeding, involve relevant teams and coordinate blood components with transfusion laboratory. Monitor temperature, calcium, coagulation and fibrinogen.

PLAB application: Never delay resuscitation while awaiting full results.

Acute transfusion reaction

Stop the transfusion immediately, keep IV access with normal saline using new tubing, assess ABCDE, verify patient/unit identity and notify blood bank. Manage anaphylaxis or shock appropriately.

PLAB application: Do not restart a suspected serious reaction.

Severe symptomatic anaemia

Assess haemodynamic stability, chest pain, syncope, dyspnoea and ongoing bleeding. Transfusion decisions depend on context, symptoms and thresholds; identify and treat the underlying cause.

PLAB application: A stable patient may not need automatic transfusion.

Sickle cell acute chest syndrome

New respiratory symptoms or signs, hypoxia and a new pulmonary infiltrate in sickle cell disease raise concern for acute chest syndrome. Give oxygen if hypoxic, analgesia, antibiotics when indicated and urgent specialist care.

PLAB application: This can deteriorate rapidly.

Sickle cell stroke and sequestration

Acute focal neurological deficit in sickle cell disease is a stroke emergency requiring immediate stroke and haematology pathways; splenic sequestration can cause acute shock and rapid Hb fall.

PLAB application: Escalate immediately; transfusion decisions are specialist-led.

Massive pulmonary embolism

PE with shock or sustained hypotension is high risk. Resuscitate and involve senior emergency/critical care teams; anticoagulation and reperfusion depend on haemodynamic status and bleeding risk.

PLAB application: Use the high-risk PE pathway, not routine outpatient management.

Anticoagulant-associated bleeding

Assess severity, timing and indication for anticoagulant, renal function and last dose. Stop anticoagulant temporarily when appropriate and seek urgent reversal advice for life-threatening bleeding.

PLAB application: Reversal depends on the specific drug.

Spinal cord compression in myeloma

New severe back pain, limb weakness, sensory changes or bladder/bowel dysfunction may indicate metastatic spinal cord compression. Arrange urgent assessment, MRI and specialist management.

PLAB application: Do not reassure without neurological assessment.

Tumour lysis syndrome

Rapid tumour cell breakdown can cause hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperuricaemia and acute kidney injury. Monitor ECG, electrolytes, urine output and institute urgent specialist therapy.

PLAB application: Hyperkalaemia may be immediately life-threatening.

PLAB 2 emergency communication

Use SBAR handover, explain urgency in plain language, obtain consent where possible and document observations, actions, reassessment and escalation. Safety-net with explicit warning symptoms.

PLAB application: A safe escalation plan is often the key mark.

PLAB 2 — emergency station checklist

Introduce yourself, wash hands, assess ABCDE and observations, request immediate senior support, perform focused history and examination, explain the urgent plan, arrange investigations without delaying treatment, reassess and hand over using SBAR.

Deep Dive — emergency distinctions

TTP vs DIC

TTP: microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia and thrombocytopenia, typically normal PT/APTT. DIC: consumptive coagulopathy with often prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen and a trigger such as sepsis.

Transfusion reactions

Anaphylaxis involves airway or circulatory compromise. TRALI is non-cardiogenic respiratory deterioration; TACO is volume overload. Both need immediate cessation of transfusion and urgent assessment.

Neutropenic sepsis and antibiotics

NICE CG151 recommends immediate empiric antibiotics for suspected neutropenic sepsis. Choice follows local resistance and allergy protocols; do not delay treatment for investigations.

Sickle cell emergencies

Acute chest syndrome, stroke, splenic sequestration and severe infection require urgent escalation. Avoid undertreating pain and monitor respiratory status closely.

Emergency laboratory patterns

TTP: schistocytes, low platelets, haemolysis. Tumour lysis: high potassium/phosphate/urate, low calcium. APL: coagulopathy and low fibrinogen. Interpret trends and clinical status.

PLAB final exam strategy

Recognise red flags, state first action, then targeted tests and definitive management. Explain decisions and escalate early. In a deteriorating patient, do not choose outpatient investigation.
