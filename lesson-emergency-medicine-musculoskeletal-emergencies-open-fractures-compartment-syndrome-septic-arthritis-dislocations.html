Musculoskeletal Emergencies: Open Fractures, Compartment Syndrome, Septic Arthritis & Dislocations

Emergency red flags: pulseless or cold limb, uncontrolled bleeding, suspected compartment syndrome, open fracture, septic joint or neurovascular deficit require urgent escalation.

Initial assessment and priorities

Begin with ABCDE and control catastrophic bleeding. Look for deformity, open wounds, threatened skin, neurovascular compromise, systemic illness and pain out of proportion. Provide appropriate analgesia early, immobilise the limb and call senior orthopaedic help. Assess mechanism, time, comorbidities, anticoagulants and tetanus status.

PLAB application: Do not let imaging delay resuscitation or treatment of an immediately threatened limb.

Neurovascular examination

Document pulses, capillary refill, colour, temperature, active movement and sensory function distal to the injury. Compare sides and examine individual nerve territories. Palpable pulses do not exclude compartment syndrome or arterial injury. Repeat and document examinations after reduction, splinting or any clinical change.

PLAB application: Record pre- and post-reduction neurovascular findings.

Open fractures: recognition

Any fracture communicating with a skin wound is open, even if the wound is small. Cover with a sterile saline-soaked dressing and protective layer according to local protocol, avoid repeated wound exploration, photograph when appropriate, splint and arrange urgent orthoplastic assessment. Check for other injuries.

PLAB application: Do not repeatedly uncover or probe an open fracture in the emergency department.

Open fractures: antibiotics and tetanus

Give IV prophylactic antibiotics as soon as possible in accordance with the local open-fracture protocol; early administration is important. Assess tetanus immunisation and provide prophylaxis if indicated. Definitive wound debridement, stabilisation and soft-tissue coverage are specialist decisions.

PLAB application: Antibiotics do not replace urgent surgical debridement and appropriate wound management.

Major limb haemorrhage and vascular injury

Control external bleeding with direct pressure and appropriate haemorrhage measures. Hard signs such as absent distal pulses with ischaemia, expanding haematoma or active arterial bleeding require immediate vascular/trauma involvement. Reduce a grossly deformed limb urgently when circulation is threatened under suitable supervision.

PLAB application: A pulseless, cold limb is a time-critical emergency.

Acute compartment syndrome

Rising pressure within a fascial compartment compromises tissue perfusion. Classically follows tibial fractures, crush injuries or reperfusion, but can occur without fracture. Early findings include escalating pain, pain on passive stretch and tense compartments; paraesthesia or weakness may follow. Pulses can remain present.

PLAB application: Severe pain out of proportion with passive-stretch pain needs immediate orthopaedic review.

Compartment syndrome management

Remove constrictive casts or dressings promptly, maintain the limb at heart level, support perfusion and urgently involve the surgical team. Clinical suspicion is sufficient to escalate; pressure measurements may help when examination is unreliable but must not delay treatment. Definitive treatment is emergency fasciotomy.

PLAB application: Do not elevate the limb high above heart level or wait for absent pulses.

Septic arthritis: recognition

Acute painful swollen joint with restricted movement, fever or inability to bear weight is concerning. Fever may be absent, especially in older or immunosuppressed patients. The knee and hip are common sites. Risk factors include prosthetic joints, diabetes, recent procedures, bacteraemia and immunosuppression.

PLAB application: Treat suspected septic arthritis as an emergency because cartilage destruction can occur rapidly.

Septic arthritis: investigation and treatment

Obtain blood cultures and arrange urgent joint aspiration for cell count, Gram stain, culture and crystal analysis where feasible. Crystals do not exclude infection. Start empiric IV antibiotics promptly following cultures/aspiration if this does not cause harmful delay; an unstable patient needs immediate treatment. Urgent orthopaedic drainage or washout may be needed.

PLAB application: Never delay antibiotics in a septic or unstable patient just to aspirate a joint.

Dislocations: first principles

Look for abnormal joint contour, deformity, severe pain and inability to move. Assess and document neurovascular status before reduction. Provide adequate analgesia and appropriate sedation with monitoring if required. Obtain imaging when appropriate, reduce promptly under supervision, immobilise, repeat neurovascular examination and confirm alignment.

PLAB application: A dislocation with neurovascular compromise requires urgent reduction/escalation.

Shoulder dislocation

Anterior shoulder dislocation commonly follows abduction and external rotation; the shoulder appears squared off. Check axillary nerve sensation over the lateral deltoid and distal vascular status. Consider associated fractures. Use an appropriate reduction technique with analgesia/sedation, post-reduction imaging and sling/follow-up.

PLAB application: Loss of lateral shoulder sensation suggests axillary nerve injury.

Hip dislocation

A posterior hip dislocation often presents after high-energy trauma with the limb flexed, adducted and internally rotated. It is associated with acetabular fractures, sciatic nerve injury and avascular necrosis risk. Arrange urgent reduction, specialist review and imaging; assess for other trauma.

PLAB application: Do not delay urgent reduction of a traumatic hip dislocation.

Knee dislocation and vascular risk

A knee may spontaneously reduce after dislocation, so a relatively normal appearance does not exclude injury. Popliteal artery damage can cause limb ischaemia. Check pulses, ankle-brachial pressure index where appropriate and urgent vascular imaging or review according to findings and protocol.

PLAB application: Normal-looking knee alignment does not exclude a dangerous vascular injury.

Fracture complications and special injuries

Recognise fat embolism syndrome after long-bone trauma (respiratory distress, neurological changes and sometimes petechiae), crush-related rhabdomyolysis, infection, nerve injury and non-union. In children consider growth-plate injury and safeguarding when injury history is inconsistent.

PLAB application: Always consider associated injuries rather than focusing only on the obvious fracture.

Imaging and safe discharge

Use targeted X-rays, usually including the joints above and below a long-bone fracture when indicated. CT may define complex injuries; ultrasound or MRI can aid selected infection assessments. Before discharge ensure pain control, safe mobility, written immobilisation advice, appropriate follow-up and red flags for worsening pain, numbness, swelling or fever.

PLAB application: Discharge is unsafe with evolving neurovascular symptoms or uncontrolled pain.

PLAB 2 approach and handover

Explain what you are doing, obtain consent, offer a chaperone when appropriate and treat pain. For a threatened limb use ABCDE, inspect, assess distal circulation/sensation/movement, cover wounds, splint, start indicated antibiotics and call orthopaedics. Give an SBAR handover with mechanism, time, observations, neurovascular findings, interventions and response.

PLAB application: Say explicitly when you would escalate immediately and repeat neurovascular checks.

PLAB 2 OSCE — threatened limb

Introduce yourself and assess ABCDE; control bleeding, give analgesia, inspect injury and document distal pulses, sensation and movement. Cover open wounds, immobilise, administer antibiotics for open fracture, and call orthopaedic/vascular teams. Reassess after interventions and hand over using SBAR.

Deep Dive — Musculoskeletal Emergencies

Compartment syndrome is a clinical emergency

The earliest useful clues are progressive pain and pain with passive stretch. A present pulse is not reassuring. Remove external compression, keep the limb at heart level and escalate for emergency fasciotomy.

Open fracture pathway

Early IV antibiotics, sterile dressing, realignment and splintage, neurovascular documentation and orthoplastic referral are priorities. Specialist teams determine operative timing and coverage.

Septic arthritis versus crystal arthritis

A hot joint can represent either condition and they can coexist. Joint fluid culture and specialist assessment are crucial. Do not delay treatment in sepsis.

Dislocations and vascular injury

Document nerve and vascular function before and after reduction. Knee dislocations can reduce spontaneously yet retain popliteal arterial damage; hip dislocations need prompt reduction.

Pain, sedation and reassessment

Provide timely analgesia; procedural sedation needs trained staff, monitoring, equipment and recovery arrangements. Re-examine neurovascular status after every manoeuvre.

Safety-netting and communication

Explain worsening pain despite analgesia, new numbness, weakness, cold digits, fever and increasing swelling as reasons to return immediately. Document discussions and referrals.
