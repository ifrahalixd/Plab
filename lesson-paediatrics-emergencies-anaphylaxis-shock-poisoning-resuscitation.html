Paediatric Emergencies: Anaphylaxis, Shock, Poisoning & Resuscitation

Emergency priority: Call for help, assess ABCDE, treat immediate threats and reassess. IM adrenaline is first-line for anaphylaxis; suspected shock, poisoning and seizures require rapid escalation.

Recognising the critically ill child

Use a structured ABCDE assessment and summon senior paediatric/resuscitation support early. Look for altered consciousness, exhaustion, central cyanosis, stridor, silent chest, prolonged capillary refill, weak pulses, hypotension and reduced urine output. Reassess after each intervention.

PLAB application: Do not wait for hypotension: it is a late sign of paediatric shock.

Airway emergencies

Check airway patency, position and breathing effort. Stridor, drooling, muffled voice or inability to swallow suggest upper airway obstruction. Avoid upsetting a child with suspected epiglottitis; obtain expert airway assistance. Use age-appropriate airway manoeuvres and equipment.

PLAB application: A child with stridor and deteriorating consciousness needs immediate expert help.

Breathing and respiratory failure

Assess respiratory rate, work of breathing, air entry, oxygen saturation and fatigue. Severe asthma, bronchiolitis, pneumonia, anaphylaxis and foreign-body aspiration can progress to respiratory failure. Oxygen and ventilatory support follow resuscitation guidance and the clinical context.

PLAB application: A silent chest or falling respiratory effort can signal impending arrest.

Circulation and shock

Assess heart rate, pulse volume, capillary refill, temperature gradient, blood pressure, mental state and urine output. Shock may be hypovolaemic, distributive, cardiogenic or obstructive. Obtain vascular access, check glucose and treat the cause while monitoring response.

PLAB application: Tachycardia and poor perfusion may precede low blood pressure.

Anaphylaxis: recognition

Suspect anaphylaxis with sudden airway, breathing or circulation problems after a possible trigger; skin changes may be absent. Common triggers include foods, medicines and insect stings. Distinguish isolated urticaria from life-threatening systemic reactions.

PLAB application: Do not require a rash to diagnose anaphylaxis.

Anaphylaxis: emergency treatment

Call for help, remove the trigger if possible and give intramuscular adrenaline into the anterolateral thigh using the current Resuscitation Council UK age-appropriate algorithm. Position the patient safely, give oxygen if needed and monitor. Repeat IM adrenaline after 5 minutes if airway, breathing or circulation problems persist. IV adrenaline is for specialist settings.

PLAB application: IM adrenaline is first-line; antihistamines and steroids must not delay it.

Anaphylaxis aftercare

After stabilisation, arrange appropriate observation and specialist allergy referral, review suspected triggers and provide an emergency action plan and adrenaline auto-injector education where indicated. Explain that biphasic reactions can occur and advise when to call emergency services.

PLAB application: A child recovering from anaphylaxis still needs appropriate observation and discharge planning.

Sepsis and septic shock

Sepsis may present with fever or hypothermia, altered behaviour, poor feeding, mottled skin, tachycardia and reduced urine output. Begin ABCDE, obtain cultures where this does not delay care, administer timely IV antibiotics according to local protocol, and involve senior/critical care teams.

PLAB application: Normal temperature or blood pressure does not rule out sepsis.

Fluid resuscitation and reassessment

In shock, give fluid boluses only according to current paediatric guidance, considering cardiac disease and other contraindications. Reassess work of breathing, lung findings, perfusion and hepatomegaly after each bolus; excessive fluid can harm. Escalate early if shock persists.

PLAB application: Do not blindly repeat boluses without reassessment.

Hypoglycaemia and altered consciousness

Check bedside glucose promptly in any child with seizures, confusion or reduced consciousness. Treat confirmed hypoglycaemia using age-appropriate local emergency protocols, protect the airway and look for underlying causes such as diabetes treatment, infection or metabolic disease.

PLAB application: Never overlook glucose in an unconscious or seizing child.

Poisoning: initial assessment

Prioritise ABCDE and identify substance, dose, formulation, time, co-ingestants and symptoms. Ask caregivers to bring packaging. Check ECG and targeted tests when indicated. Contact the UK National Poisons Information Service through TOXBASE via local clinical access.

PLAB application: A well-looking child may deteriorate after delayed-release or toxic ingestions.

Paracetamol overdose

Paracetamol toxicity may be initially asymptomatic. Record exact timing and dose, arrange a timed paracetamol concentration and other investigations as indicated, and follow current TOXBASE/NHS guidance for N-acetylcysteine. Do not rely on symptoms to exclude toxicity.

PLAB application: Urgent toxicology assessment matters even when the child looks well.

Other dangerous ingestions

Button batteries, strong magnets, iron, opioids, tricyclic antidepressants and caustic substances need urgent specific assessment. Do not induce vomiting. Button battery ingestion requires immediate emergency evaluation and imaging; a battery in the oesophagus is time-critical.

PLAB application: Do not give home remedies or delay referral for button batteries.

Seizures and status epilepticus

Protect the child from injury, time the seizure, assess airway and breathing, and check glucose. A convulsive seizure lasting 5 minutes or longer requires emergency treatment according to current paediatric status epilepticus guidance. Escalate for refractory seizures and consider infection or poisoning.

PLAB application: Never restrain a convulsing child or put objects in the mouth.

Paediatric basic life support

Check responsiveness and breathing, call for help and follow current Resuscitation Council UK paediatric basic life support guidance, including initial rescue breaths and age-appropriate compressions. Use an AED when available and follow its instructions. Team roles and repeated reassessment are crucial.

PLAB application: Paediatric arrests often follow respiratory deterioration: ventilation is vital.

PLAB 2 communication and safeguarding

Explain the emergency clearly, use short language, obtain relevant history without delaying treatment, and update caregivers as the team acts. Consider safeguarding in unexplained injuries or poisoning, document objectively and follow local pathways. Provide tailored safety-netting after stabilisation.

PLAB application: Communicate urgency while continuing ABCDE and escalation.

PLAB 2 — Emergency station

Introduce yourself, ask for immediate senior assistance, verbalise ABCDE, give clear instructions, check allergies and medication history, reassess after treatment, and communicate a concise SBAR handover. In a parent station, explain the suspected emergency and next steps without false reassurance.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Paediatric Emergencies

Anaphylaxis versus simple allergy

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis based on acute airway, breathing or circulation compromise. Skin signs can be absent; isolated hives without systemic compromise are not by themselves anaphylaxis.

Shock phenotypes

Hypovolaemia causes volume loss; distributive shock includes sepsis and anaphylaxis; cardiogenic shock may worsen with indiscriminate fluid; obstructive shock requires correction of the obstruction. Use serial perfusion findings.

Poisoning pitfalls

Do not use activated charcoal routinely or induce vomiting. Specialist advice is substance- and timing-specific. Obtain a 12-lead ECG where cardiotoxic ingestion is possible.

Paediatric resuscitation

Use current RCUK paediatric life support algorithms. Good-quality compressions and effective ventilation are essential; correct reversible causes and minimise interruptions.

Fluid caution

Reassess after each bolus and consider cardiogenic shock, severe malnutrition and other special populations requiring modified fluid approaches.

Communication under pressure

A structured SBAR handover should include age, presentation, ABCDE findings, interventions, response and immediate plan.
