Pain Pharmacology · Deep Dive

Nociception and descending control

Peripheral nociceptors respond to tissue damage and inflammatory mediators. A-delta fibres carry fast sharp pain; C fibres carry slower diffuse pain. The dorsal horn integrates inputs and descending noradrenergic and serotonergic pathways modulate transmission. Chronic pain may involve central sensitisation, altered inhibition and psychosocial factors.

COX and prostaglandins

COX enzymes convert arachidonic acid to prostanoid precursors. Prostaglandins sensitise nociceptors but also support gastric mucosal defence, renal perfusion and vascular homeostasis. NSAID analgesia and toxicity arise from these same pathways.

Paracetamol biotransformation

Most paracetamol is conjugated by glucuronidation and sulfation. A smaller fraction is oxidised to NAPQI, usually detoxified by glutathione. Excess exposure overwhelms detoxification and injures hepatocytes. Acetylcysteine replenishes glutathione-related defence; management depends on timing and current toxicology protocols.

Opioid receptor signalling

Mu receptors are Gi/o-coupled: activation reduces presynaptic calcium influx, increases postsynaptic potassium conductance and decreases neurotransmitter release. Analgesia, euphoria, constipation and respiratory depression reflect receptor actions across neural circuits.

Why opioid conversions are hazardous

Bioavailability, active metabolites, renal clearance, receptor affinity and incomplete cross-tolerance vary by drug. Conversions require patient-specific reductions and monitoring; methadone and transdermal fentanyl need specialist-level care.

Neuropathic analgesic targets

Gabapentinoids reduce excitatory neurotransmitter release via alpha-2-delta calcium-channel subunits; TCAs/SNRIs augment descending inhibitory signalling. They are not immediate rescue analgesics and may require titration and functional outcome assessment.

PLAB prescribing method

Identify the syndrome and urgent causes, agree a functional goal, assess contraindications and interactions, choose the smallest effective regimen, document maximum PRN use and review/stop dates, and give an explicit safety net.

Mechanism-to-bedside reasoning

Ascending and descending pain pathways

Transduction starts when thermal, mechanical or chemical stimuli activate peripheral nociceptors. A-delta fibres transmit rapid sharp pain and unmyelinated C fibres carry slower, diffuse signals. First-order neurones synapse in the dorsal horn; second-order pathways cross and ascend in the anterolateral system toward thalamic and cortical networks. Descending projections from periaqueductal grey and rostroventromedial medulla modulate transmission through opioidergic, noradrenergic and serotonergic signalling. Persistent input can increase spinal excitability (central sensitisation), explaining why pain severity need not correlate linearly with tissue damage.

COX-1 versus COX-2 is not a simple good/bad split

Both isoenzymes have physiological and inflammatory functions. Prostaglandins help maintain gastric mucosal blood flow and bicarbonate production; in volume depletion, renal prostaglandins help preserve afferent perfusion. Platelet thromboxane A2 and endothelial prostacyclin also affect thrombosis. This explains why COX selectivity changes some GI risk but does not abolish cardiovascular or renal toxicity.

Paracetamol kinetics and antidote rationale

At therapeutic exposure, conjugation pathways dominate elimination. Overdose saturates pathways and increases toxic NAPQI formation, causing centrilobular hepatocellular injury when glutathione defences are exhausted. Acetylcysteine replenishes substrates for detoxification and has additional benefits in established injury. The clinical pathway differs between single timed ingestion, staggered ingestion and uncertain ingestion; use the current UK poison service algorithm rather than a memorised cutoff.

Opioid tolerance, dependence and withdrawal

Repeated opioid exposure produces adaptive receptor and neural-network changes. Tolerance may develop to analgesia and some adverse effects at different rates; constipation often persists. Physical dependence can produce autonomic and gastrointestinal withdrawal symptoms on abrupt cessation, whereas opioid use disorder involves a distinct behavioural syndrome. Use planned tapering and assess patient-specific risks rather than stopping abruptly.

Why gabapentinoids are not GABA agonists

Despite their names, gabapentin and pregabalin act principally through the alpha-2-delta auxiliary subunit of presynaptic voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing release of excitatory transmitters. They do not act as direct GABA-A receptor agonists. Renal elimination makes reduced kidney function clinically important for accumulation and sedation.

Integrated clinical reasoning

In each prescription ask: What is the pain mechanism? What diagnosis must not be missed? Which organ clears the medicine or active metabolite? Which co-prescribed drugs amplify toxicity? How will the patient know if the drug helps? When will it be reviewed, reduced or stopped?

Pain Pharmacology · Clinical Masterclass

60-second clinical summary

Assess the pain mechanism and red flags before choosing analgesia. Paracetamol overdose can cause delayed liver failure. NSAIDs carry GI, renal and cardiovascular risks. Opioids can cause respiratory depression, constipation and dependence. Neuropathic pain usually needs a different drug strategy from inflammatory pain. Review benefit, function and harm after every prescribing decision.

1. Pain assessment and mechanisms

Classify acute versus chronic pain and nociceptive, neuropathic or nociplastic features. Ask about location, onset, function, sleep, red flags, previous treatments, medicines, substance use and patient goals. Severe new pain may indicate an emergency: treat the cause as well as symptoms.

2. The analgesic prescribing ladder

Use individualised multimodal care: non-drug measures, paracetamol when appropriate, an NSAID for suitable inflammatory pain, and a short opioid course only when benefits outweigh harms. Reassess effect and adverse effects; a rigid ladder is not appropriate for every pain syndrome.

3. Paracetamol: mechanism and uses

Paracetamol is analgesic and antipyretic with weak peripheral anti-inflammatory activity. Its precise central mechanisms are incompletely understood. Useful for mild to moderate pain, sometimes in combination; review total dose across cold/flu and combination preparations.

4. Paracetamol overdose

Overdose can produce severe hepatic injury from the reactive metabolite NAPQI when glutathione is depleted. Patients may initially appear well. Obtain urgent emergency/toxicology assessment, appropriate timed levels and treatment with acetylcysteine according to current UK protocols; never rely on early symptoms.

5. NSAIDs: COX inhibition

Ibuprofen, naproxen and diclofenac inhibit cyclo-oxygenase, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. They are useful for inflammatory pain but can cause peptic ulceration/bleeding, AKI, sodium retention, hypertension and cardiovascular events. Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate time.

6. NSAID contraindications and interactions

Assess prior ulcer/GI bleed, kidney disease, dehydration, heart failure, cardiovascular risk, pregnancy, asthma triggered by NSAIDs and anticoagulant use. NSAID plus ACE inhibitor/ARB plus diuretic raises AKI risk. Consider gastroprotection when indicated and avoid combining two systemic NSAIDs.

7. COX-2 inhibitors and aspirin

Selective COX-2 inhibitors may cause fewer upper-GI ulcers than nonselective NSAIDs but still carry renal and cardiovascular risks. Low-dose aspirin is antiplatelet rather than routine analgesia. Ibuprofen can interfere with aspirin antiplatelet action depending on timing; check BNF advice.

8. Opioid receptors and effects

Morphine, oxycodone and other opioids activate primarily mu opioid receptors, reducing nociceptive neurotransmission. Adverse effects include constipation, nausea, sedation, respiratory depression, urinary retention and dependence. Monitor alertness and breathing, especially after starting or escalating doses.

9. Choosing and prescribing opioids

Specify drug, immediate- versus modified-release formulation, route, dose, interval, maximum PRN dose and review date. Use careful dose conversions; equianalgesic tables are approximations and cross-tolerance is incomplete. Seek specialist advice for complex conversions and transdermal preparations.

10. Opioid toxicity and naloxone

Suspect opioid toxicity with reduced consciousness, slow or absent breathing and often pinpoint pupils (not universal). Prioritise airway and ventilation, summon emergency help and give naloxone according to local protocol. Naloxone can wear off before long-acting opioids; observe and repeat treatment as needed.

11. Opioid adverse-effect prevention

Anticipate constipation and prescribe appropriate laxatives for regular opioids unless contraindicated. Assess nausea, sedation, falls, driving, alcohol and other CNS depressants. Benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids and alcohol increase sedation/respiratory risks. Reassess efficacy and taper when no longer indicated.

12. Tramadol, codeine and pharmacogenetics

Codeine is partly activated to morphine by CYP2D6, so effects vary with genotype and interacting drugs. Tramadol also has serotonergic/noradrenergic actions and can precipitate seizures or serotonin syndrome with interacting medicines. Both require careful patient selection.

13. Chronic primary pain and opioids

NICE guidance for chronic primary pain does not recommend initiating opioids, NSAIDs or paracetamol for that indication. Emphasise supported self-management, activity and psychological approaches, with selected antidepressants after shared decision-making. Distinguish chronic primary from chronic secondary pain.

14. Neuropathic pain: first-line choices

For neuropathic pain other than trigeminal neuralgia, NICE commonly recommends offering a choice of amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin, considering patient factors. Assess response, function and adverse effects. Trigeminal neuralgia has a distinct pathway, commonly carbamazepine first line.

15. Gabapentin and pregabalin

Gabapentinoids bind the alpha-2-delta subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels. They can cause dizziness, somnolence, oedema and dependence/misuse; renal dose adjustment is important. Combined use with opioids can increase respiratory depression. Taper rather than abruptly stopping prolonged treatment.

16. Amitriptyline and duloxetine

Low-dose amitriptyline can help neuropathic pain but anticholinergic effects, sedation, falls and cardiac risk matter. Duloxetine inhibits serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake and can cause nausea, blood pressure changes and withdrawal symptoms. Check interactions and contraindications.

17. Migraine and headache analgesia

Migraine acute treatment often combines a triptan with an NSAID or paracetamol, where suitable, and may include an antiemetic. Avoid opioids for migraine. Frequent acute analgesic use can cause medication-overuse headache; review headache days and medicine days.

18. Acute postoperative and trauma pain

Assess injuries and haemodynamics, immobilise where needed and use multimodal analgesia with regular reassessment. Regional anaesthesia can reduce opioid need in selected settings. Do not allow analgesia to delay urgent investigation or treatment of compartment syndrome or other emergencies.

19. Renal, hepatic and older-patient prescribing

AKI or CKD may make NSAIDs unsafe and can alter clearance of morphine metabolites and gabapentinoids. Hepatic disease affects paracetamol and opioid risk assessment. Older adults are vulnerable to delirium, constipation, falls and polypharmacy. Consult current BNF and local protocols.

20. Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Medication selection changes across pregnancy and breastfeeding. NSAIDs have gestation-dependent fetal risks and are generally avoided in later pregnancy; opioids can affect the neonate. Check current UKTIS/BNF advice and individual clinical circumstances rather than applying one rule to all trimesters.

21. Case 1: NSAID and AKI

A 74-year-old with CKD takes ramipril and furosemide, then starts ibuprofen during gastroenteritis. Creatinine rises. Recognise dehydration and the NSAID–RAAS inhibitor–diuretic combination; stop inappropriate nephrotoxins, assess volume status and urgently manage AKI.

22. Case 2: opioid overdose

A patient on morphine and diazepam becomes difficult to rouse with a respiratory rate of six. Call emergency help, support airway and breathing, give naloxone as appropriate and monitor for recurrent sedation. Do not wait for a toxicology result.

23. Case 3: neuropathic pain

A person with painful diabetic neuropathy has burning feet and sleep disturbance. Assess glucose control, foot red flags and function; offer a guideline-supported neuropathic medicine considering comorbidities, explain delayed benefit and arrange review.

24. Case 4: paracetamol overdose

A 19-year-old presents several hours after taking many paracetamol tablets but feels well. Urgent emergency assessment is needed; treatment decisions use timing, levels and protocols, not symptoms alone.

25. PLAB 2: opioid counselling

Explain the expected benefit, time-limited plan, drowsiness and driving risk, constipation prevention, alcohol/sedative interactions, safe storage, dependence risk, review and tapering. Ask about goals and use teach-back.

26. High-yield prescribing checklist

Confirm pain mechanism and red flags → check renal/hepatic function, pregnancy, allergies and interactions → select medicine/formulation and safe duration → prescribe clear PRN limits → review function, harms and stopping plan. Verify current NICE/BNF guidance.

Clinical framework: choose treatment by pain mechanism

Begin with ABCDE when unstable, then assess onset, site, radiation, severity, quality, aggravating factors, function, sleep, and treatment already tried. Examine for neurological deficits, vascular compromise, infection, fracture, compartment syndrome, cauda equina symptoms, acute abdomen and other time-critical diagnoses. An analgesic response does not exclude serious disease.

Paracetamol: clinical pharmacology and overdose

Paracetamol provides analgesia and antipyresis, but has little clinically useful peripheral anti-inflammatory activity. Its central mechanisms remain incompletely characterised. For many adults, standard oral dosing is 500 mg–1 g per dose with at least four hours between doses and a usual maximum of 4 g in 24 hours; lower limits or specialist advice may be required for low body weight, frailty, malnutrition, liver disease or other risk factors. Verify the current BNF and local protocol for the individual patient. Count combination cold/flu and co-codamol products.

Most is metabolised through glucuronidation/sulfation; a smaller CYP-mediated fraction forms NAPQI, normally detoxified by glutathione. Overdose can be initially symptom-free. Record exact product, formulation, dose, timing, staggered ingestion and co-ingestants; seek urgent ED/toxicology advice and follow current UK paracetamol poisoning protocols for levels and acetylcysteine. Do not apply the single-acute-ingestion nomogram to staggered or uncertain-time ingestion.

Exam trap: “Looks well” does not rule out paracetamol toxicity. Never delay emergency referral while waiting for jaundice or abnormal liver tests.

NSAIDs: when benefit becomes harm

COX-1 and COX-2 catalyse prostanoid synthesis. Inhibition reduces inflammatory nociceptor sensitisation, but also removes prostaglandin protection of the stomach and prostaglandin-mediated renal afferent arteriolar vasodilation. Consequently, NSAIDs may precipitate upper-GI bleeding, AKI, fluid retention, hypertension and cardiovascular events. Risks differ by agent, dose and patient; they are not eliminated by choosing a COX-2 selective drug.

Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest clinically appropriate duration; avoid two systemic NSAIDs together. Consider PPI gastroprotection only when appropriate—it does not protect the kidneys or cardiovascular system.

Opioids: prescribing and surveillance

Mu-opioid receptors are Gi/o-coupled: presynaptic calcium-channel inhibition reduces transmitter release and postsynaptic potassium-channel activation reduces neuronal excitability. Benefits must be weighed against respiratory depression, sedation, nausea, constipation, urinary retention, falls, tolerance and physical dependence. Dependence is not identical to addiction; avoid stigmatising language while assessing harms.

For acute severe pain, choose an appropriate immediate-release medicine, route and time-limited review plan. State formulation, exact dose, frequency, PRN interval and maximum use; assess prior opioid exposure, respiratory disease, renal/hepatic function, sleep apnoea, concurrent alcohol, benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids. Regular opioid treatment usually requires proactive constipation prevention and ongoing review. Modified-release products and patches are not interchangeable with immediate-release rescue treatment; opioid-naïve patients require particular caution.

Conversion warning: Equianalgesic calculations are approximations. Incomplete cross-tolerance and patient-specific clearance can cause overdose when switching drugs. Complex conversion, methadone, transdermal fentanyl and high doses warrant specialist input; do not treat an online conversion figure as a prescribing instruction.

Opioid toxicity: clinical decision pathway

  1. Recognise: reduced consciousness with slow/shallow breathing; miosis may occur but is not universal.
  2. Call for emergency help and perform ABCDE; maintain airway, oxygenate and support ventilation as indicated.
  3. Administer naloxone according to local emergency guidance, titrated to restore adequate ventilation where appropriate. Avoid allowing administration to delay ventilation.
  4. Observe and reassess: naloxone may wear off before long-acting opioids, so recurrent respiratory depression can occur. Consider infusion and higher-level care per protocol.
  5. After stabilisation: review co-ingestants, prescription, mental health risk and prevention of recurrence.

PLAB distinction: Naloxone reverses opioid effects; acetylcysteine treats paracetamol poisoning. Neither replaces initial resuscitation.

Neuropathic pain: selecting and monitoring a drug

Neuropathic pain reflects lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system. Ask about burning pain, allodynia, electric-shock sensations and sensory deficits; investigate the cause (e.g., diabetes, shingles, nerve entrapment). NICE CG173 recommends offering a choice of amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin for neuropathic pain other than trigeminal neuralgia, considering contraindications, comorbidities, preferences and risks. Carbamazepine is commonly the initial drug for trigeminal neuralgia under its separate pathway.

Chronic primary pain, communication and deprescribing

NICE NG193 distinguishes chronic primary pain from chronic secondary pain. For chronic primary pain, do not initiate paracetamol, NSAIDs, opioids, gabapentinoids or benzodiazepines solely for that indication. Consider supervised exercise and psychological therapies, and selected antidepressants after discussion of benefits and harms. Do not abruptly stop established long-term medicines: agree an individualised review and reduction plan, accounting for withdrawal, dependence and the patient's circumstances.

Use an empathic explanation: “The pain is real, even when a scan does not show a single injury that explains it. Our aim is to improve what you can do and reduce medicine harms.” Ask what matters most to the patient; avoid implying pain is imaginary.

Four worked PLAB cases

Case A — AKI after ibuprofen

A 74-year-old with CKD, heart failure, ramipril and furosemide develops vomiting and takes ibuprofen for back pain. Oliguria and rising creatinine follow. Reasoning: volume depletion plus afferent prostaglandin blockade and efferent RAAS blockade impair glomerular filtration. Assess urgently for AKI, discontinue inappropriate nephrotoxins, assess volume and potassium, and manage according to local AKI guidance. Do not simply add a PPI.

Case B — Slow breathing after morphine

A postoperative patient given morphine and a benzodiazepine is difficult to rouse, respiratory rate 6/min. Reasoning: additive CNS depression; airway and ventilation first, emergency escalation and naloxone according to protocol, with observation for recurrence.

Case C — Burning diabetic foot pain

A patient describes nocturnal burning feet, reduced pinprick sensation and poor sleep. Reasoning: investigate neuropathy and foot risk, address diabetes care and offer an individualised CG173 first-line option. Renal impairment may favour avoiding or adjusting some agents. Reassess function and tolerability after titration.

Case D — Apparently well after overdose

A teenager presents six hours after an intentional paracetamol overdose, without symptoms. Reasoning: emergency toxicology pathway immediately, assess ingestion history, levels and antidote need, and arrange psychosocial assessment after medical stabilisation.

PLAB 2 OSCE: counselling and safety net

Station: A patient is being discharged after a short course of an opioid for an acute injury. Introduce yourself, confirm understanding and ask about other medicines and alcohol. Explain the intended short duration and how to take the exact prescribed formulation. Warn about drowsiness, driving and machinery, constipation and nausea; avoid alcohol and sedatives unless explicitly reviewed. Discuss safe storage, not sharing medicines, and what to do if pain persists. Advise urgent help for very slow breathing, severe drowsiness or inability to wake. Check understanding with teach-back and document the plan.

Examiner marks: consent and empathy; dose/formulation clarity; interactions; constipation prevention; overdose safety net; planned review; shared decision-making.

    NICE / BNF · Pain prescribing pathways

    Educational algorithms; verify current guidance and local emergency protocols.

    New pain presentation

    Assess red flags and pain mechanism → treat underlying cause → consider non-drug measures → select suitable analgesic after renal/GI/CV and interaction checks → agree review and stopping plan.

    Suspected analgesic toxicity

    ABCDE, emergency help and ventilation if needed → identify drug, timing and co-ingestants → opioid toxicity: naloxone and observation per protocol; paracetamol overdose: urgent toxicology pathway and acetylcysteine assessment → monitor and escalate.

    Neuropathic pain

    Confirm neuropathic features and exclude urgent pathology → discuss amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin as appropriate (trigeminal neuralgia differs) → titrate, monitor adverse effects and function → review response and taper/switch safely.

    Opioid safety review

    Assess analgesia, function, sedation, breathing, constipation, falls and misuse → check concurrent sedatives and kidney function → continue only if benefit outweighs harm → prescribe bowel regimen and a monitored taper where appropriate.
