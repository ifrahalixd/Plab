Plastic & Reconstructive Surgery: Wound Closure, Skin Grafts, Flaps & Hand Injuries

Surgery · Lesson 37 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Plastic and reconstructive surgery: PLAB essentials

Do not miss: a hand wound with tendon, nerve or vascular damage; flexor sheath infection; compartment syndrome; open fracture; bite injury; or a rapidly spreading necrotising infection. Document neurovascular and tendon function before local anaesthetic, and urgently involve hand/plastic surgery when indicated.

1 · Wound assessment

Use ABCDE for major trauma. Ask mechanism, timing, contamination, bite/crush/glass injury, hand dominance, occupation, tetanus and anticoagulants. Examine location, depth, size, contamination, exposed tendon/bone, active bleeding, distal perfusion, sensation and movement. Check for retained foreign body; obtain radiographs for suspected fracture or radiopaque material. Explore only when safe with adequate anaesthesia, lighting and expertise. Document and photograph with consent.

2 · Wound healing

Haemostasis (platelets/clot), inflammation (debridement and immune response), proliferation (granulation, angiogenesis, collagen and epithelialisation), and remodelling (scar maturation). Delayed healing risks: diabetes, smoking, poor perfusion, infection, malnutrition, steroids, pressure and excessive tension. Recognise hypertrophic scars (within original wound) versus keloids (extend beyond wound).

3 · Closure choices

Primary closure: clean, viable wound with well-approximated edges and acceptable tension after irrigation/debridement. Delayed primary closure: contaminated wound managed open initially, reassessed before closure. Secondary intention: allow granulation/epithelialisation for suitable wounds. Use sutures, staples, adhesive strips or tissue glue depending on site, tension and depth. Avoid closing infected wounds or high-risk bite punctures without specialist assessment. Layered closure reduces dead space and tension; do not close over unrecognised deep structural injury.

4 · Initial management

Analgesia, haemostasis with direct pressure, thorough irrigation, remove devitalised tissue when appropriate, sterile non-adherent dressing and elevation. Assess tetanus prophylaxis under current UK guidance. Antibiotics are not routine for every clean laceration; consider for bites, heavy contamination, open fractures, established infection and other high-risk wounds following local guidance. Discuss wound care, infection signs and review/suture-removal plan.

5 · Skin grafts

Split-thickness skin graft (STSG): epidermis plus part of dermis; larger surface coverage, donor site can heal, greater contraction and texture difference. Full-thickness skin graft (FTSG): epidermis plus full dermis; generally less secondary contraction, donor site needs closure, demands robust recipient-bed perfusion. Grafts need a vascularised bed; exposed tendon without paratenon or bare bone without periosteum may require a flap. Causes of graft failure: haematoma, infection, shear, inadequate vascularity and smoking.

6 · Flaps and reconstructive ladder

Choose simplest suitable reconstruction: healing by secondary intention → primary closure → graft → local/regional flap → free flap (depending on defect). Flaps carry their own blood supply and can cover exposed tendon, bone, vessels or hardware. Local flaps use adjacent tissue; pedicled flaps maintain an attached vascular pedicle; free flaps require microsurgical vascular anastomosis. Monitor flap colour, warmth, capillary refill, swelling and Doppler signals according to specialist protocol; sudden pallor or congestion is urgent.

7 · Hand examination

Inspect posture, wounds, swelling and deformity. Check median nerve (thumb opposition and index-finger pulp sensation), ulnar nerve (finger abduction and little-finger sensation), radial nerve (wrist/finger extension and first dorsal web-space sensation). Check capillary refill, radial/ulnar pulses where appropriate. Test each flexor and extensor tendon separately: isolate FDP by holding PIP extended and asking DIP flexion; isolate FDS by holding other fingers extended and asking PIP flexion. Assess active movement before anaesthesia if possible.

8 · Tendon and nerve injuries

Sharp palmar laceration with loss of active DIP flexion suggests FDP injury; dorsal laceration with loss of extension suggests extensor injury. Do not assume intact tendons because some movement remains. Irrigate, cover, splint in a safe position and seek urgent hand surgery advice for suspected tendon, nerve or arterial injury. Timing and repair depend on site and contamination.

9 · Hand infections and emergencies

Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis: Kanavel signs — finger held flexed, fusiform swelling, tenderness along flexor sheath, pain on passive extension; urgent hand surgery assessment, IV antibiotics and possible drainage. Fight bite: small wound over MCP joint after punching teeth may penetrate joint/tendon; urgent exploration/irrigation and antibiotics per protocol. Compartment syndrome: severe pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch, sensory changes; pulses can remain present. Urgent surgical assessment for fasciotomy. Necrotising infection: rapidly progressive pain/systemic toxicity, urgent resuscitation, antibiotics and debridement.

10 · Facial wounds and reconstruction

Assess facial nerve, eyelids, lacrimal structures, lips/vermilion border, parotid duct and cartilage; refer complex injuries. Align anatomical landmarks precisely. For skin cancer defects, excision margin and reconstruction require multidisciplinary planning; ensure histological clearance and appropriate follow-up.

Decision pathways

Laceration assessment

ABCDE if trauma → bleeding control → mechanism and tetanus → document pre-anaesthetic tendon, nerve and vascular function → inspect for foreign body/fracture → irrigate and decide closure versus referral.

Closure decision

Clean, viable, low-tension wound → primary closure. Heavily contaminated or infected wound → debridement, dressings and reassessment; delayed closure or secondary intention as appropriate. Deep structure injury → specialist evaluation before definitive closure.

Graft versus flap

Well-perfused bed and broad superficial defect → consider STSG. Small cosmetically important defect with suitable bed → FTSG. Exposed critical structures or poorly vascularised bed → flap often required.

Suspected flexor tendon injury

Palmar cut + inability to flex DIP/PIP → assess FDP/FDS independently, sensation and perfusion → sterile dressing, immobilise, urgent hand surgery advice; do not close and discharge without follow-up.

Kanavel signs

Fusiform swelling + flexed posture + sheath tenderness + pain on passive extension → urgent hand surgery, IV antibiotics and operative consideration; do not delay for imaging.

Threatened flap

New pallor/coolness or dusky congestion → immediately notify reconstructive team; compromised perfusion can lead to rapid flap loss.

Four clinical cases with reasoning

Case 1 — Glass cut across palm

A chef cuts the palm on broken glass and cannot actively flex the index-finger DIP joint. Sensation is reduced at the fingertip. Diagnosis: likely FDP tendon injury with possible digital nerve injury. Next: document perfusion, nerve and tendon examination before anaesthesia; assess foreign body, irrigate, dress/splint and obtain urgent hand surgery review.

Case 2 — Painful swollen finger

After a puncture wound, a patient has a uniformly swollen finger held flexed, marked flexor-sheath tenderness and pain with passive extension. Diagnosis: pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis. Next: urgent hand surgery, IV antibiotics, fasting/operative preparation as directed; do not treat as simple cellulitis alone.

Case 3 — Exposed tendon after trauma

A lower-leg wound has exposed tendon without a healthy vascular covering. The patient asks for a skin graft. Reasoning: a graft may not take on an avascular surface; a vascularised flap may be needed. Next: assess limb perfusion, contamination and associated injury, then plan specialist reconstruction.

Case 4 — Small wound after a punch

A man presents with a 5-mm cut over the third MCP joint after punching someone's mouth. Concern: clenched-fist human bite may enter the joint or tendon. Next: examine with the fist clenched and extended, radiograph as indicated, urgent hand team review, irrigation and antibiotics according to protocol; avoid routine primary closure.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Hand laceration assessment

Station: A 27-year-old has a deep glass laceration of the dominant hand.

  1. Introduce yourself, obtain consent, assess pain and haemorrhage, and use ABCDE if major trauma.
  2. Ask when/how injury occurred, hand dominance, occupation, numbness, weakness, contamination, tetanus and relevant medical history.
  3. Inspect wound and posture; assess capillary refill, pulses where appropriate, light-touch sensation in median/ulnar/radial territories and each digit.
  4. Check active and resisted flexion/extension, isolating FDP and FDS; document findings before local anaesthetic.
  5. Explain concern for tendon/nerve damage and possible glass fragment or fracture; offer analgesia and appropriate imaging.
  6. Describe irrigation, sterile dressing, elevation, immobilisation and urgent hand/plastic surgery review; antibiotics/tetanus based on risk and guidance.
  7. Safety-net for increasing pain, swelling, fever, redness, colour change, numbness or reduced movement; check understanding.

Do not perform blind deep exploration or promise immediate simple closure when structural injury is suspected.

12 key revision facts

  1. Record tendon, nerve and vascular findings before local anaesthetic.
  2. Primary closure is for appropriately cleaned, viable, low-tension wounds.
  3. Contaminated or infected wounds may need delayed closure or secondary intention.
  4. Routine antibiotics are unnecessary for most clean uncomplicated lacerations.
  5. Assess tetanus risk and prophylaxis for every relevant wound.
  6. STSG includes part of dermis and can cover large areas; FTSG includes all dermis and contracts less.
  7. Skin grafts need a well-perfused bed; flaps carry their own blood supply.
  8. FDP flexes the DIP joint; isolate by holding the PIP extended.
  9. FDS flexes the PIP joint; isolate by holding other fingers extended.
  10. Kanavel signs indicate possible pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis requiring urgent specialist assessment.
  11. Fight bites over MCP joints risk deep joint and tendon infection.
  12. Compartment syndrome can occur with palpable pulses; severe pain and pain on passive stretch are early warnings.
