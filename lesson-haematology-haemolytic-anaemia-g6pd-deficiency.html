Haemolytic Anaemia & G6PD Deficiency

PLAB red flags: Severe haemolysis with shock, acute kidney injury or schistocytes plus thrombocytopenia needs emergency assessment.

Definition and mechanisms

Haemolytic anaemia results from red cell destruction exceeding marrow replacement. Haemolysis can be intravascular or extravascular.

PLAB application: Look for anaemia plus jaundice, dark urine or splenomegaly.

Clinical presentation

Fatigue, pallor, tachycardia, jaundice, dark urine, abdominal or back pain may occur. Severe acute haemolysis can cause shock or acute kidney injury.

PLAB application: Assess observations, urine output and haemodynamic stability.

Inherited versus acquired

Inherited causes include G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis, sickle cell disease and thalassaemia. Acquired causes include autoimmune haemolysis, microangiopathy, infection and drugs.

PLAB application: Use family history, ethnicity, medications and timing to guide investigation.

Intravascular haemolysis

Red cells lyse in circulation, releasing free haemoglobin; haemoglobinuria and marked LDH rise may occur. Haptoglobin is often reduced.

PLAB application: Dark urine can reflect haemoglobinuria rather than intact red blood cells.

Extravascular haemolysis

Macrophages in spleen or liver remove damaged or antibody-coated red cells; splenomegaly and unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia may occur.

PLAB application: Consider spherocytosis and warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia.

Core investigations

Request FBC, reticulocyte count, blood film, LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, renal function and urinalysis; add group and screen/crossmatch if severe.

PLAB application: Typical pattern: raised reticulocytes, LDH and unconjugated bilirubin with low haptoglobin.

Blood film interpretation

Spherocytes suggest hereditary spherocytosis or immune haemolysis; schistocytes suggest microangiopathic haemolysis; bite/blister cells suggest oxidant damage.

PLAB application: Schistocytes with thrombocytopenia warrant urgent assessment for TMA.

Direct antiglobulin test

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs) detects immunoglobulin or complement bound to red cells. It supports immune haemolysis but must be interpreted clinically.

PLAB application: G6PD deficiency usually has a negative DAT.

G6PD deficiency genetics

G6PD deficiency is usually X-linked and impairs the red cell response to oxidative stress. Severity varies by variant and individuals of any sex may be affected.

PLAB application: Ask about family history and previous episodes after illness or drugs.

G6PD triggers

Infections, fava beans and certain oxidant medicines can precipitate haemolysis. Drug safety depends on agent and dose; consult current specialist drug guidance.

PLAB application: Do not give an unverified universal list of safe or unsafe drugs.

G6PD attack

An episode may cause sudden fatigue, jaundice, dark urine and falling haemoglobin after a trigger. Film may show bite cells; Heinz bodies need supravital stain.

PLAB application: Check kidney function, potassium and clinical severity.

G6PD testing pitfall

Quantitative G6PD enzyme activity may be falsely normal during or soon after acute haemolysis because older deficient cells have been destroyed and reticulocytes have higher activity.

PLAB application: Repeat testing after recovery if suspicion remains high.

Managing acute G6PD haemolysis

Stop the trigger, treat infection, provide supportive care and hydration appropriate to clinical status. Severe anaemia may need transfusion and specialist care.

PLAB application: Escalate severe symptoms, hypotension, oliguria or significant haemoglobin fall.

Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia

Warm AIHA is commonly IgG-mediated and may be associated with autoimmune disease, lymphoproliferative disease or drugs. Specialist-guided treatment often includes corticosteroids.

PLAB application: A positive DAT plus haemolysis supports immune aetiology.

TMA and other emergencies

TTP, HUS and DIC may produce schistocytes, thrombocytopenia and organ injury. Suspected TTP requires emergency haematology input and time-critical treatment.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency referral for confirmatory testing.

PLAB 2 counselling and prevention

Explain inherited nature of G6PD deficiency, avoiding known triggers, checking medicines with clinicians/pharmacists, seeking help for jaundice or dark urine and informing future healthcare teams.

PLAB application: Offer written information and appropriate family discussion or genetic counselling.

PLAB 2 — G6PD counselling

Introduce yourself, obtain consent and ask about previous episodes, family history, infection, diet and medicines. Explain oxidative haemolysis, why certain medicines and fava beans may trigger episodes, how to check medication safety and when to seek urgent help for jaundice, dark urine, severe weakness or reduced urine output.

Deep Dive

Why G6PD matters

G6PD generates NADPH in the pentose phosphate pathway. NADPH maintains reduced glutathione, protecting red cells from oxidant injury.

Heinz bodies and bite cells

Oxidised haemoglobin precipitates as Heinz bodies; splenic macrophages remove inclusions, producing bite cells.

DAT interpretation

A positive DAT can support immune haemolysis but does not alone prove active haemolysis; correlate with clinical and biochemical findings.

Reticulocyte caveats

Reticulocytes may not rise promptly in early haemolysis or with marrow suppression, infection or nutrient deficiency.

G6PD assay timing

Testing during an acute episode may be falsely reassuring. Arrange repeat quantitative testing after recovery when indicated.

TTP urgency

Untreated TTP can rapidly cause life-threatening organ injury; urgent specialist treatment is required.
