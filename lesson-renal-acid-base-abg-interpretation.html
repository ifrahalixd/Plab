Acid–Base Disorders & Arterial Blood Gas Interpretation

UKMLA / PLAB-focused clinical reasoning, safe escalation and worked arterial blood gases.

01. First look: the sick patient

Use ABCDE, respiratory rate, SpO₂, BP, consciousness and perfusion before interpreting numbers. Treat airway, breathing and shock urgently. An ABG supports—but never replaces—clinical assessment.

PLAB pearl: Escalate severe respiratory distress, shock, reduced consciousness or marked acidaemia immediately.

02. What an ABG measures

pH reflects acid–base state; PaCO₂ reflects the respiratory component; bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) reflects the metabolic component. PaO₂ assesses arterial oxygenation; lactate suggests impaired perfusion or other increased production/reduced clearance.

PLAB pearl: Read oxygen delivery (room air or FiO₂), sample time and patient context.

03. Typical adult reference ranges

Approximate values: pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa (35–45 mmHg); HCO₃⁻ 22–26 mmol/L; PaO₂ around 10–13 kPa on room air in a healthy young adult (varies with age and altitude); lactate often <2 mmol/L. Use local laboratory ranges.

PLAB pearl: A normal pH can conceal a mixed disorder.

04. A reliable five-step method

1. Check pH: acidaemia <7.35 or alkalaemia >7.45. 2. Check PaCO₂: high acidifies; low alkalinises. 3. Check HCO₃⁻: low acidifies; high alkalinises. 4. Assess compensation and mixed disorders. 5. Review oxygenation, lactate, electrolytes and clinical context.

PLAB pearl: State the primary disorder out loud, then whether compensation is appropriate.

05. Respiratory acidosis

Raised PaCO₂ from alveolar hypoventilation: COPD exacerbation, severe asthma with fatigue, opioid toxicity, neuromuscular weakness, obesity hypoventilation. Acute cases have limited renal compensation; chronic cases may have higher HCO₃⁻.

PLAB pearl: A rising or normalising PaCO₂ in a tiring severe asthma patient can be ominous.

06. Respiratory alkalosis

Low PaCO₂ from hyperventilation: pain, anxiety, pregnancy, sepsis, pulmonary embolism, hypoxaemia. Do not label unexplained tachypnoea as anxiety without excluding dangerous causes.

PLAB pearl: An ABG with low PaCO₂ does not exclude PE or sepsis.

07. Metabolic acidosis

Low HCO₃⁻ from added acid or bicarbonate loss: DKA, lactic acidosis, kidney failure, diarrhoea, toxins. Compensation is increased ventilation, reducing PaCO₂.

PLAB pearl: Kussmaul breathing in DKA is a compensatory response.

08. Metabolic alkalosis

High HCO₃⁻ from acid loss or alkali retention: vomiting, nasogastric suction, diuretics, mineralocorticoid excess. Check chloride, potassium, volume status and medication history.

PLAB pearl: Hypokalaemia and hypochloraemia often coexist.

09. Anion gap

Calculated as Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), usually without potassium. A common approximate reference range is 8–12 mmol/L, but ranges depend on the lab. Increased gap suggests unmeasured acids (lactate, ketones, uraemic acids, some toxins); normal-gap acidosis suggests bicarbonate loss or renal tubular causes.

PLAB pearl: Low albumin lowers the measured gap; correct interpretation for albumin when relevant.

10. Expected compensation

For metabolic acidosis, Winter’s formula estimates expected PaCO₂ in mmHg = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 ± 2 (divide by 7.5 for kPa). Higher measured PaCO₂ suggests additional respiratory acidosis; lower suggests respiratory alkalosis. Other disorders have different acute/chronic rules.

PLAB pearl: Compensation never overcorrects pH into the opposite primary disorder.

11. Mixed acid–base disorders

Examples: sepsis with lactic acidosis plus respiratory alkalosis; COPD with CO₂ retention plus diuretic-related metabolic alkalosis; salicylate toxicity with respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis. Compare actual PaCO₂ to expected compensation.

PLAB pearl: Near-normal pH with markedly abnormal PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ demands careful analysis.

12. Oxygenation and type 1 respiratory failure

Type 1 respiratory failure is hypoxaemia (commonly PaO₂ <8 kPa) without hypercapnia. Causes include pneumonia, pulmonary oedema and pulmonary embolism. Interpret PaO₂ against inspired oxygen and clinical state.

PLAB pearl: Pulse oximetry cannot tell you PaCO₂ or acid–base status.

13. Type 2 respiratory failure

Type 2 respiratory failure is hypoxaemia with raised PaCO₂ (>6 kPa). Causes include COPD, sedative overdose, obesity hypoventilation and neuromuscular disease. Oxygen should be titrated to a prescribed target, often 88–92% if at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure pending blood gases.

PLAB pearl: Escalate for ventilatory support if persistent acidosis and respiratory failure despite initial treatment.

14. Lactate interpretation

Lactate can rise with shock and hypoperfusion, seizures, severe exertion, beta-agonists, liver dysfunction and some medicines. A high lactate prompts reassessment of perfusion and causes; a normal lactate does not rule out serious infection.

PLAB pearl: Repeat lactate when clinically indicated and assess trends, not a single number alone.

15. Worked ABG: DKA

pH 7.18, PaCO₂ 3.0 kPa, HCO₃⁻ 9 mmol/L, ketones 5 mmol/L: primary metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation. Winter’s expected PaCO₂ = 21.5 ± 2 mmHg (~2.9 kPa), matching the measured value.

PLAB pearl: Manage DKA using a recognised local/JBDS pathway; check potassium and ketones.

16. Worked ABG: COPD exacerbation

pH 7.27, PaCO₂ 9.0 kPa, HCO₃⁻ 29 mmol/L, PaO₂ 7.2 kPa on controlled oxygen: respiratory acidosis with some metabolic compensation, consistent with acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure. Assess for NIV eligibility and escalation.

PLAB pearl: Give controlled oxygen, treat exacerbation and reassess clinically and with blood gases.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship

Blood pH depends on the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO₂. Lungs regulate CO₂ over minutes; kidneys adjust bicarbonate over hours to days.

Why compensation matters

A second disorder is suspected when the measured compensatory response differs materially from expectation. Renal compensation for chronic respiratory disturbances is greater than for acute ones.

Anion gap and albumin

Albumin is a major unmeasured anion. Hypoalbuminaemia can mask a raised anion gap; consider an albumin-adjusted gap when appropriate.

Delta gap concept

In high-gap acidosis, compare rise in anion gap to fall in bicarbonate to look for additional metabolic processes; this is supportive, not definitive.

ABG versus VBG

Venous gases often help assess pH and bicarbonate, but venous PO₂ cannot substitute for arterial oxygenation; arterial sampling may be needed for precise PaO₂ and PaCO₂ decisions.

PLAB 2 OSCE script

Explain the sample, consent, assess pain and circulation, use aseptic technique, and communicate results in plain language. Escalate immediately for severe acidosis, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia with reduced consciousness, or shock.
