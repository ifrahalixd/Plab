Diverticular Disease, Diverticulitis & Colorectal Emergencies

Surgery · Lesson 08 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Diverticular disease and acute diverticulitis

Exam priority: Distinguish uncomplicated diverticulitis from perforation, abscess, obstruction, fistula or sepsis. A patient with generalised peritonitis, shock or suspected perforation needs immediate resuscitation and urgent surgical review.

1 · Definitions

TermMeaning
DiverticulosisColonic diverticula without symptoms; common with increasing age.
Diverticular diseaseSymptoms attributable to diverticula without acute diverticulitis.
Acute diverticulitisInflammation of diverticula, usually sigmoid colon, causing acute pain and tenderness.
Complicated diverticulitisAssociated abscess, perforation, peritonitis, fistula or obstruction.

2 · Presentation and differential

Typical presentation is constant left iliac fossa pain, tenderness, altered bowel habit and sometimes fever. Older or immunosuppressed patients may have atypical features. Ask about rectal bleeding, urinary symptoms, previous episodes, steroid/NSAID use, pregnancy possibility and colorectal cancer red flags. Consider colorectal cancer, colitis, IBS, appendicitis, renal colic, UTI, ectopic pregnancy and ovarian pathology.

3 · Assess severity

  1. ABCDE and observations; assess sepsis, shock, hydration and analgesia needs.
  2. Examine for focal tenderness, guarding, rebound, distension, palpable mass and peritonism; examine hernial sites and perform other examinations when clinically indicated with consent.
  3. Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&Es/renal function; pregnancy test where relevant; cultures and lactate if sepsis is suspected.
  4. Consider contrast-enhanced CT abdomen/pelvis when complicated diverticulitis is suspected, to identify abscess, free air, obstruction or alternative diagnosis; use local imaging protocols and renal/contrast assessment.

4 · Uncomplicated diverticulitis: NICE NG147

For a systemically well person with suspected acute diverticulitis, consider a no-antibiotic strategy, provide analgesia and clear safety-net advice. Offer antibiotics if the person is systemically unwell, immunosuppressed or has significant comorbidity. Hospital assessment is needed for suspected complications, severe illness, inability to tolerate oral intake or uncontrolled pain. Review antibiotic choice and duration against current NICE antimicrobial tables and local resistance guidance; do not automatically prescribe antibiotics to everyone.

5 · Complicated diverticulitis

Abscess: admit, IV antibiotics and imaging; larger accessible abscesses may need image-guided drainage. NICE advises considering percutaneous drainage or surgery for abscesses larger than 3 cm, according to anatomy and clinical condition. Free perforation/generalised peritonitis: resuscitation, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and urgent surgery for source control. Obstruction: nil by mouth, IV fluids, decompression if indicated, and surgical assessment. Colovesical fistula: pneumaturia, faecaluria and recurrent UTIs; refer for specialist evaluation.

6 · Diverticular bleeding and other colorectal emergencies

Diverticular bleeding often causes painless fresh rectal bleeding and can be substantial. Assess haemodynamic status and resuscitate; consider CT angiography for active severe bleeding and involve gastroenterology, interventional radiology and surgery as appropriate. Other emergencies include toxic megacolon (systemic toxicity with colonic dilatation), large-bowel obstruction and perforated colorectal cancer. Severe abdominal pain, distension, fever, shock or peritonitis demands escalation.

7 · Follow-up and prevention

Do not assume persistent symptoms are always diverticulitis: reassess for colorectal malignancy or inflammatory bowel disease when clinically indicated. Colonoscopy is not performed during the acute inflamed phase because of perforation risk; subsequent colonic assessment is based on imaging, risk factors, alarm features and specialist advice. Encourage balanced high-fibre dietary habits after recovery when appropriate; avoid unnecessary routine restrictions on nuts or seeds.

PLAB traps: LLQ pain does not exclude serious disease; a normal temperature does not exclude complications. Antibiotics are not mandatory for every well patient with uncomplicated diverticulitis. Pneumaturia suggests colovesical fistula. Painless major bleeding suggests diverticular haemorrhage rather than acute diverticulitis.

Decision pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Suspected diverticulitis

Left-sided abdominal pain → observations + focused examination → identify peritonitis/sepsis/complications → if systemically well and uncomplicated, consider outpatient care, analgesia and no antibiotics with safety-netting → if unwell or complicated, hospital assessment and CT as appropriate.

Pathway B — CT shows abscess

Admit + IV antibiotics + senior colorectal review → assess abscess size/accessibility → consider image-guided drainage or surgery if >3 cm or deteriorating → reassess clinical response and arrange follow-up.

Pathway C — Free perforation

Generalised peritonitis or shock → ABCDE, IV access, bloods/crossmatch, fluids tailored to perfusion, IV antibiotics → urgent anaesthetic and surgical review → emergency source control.

Slide 1 · Pain versus bleeding

Diverticulitis = usually painful, inflamed sigmoid colon. Diverticular haemorrhage = often painless haematochezia.

Slide 2 · Three complications

Abscess; perforation/peritonitis; fistula or obstruction.

Slide 3 · Antibiotic decision

Systemically well uncomplicated diverticulitis: consider no antibiotics. Systemic illness, immunosuppression or significant comorbidity: offer antibiotics.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Mild uncomplicated diverticulitis

A 54-year-old has left iliac fossa pain, mild local tenderness, normal observations and tolerates fluids. Approach: assess for alternative diagnoses and complications; consider outpatient management with analgesia, no antibiotics if systemically well, clear return precautions and reassessment if worsening.

Case 2 — Diverticular abscess

A 70-year-old has fever, persistent pain and raised inflammatory markers. CT shows a 5-cm pericolic abscess. Approach: admit, IV antibiotics, senior colorectal review and consider image-guided drainage if technically feasible; monitor for deterioration.

Case 3 — Perforation and peritonitis

A 76-year-old has severe diffuse abdominal pain, guarding, tachycardia and hypotension. Approach: ABCDE, sepsis/shock resuscitation, urgent IV antibiotics, senior surgical and anaesthetic escalation, and urgent source control.

Case 4 — Colovesical fistula

A 63-year-old with recurrent UTIs describes bubbles in the urine and occasional faecal material. Approach: suspect colovesical fistula, assess infection, arrange specialist colorectal/urological evaluation and appropriate imaging.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute left iliac fossa pain station

Brief: You are an FY2 in ED. A 62-year-old presents with left-sided abdominal pain and fever. Take a focused history, assess risk and explain the plan.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and assess immediate stability.
  2. Explore onset, site, severity, radiation, bowel habit, nausea, vomiting and bleeding.
  3. Ask about fever, urinary symptoms, recurrent episodes, medications and immunosuppression.
  4. Ask about weight loss, persistent change in bowel habit and family history of colorectal cancer.
  5. Explain observations, abdominal examination, bloods and CT if complications are suspected.
  6. Recognise peritonitis, sepsis, dehydration or inability to tolerate oral intake.
  7. Explain analgesia, oral/IV fluids, selective antibiotics and possible admission/surgery.
  8. Safety-net: worsening pain, persistent vomiting, fainting, fever, inability to drink or bleeding require urgent reassessment.

Patient explanation: “Small pouches can form in the bowel wall. Sometimes they become inflamed. Most mild episodes settle, but we need to check for infection or complications that might need hospital treatment.”

Rapid revision and UK references

  1. Diverticulosis is not the same as diverticulitis.
  2. Acute diverticulitis classically causes left iliac fossa pain.
  3. Complications: abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction.
  4. Systemically well uncomplicated disease: consider no antibiotics, with safety-netting.
  5. CT with contrast helps assess suspected complications.
  6. Abscess >3 cm: consider drainage or surgery alongside antibiotics, depending on clinical context.
  7. Peritonitis/shock: urgent resuscitation, antibiotics and source control.
  8. Pneumaturia/faecaluria suggests colovesical fistula.
  9. Painless large-volume rectal bleeding may be diverticular haemorrhage.
