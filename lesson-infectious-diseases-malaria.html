Malaria

PLAB priority: Suspect malaria in any febrile returned traveller, assess severity, obtain urgent blood films, and treat severe malaria immediately with specialist support.

01. Overview and transmission

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted by infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. P. falciparum causes most severe disease; P. vivax and P. ovale can relapse from dormant liver hypnozoites. P. malariae and P. knowlesi are additional species.

PLAB application: Ask about travel or residence in endemic regions, even if chemoprophylaxis was taken.

02. Clinical presentation

Fever, rigors, sweats, headache, myalgia, fatigue, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea are common. Fever may be irregular, particularly early in infection. Anaemia, jaundice and splenomegaly may occur.

PLAB application: Any fever after travel to a malaria-endemic area is malaria until urgently excluded.

03. Travel and exposure history

Record countries and regions visited, dates, mosquito exposure, prophylaxis, prior malaria, pregnancy, immune suppression and onset of symptoms. Symptoms can arise weeks or months after travel.

PLAB application: Ask about travel within the preceding year and beyond if suspicion remains.

04. Recognising severe malaria

Red flags include impaired consciousness, seizures, shock, respiratory distress or acidosis, severe anaemia, hypoglycaemia, acute kidney injury, jaundice with organ dysfunction and high parasitaemia. Severe falciparum malaria can deteriorate rapidly.

PLAB application: ABCDE, urgent senior/infectious diseases or tropical medicine advice and monitored admission.

05. Immediate assessment

Assess ABCDE, temperature, pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, GCS, hydration and urine output. Check bedside glucose immediately. Establish IV access if unwell and evaluate for sepsis and alternative diagnoses concurrently.

PLAB application: Do not delay resuscitation or specialist discussion for laboratory confirmation.

06. Blood film diagnosis

Urgent thick and thin blood films are the reference diagnostic approach in many UK settings: thick films detect parasites; thin films identify species and estimate parasitaemia. Request urgent malaria testing and explicitly inform the laboratory of travel history.

PLAB application: A single negative film does not exclude malaria; repeat testing if suspicion persists, typically 2–3 sets over 24–48 hours per local laboratory protocol.

07. Rapid diagnostic tests and PCR

Malaria antigen rapid diagnostic tests may support prompt diagnosis but do not replace expert microscopy where available; false negatives and species limitations occur. PCR can aid species confirmation and complex cases but should not delay treatment.

PLAB application: Do not use a negative rapid test alone to dismiss a high-risk presentation.

08. Initial blood investigations

Obtain FBC, platelets, U&Es/creatinine, LFTs, glucose, venous or arterial gas with lactate when severe, clotting and blood cultures as indicated. Thrombocytopenia is common; anaemia and renal impairment may indicate severity.

PLAB application: Look for hypoglycaemia, acidosis, renal injury and other organ dysfunction.

09. Uncomplicated falciparum malaria

Treat confirmed or strongly suspected uncomplicated falciparum malaria promptly with an appropriate oral antimalarial regimen, often an artemisinin-based combination, following UK specialist/local guidance and resistance considerations. Confirm the patient can absorb oral medication.

PLAB application: Antimalarial choice depends on species, severity, geography, pregnancy and contraindications.

10. Severe malaria treatment

Severe malaria requires urgent parenteral treatment, generally IV artesunate, specialist-led monitoring and supportive care; transition to a full effective oral regimen once clinically appropriate. Intensive care may be required.

PLAB application: Never delay IV artesunate for transfer, confirmatory PCR or a complete diagnostic work-up when severe malaria is strongly suspected.

11. Vivax and ovale relapse prevention

P. vivax and P. ovale can persist in the liver and relapse. In addition to treating blood-stage infection, specialist-directed radical cure with primaquine or tafenoquine may be needed after quantitative G6PD testing.

PLAB application: G6PD deficiency can cause dangerous haemolysis with 8-aminoquinolines; pregnancy and breastfeeding affect eligibility.

12. Malaria in pregnancy and children

Pregnant patients and young children have higher risk of complications. Treatment choices and monitoring are specialist-led and vary with gestation, severity and species. Check glucose frequently in severe disease.

PLAB application: Urgent obstetric/paediatric and infectious diseases input; avoid assuming standard adult regimens apply.

13. Complications and follow-up

Monitor for cerebral malaria, acute kidney injury, severe anaemia, ARDS, hypoglycaemia and shock. Recheck parasitaemia and laboratory abnormalities according to protocol. Delayed haemolysis can occur after IV artesunate.

PLAB application: Ensure clinical and laboratory follow-up, especially after severe malaria.

14. Differential diagnoses

Consider dengue, enteric fever, viral infections, bacterial sepsis, leptospirosis, rickettsial infections and meningitis based on exposure and presentation. Coinfection is possible.

PLAB application: Malaria testing should run alongside appropriate sepsis evaluation rather than replacing it.

15. Prevention and travel counselling

Discuss mosquito avoidance: insecticide-treated bed nets, repellents, covered clothing and screened accommodation. Recommend country-specific chemoprophylaxis and travel-clinic advice; no prophylaxis is fully protective.

PLAB application: A traveller with fever after return needs urgent medical review even if prophylaxis was taken.

16. PLAB 2 communication and safety-netting

Explain that malaria is a blood infection transmitted by mosquitoes, testing is urgent, and treatment is effective when started promptly. Discuss admission if severe, medication adherence and warning signs including confusion, fainting, breathlessness or inability to drink.

PLAB application: Use clear language, assess travel history sensitively and escalate immediately when severe features appear.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Parasite lifecycle

Mosquito-injected sporozoites invade liver cells. Merozoites subsequently infect red blood cells; erythrocytic cycles cause fever and haemolysis. Vivax and ovale may form dormant liver hypnozoites.

Why falciparum becomes severe

Infected erythrocytes can adhere to microvascular endothelium, contributing to impaired perfusion, acidosis and cerebral or renal complications.

Interpreting films

Thick films are sensitive for detection; thin films permit speciation and parasitaemia measurement. Repeated films are important when initial testing is negative but suspicion remains high.

Why glucose matters

Severe infection and some treatments can cause hypoglycaemia, which may present as confusion, seizures or deterioration.

Antimalarial treatment decisions

Treatment is determined by severity, parasite species, region of acquisition and resistance, pregnancy, drug interactions and oral tolerance. Severe malaria warrants parenteral therapy.

Relapse and G6PD

Hypnozoites are not cleared by blood-stage therapy. Radical cure drugs can oxidatively damage red cells in G6PD deficiency; eligibility requires careful specialist assessment.
