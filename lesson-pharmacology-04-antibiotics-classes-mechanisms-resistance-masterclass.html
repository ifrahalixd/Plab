Antibiotics · Clinical deep dive

Mechanisms, microbiology and clinical consequences explained together.

MICROBIOLOGY

1 · Gram-positive versus Gram-negative envelopes

Gram-positive bacteria have thick exposed peptidoglycan; Gram-negative bacteria have thin peptidoglycan plus an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide. Porins control entry of some hydrophilic antibiotics. Periplasmic beta-lactamases can destroy susceptible beta-lactams before they reach their targets.

Clinical link: vancomycin does not penetrate the Gram-negative outer membrane effectively; polymicrobial intra-abdominal infections may need Gram-negative and anaerobic coverage.

PHARMACOLOGY

2 · Pharmacodynamic exposure

For many beta-lactams, maintaining free drug concentrations above the MIC for an adequate fraction of the dosing interval predicts activity. Aminoglycosides display concentration-dependent killing and a post-antibiotic effect. Dosing schedules are therefore not interchangeable between classes.

Clinical link: impaired kidney function can increase exposure and toxicity; critical illness may change volume of distribution and clearance. Follow validated dosing protocols.

MOLECULAR

3 · Beta-lactamases and ESBLs

Beta-lactamases hydrolyse the beta-lactam ring. ESBLs commonly inactivate third-generation cephalosporins. Carbapenemases can hydrolyse carbapenems. The exact enzyme and additional resistance mechanisms determine which combinations retain activity; a blanket rule based on drug class is unsafe.

GENETICS

4 · Horizontal gene transfer

Resistance genes spread by conjugation (plasmid transfer), transformation (uptake of DNA) and transduction (bacteriophage-mediated transfer). Selection pressure allows resistant subpopulations to expand. Infection prevention and stewardship reduce opportunities for transmission and selection.

IMMUNOLOGY

5 · Infection versus inflammation

Fever, leukocytosis and CRP elevation are not specific for bacterial infection. Viral illness, inflammatory disease and tissue injury can produce similar markers. Conversely, severe bacterial infection can occur without fever in older or immunosuppressed patients. Clinical assessment and microbiology remain central.

SOURCE CONTROL

6 · Why antibiotics may fail despite susceptibility

Abscess cavities, infected devices, obstructed urinary tracts and necrotic tissue may prevent cure without drainage, device removal or surgery. Biofilms can protect bacteria from antibiotics and immune clearance. Reassess diagnosis, source control, adherence, absorption and resistance before reflexively broadening therapy.

HIGH RISK

7 · Severe adverse effects to recognise

Immediate allergy/anaphylaxis; antibiotic-associated C. difficile colitis; gentamicin nephrotoxicity/ototoxicity; macrolide-associated QT prolongation; fluoroquinolone tendon, neurologic and other serious harms; linezolid-associated cytopenias and interaction-related serotonin toxicity. Risks differ by drug, dose and patient.

PRESCRIBING

8 · The antibiotic review conversation

Ask: Is the diagnosis still bacterial? Is the patient improving? Are cultures back? Is there a drainable source? Can we narrow therapy or switch to oral? What is the planned stop date? Document the reason for continuation and communicate it at handover.

Antibiotics treat bacterial infection by interfering with essential bacterial structures or functions. The correct prescription depends on the likely organism, infection site, severity, resistance, patient factors and local susceptibility data—not on choosing the broadest drug.

In 60 seconds: the essentials

  • Start with the clinical syndrome: bacterial infection, colonisation and viral illness are not interchangeable.
  • In suspected sepsis with high risk of serious illness, escalate and administer appropriate IV antibiotics urgently according to the current local sepsis pathway; do not delay life-saving therapy for cultures.
  • Beta-lactams inhibit bacterial cell-wall synthesis; macrolides and tetracyclines inhibit protein synthesis; fluoroquinolones interfere with DNA replication.
  • Choose empiric therapy from the infection site, likely pathogens, allergies, pregnancy status, kidney function and local resistance.
  • Obtain appropriate cultures before antibiotics when feasible without delaying urgent treatment; review results and narrow or stop therapy.
  • Check the current BNF, NICE guidance and local antimicrobial formulary for drug, dose, route and duration.

1. Foundations: how antibiotics work

Bacteria have structures absent from human cells, including peptidoglycan cell walls and 70S ribosomes. Selective toxicity exploits these differences. The bacterial envelope also determines penetration: Gram-positive organisms have a thick peptidoglycan layer; Gram-negative organisms possess an outer membrane with porins and a periplasmic space, which can restrict entry and harbour beta-lactamases.

Bactericidal drugs kill susceptible bacteria; bacteriostatic drugs mainly inhibit multiplication. These categories are context-dependent and do not independently determine the best clinical treatment. Host immunity, achievable drug concentration and site penetration matter. Antibiotic exposure is often described through time above MIC (many beta-lactams), peak/MIC (aminoglycosides) or AUC/MIC (some other agents). MIC is the minimum inhibitory concentration measured under laboratory conditions, not a universal bedside dose.

Prescribing question: What infection am I treating, which pathogens are plausible, what severity is present, what evidence supports bacteria, and what is the narrowest effective regimen likely to reach the site?

Culture and microbiology

Take relevant specimens when indicated: blood cultures for suspected bloodstream infection, urine culture for selected urinary infections, sputum or other site samples when clinically useful. Interpret positive cultures in context—skin flora may contaminate blood cultures, and asymptomatic bacteriuria usually should not be treated except in defined circumstances such as pregnancy or before selected urological procedures. A culture can be negative despite true infection, especially after antibiotics. A susceptibility report should guide de-escalation, but susceptible in vitro does not guarantee success if tissue penetration or source control is inadequate.

2. Cell-wall inhibitors: beta-lactams and glycopeptides

Beta-lactams bind penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases), blocking peptidoglycan cross-linking and weakening the cell wall. They generally have time-dependent activity. Major groups include penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems and monobactams.

Beta-lactamase inhibitors protect certain partner antibiotics from some enzymes, but do not overcome all ESBL, AmpC or carbapenemase mechanisms. MRSA carries an altered PBP (PBP2a, encoded by mecA or related mechanisms) with reduced binding to most beta-lactams. The precise agent and susceptibility testing determine exceptions.

Allergy nuance: A documented severe immediate reaction to a penicillin is not the same as nausea or a remote non-specific rash. Clarify timing, symptoms and severity. Cross-reactivity among beta-lactams depends partly on side-chain similarity; do not automatically assume every cephalosporin is safe or every beta-lactam is forbidden. Follow local allergy pathways and specialist advice for severe reactions.

3. Protein-synthesis inhibitors

Bacterial ribosomes contain 30S and 50S subunits. Blocking translation prevents the organism from making essential proteins. The site of binding helps explain spectrum, adverse effects and resistance.

Gentamicin concentration monitoring must follow the specific local dosing schedule; a random level cannot be interpreted without dose timing.

5. Choosing antibiotics for real clinical syndromes

Community-acquired pneumonia: assess severity, oxygenation, comorbidities and likely organisms. NICE recommendations depend on age, severity and circumstances; amoxicillin is a common first-line option for suitable low-severity adult community-acquired pneumonia, but atypical infection, allergy, pregnancy and severity can change choice. Do not treat all coughs with antibiotics.

Cellulitis: non-purulent cellulitis often involves streptococci and MSSA; flucloxacillin is a common UK first choice when appropriate. An abscess needs drainage assessment; antibiotics alone may not achieve source control. Distinguish uncomplicated cellulitis from necrotising infection (pain out of proportion, rapid progression, systemic toxicity), which needs emergency surgical review and IV therapy.

UTI: distinguish lower UTI, pyelonephritis, catheter-associated infection and asymptomatic bacteriuria. Nitrofurantoin is useful for many lower UTIs when kidney function and susceptibility permit but is not suitable for kidney parenchymal infection or systemic illness. Pyelonephritis with vomiting, pregnancy or sepsis may require hospital assessment and parenteral treatment.

Meningitis: suspected bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency. Obtain blood cultures when feasible, but do not delay immediate empiric IV antibiotics for lumbar puncture or imaging. The regimen depends on age, immunocompromise, pregnancy and local guidance; specialist advice is essential. Consider dexamethasone in relevant pathways.

Intra-abdominal infection: source control—drainage or surgery—is often as important as antibiotic coverage. Consider enteric Gram-negative organisms and anaerobes; choose according to severity and local policy. Sepsis: assess for shock, identify source, obtain cultures promptly where feasible, and give urgent antibiotics according to risk-stratified local sepsis guidance.

Do not memorise one universal antibiotic for every syndrome. For PLAB, recognise the syndrome and first-line UK approach, then ask whether resistance, allergy, pregnancy, renal function or severity changes the answer.

6. Prescribing safety, monitoring and special populations

Before prescribing, document the indication, suspected source, severity, allergy history, current medicines, pregnancy/breastfeeding status, weight where relevant, kidney and liver function, culture plan, dose, route, review date and expected duration. Review IV-to-oral switch once stable, able to absorb oral therapy and without a condition requiring prolonged IV treatment.

Adverse effects: beta-lactams can cause allergy and gastrointestinal symptoms; macrolides may cause QT effects; doxycycline may cause photosensitivity; fluoroquinolones carry serious musculoskeletal, neurologic and other safety warnings; metronidazole may cause neuropathy with prolonged use. For suspected anaphylaxis, stop the trigger and initiate emergency treatment according to resuscitation guidance.

7. How antimicrobial resistance develops

Resistance arises from spontaneous mutations and acquisition of genes via plasmids, transposons or other mobile elements. Antibiotic exposure selects resistant organisms; it does not deliberately teach individual bacteria to adapt. Important mechanisms include (1) enzymatic drug destruction, such as beta-lactamases; (2) altered drug targets, such as MRSA PBP2a; (3) reduced permeability through porin changes; (4) active efflux pumps; and (5) metabolic bypass or biofilm-associated tolerance.

ESBL-producing Enterobacterales can hydrolyse many penicillins and cephalosporins; susceptibility and infection severity guide treatment. Carbapenem-resistant organisms may use carbapenemases or combinations of permeability changes and other enzymes. VRE changes glycopeptide binding targets. Resistant organisms are not necessarily more virulent, but treatment options can be limited.

Antimicrobial stewardship means choosing antibiotics only when indicated, using local empiric guidelines, obtaining useful cultures, reassessing at 48–72 hours (or local review interval), de-escalating based on results, using the shortest evidence-based course and documenting stop/review dates. Avoid prescribing antibiotics for viral upper respiratory infections and avoid unnecessary treatment of colonisation.

Start smart → then focus

Assess and treat severe infection promptly → collect appropriate microbiology → document indication and review plan → reassess clinical response and results → stop, narrow, switch route or continue with a justified duration.

8. Worked clinical cases: think like a prescriber

Case A · Dysuria without fever

A non-pregnant adult woman has dysuria and frequency without flank pain, fever or systemic features. Consider uncomplicated lower UTI, symptom severity, local guidance and whether immediate or back-up antibiotics are appropriate. If prescribing nitrofurantoin, check kidney function and contraindications. Explain return precautions for fever, flank pain, vomiting or deterioration. Do not use nitrofurantoin if pyelonephritis is suspected.

Case B · Cellulitis with a painful fluctuant centre

A patient has a red, warm lower-leg lesion with a fluctuant focus. A purulent collection needs assessment for incision and drainage, not simply escalating to broad-spectrum oral antibiotics. Evaluate systemic toxicity, diabetes, immunosuppression and necrotising infection red flags. Culture pus when clinically indicated and choose antibiotics based on likely pathogens and local guidance.

Case C · Fever, hypotension and confusion

A patient is febrile, confused and hypotensive with suspected urinary sepsis. Call senior help, perform ABCDE, assess perfusion and lactate, obtain blood and urine cultures promptly if they will not delay treatment, and start appropriate empiric IV antibiotics under the emergency sepsis pathway. Review prior resistant isolates and kidney function. Arrange fluids and source-control assessment as indicated. Reassess and narrow therapy when results return.

Case D · Gentamicin with worsening kidney function

A hospitalised patient receiving gentamicin develops acute kidney injury. Review indication and duration urgently, check the timed drug level and administration record, withhold further doses pending protocol-guided assessment if indicated, review other nephrotoxins and seek pharmacy/microbiology input. A level cannot be interpreted without knowing the regimen and sample timing.

9. PLAB 2: explaining why antibiotics are not needed

Station: A 28-year-old with three days of runny nose, sore throat and cough requests amoxicillin. They are afebrile, haemodynamically stable and have no features suggesting pneumonia or a serious bacterial infection.

Structure (8 minutes)

0–2 min: Introduce yourself, confirm concerns and ask about onset, fever, breathlessness, chest pain, swallowing, hydration, comorbidities and red flags. 2–4 min: Explain the likely viral illness and expected recovery. 4–6 min: Explain that antibiotics target bacteria, may cause adverse effects and do not shorten most viral colds; discuss symptom relief and patient expectations. 6–8 min: Agree a plan, check understanding, give safety-netting for worsening breathing, persistent high fever, dehydration, confusion or deterioration, and arrange review if indicated.

Suggested wording: “I can see you feel unwell and want to recover quickly. Your symptoms currently fit a viral infection, and antibiotics would be unlikely to help. They can also cause side effects. Let’s discuss what will relieve your symptoms and which warning signs mean you should seek help.”

10. PLAB examination traps

  • Positive urine culture without symptoms does not automatically require antibiotics.
  • Nitrofurantoin is for lower urinary infection, not pyelonephritis.
  • Flucloxacillin does not reliably treat MRSA.
  • Gentamicin dosing requires weight, kidney function and regimen-specific drug-level timing.
  • Do not delay emergency treatment of suspected meningitis or high-risk sepsis to obtain tests.
  • A fluctuant abscess needs source-control assessment.
  • Broad-spectrum treatment should be reviewed and narrowed when possible.
  • Fluoroquinolones have important MHRA restrictions; do not use them reflexively for routine infections.
