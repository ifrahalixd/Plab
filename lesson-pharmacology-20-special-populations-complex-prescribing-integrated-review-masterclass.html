Deep Dive · Pharmacology across the lifespan and complex illness

1 · Allometric scaling and developmental physiology

A medicine’s clearance may correlate with size, organ blood flow and developmental enzyme/renal maturation, but a simple proportional weight rule cannot reliably predict neonatal dosing. Total body water is proportionately higher in neonates; lipid stores, plasma proteins and blood–brain barrier transport also evolve. Certain enzymes reach adult-like activity at different ages, and renal filtration/tubular secretion mature after birth. Population pharmacokinetic models sometimes use allometric scaling: roughly, clearance often scales with body mass to a power near 0.75 and volume with a power near 1, but these are modelling approximations, not bedside prescriptions. Actual dosing comes from drug-specific paediatric guidance and therapeutic response.

2 · Pharmacokinetic calculations: exposure and clearance

In a simple linear one-compartment model, clearance (CL) = dose rate / steady-state concentration, and half-life ≈ 0.693 × volume of distribution / clearance. If clearance falls while volume remains similar, plasma exposure for a fixed maintenance dose increases, and time to a new steady state lengthens. Example for arithmetic: if clearance halves, AUC approximately doubles for the same dose under linear kinetics. But physiology, protein binding and metabolites may also change. The clinician may need a longer interval rather than a smaller single dose depending on the drug’s PK/PD target.

3 · Why creatinine misleads in older people and AKI

Creatinine depends on muscle production as well as renal excretion. An older, sarcopenic patient can have low creatinine generation even with poor filtration. In evolving AKI, the serum concentration takes time to accumulate, so steady-state formula estimates do not instantly represent renal function. Cockcroft–Gault is often required for DOAC dosing, but can itself be inaccurate at weight extremes or rapidly changing kidney function. The safest decision integrates clinical trend, body size, exact product guidance and, when indicated, measured levels or renal pharmacist input.

4 · Pregnancy PK: distribution, clearance and placental transport

Pregnancy often increases plasma volume and total body water, modifies albumin concentration, alters renal blood flow/GFR and changes activities of individual metabolising enzymes. Effects differ between drugs and across gestation. The placental barrier is selective rather than absolute; passage depends on molecular properties, transporters, protein binding and gestational development. Fetal risk varies by developmental stage. A medicine with reassuring structural teratogenic data may still cause neonatal sedation or withdrawal near delivery, and a harmful drug is not rendered safe by lowering dose without evidence. Specialist treatment aims to protect maternal health while minimising fetal exposure.

5 · Hepatic impairment: high- and low-extraction drugs

Hepatic clearance depends on blood flow, intrinsic enzyme/transporter capacity and unbound fraction. High-extraction drugs may be sensitive to altered liver blood flow and first-pass metabolism; low-extraction drugs may be more sensitive to altered enzyme capacity or protein binding. Cirrhosis complicates both and creates clinical hazards including encephalopathy, renal dysfunction and variceal bleeding. Child–Pugh category is sometimes incorporated into product restrictions but does not provide a universal fractional dose. Safe use requires agent-specific guidance, clinical assessment and monitoring.

6 · Pharmacodynamic sensitivity in frailty

Even at an unchanged plasma concentration, receptor responses and compensatory physiology can differ in frailty. Baroreflex impairment magnifies orthostatic hypotension; CNS vulnerability magnifies sedative burden; reduced cholinergic reserve makes delirium more likely with antimuscarinic medicines. Multiple modest effects can add together to produce a fall or loss of independence. Prescribing decisions must consider frailty, cognition, functional goals, caregiver capacity, hydration and practical adherence—not only a laboratory result.

7 · Drug–drug interactions: mechanism before memorisation

A pharmacokinetic inhibitor reduces metabolism or transport of a substrate, potentially raising exposure; an inducer increases expression of relevant enzymes and may reduce exposure, sometimes persisting after withdrawal. A pharmacodynamic interaction can arise without changing concentrations: NSAID plus anticoagulant increases bleeding, and opioids plus benzodiazepines depress respiratory drive. Assess the magnitude, therapeutic window, alternatives, patient susceptibility and possibility of planned monitoring. Avoid generic rules that every CYP interaction requires the same dose change.

8 · Therapeutic index, monitoring and sample timing

Therapeutic drug monitoring is useful for certain drugs with narrow margins, variable kinetics or concentration–response relationships, but only when the sample has interpretable timing and a clinically appropriate target. A lithium concentration, an aminoglycoside level and a phenytoin concentration are interpreted differently; albumin, renal function, sampling time and formulation can alter meaning. Adjust clinical care to the patient’s signs and symptoms rather than treating a lab number in isolation. A monitoring plan should specify who orders, who reviews, who acts and what to do if the result is missed.

9 · Competing risks: harm from treatment versus harm from non-treatment

Real prescribing is a choice under uncertainty. An ACE inhibitor during pregnancy can pose fetal risk, but severe maternal disease also requires effective alternative treatment. Withholding every potentially nephrotoxic medicine in CKD may prevent beneficial evidence-based therapy, while continuing one during rapidly deteriorating AKI could be dangerous. In frailty, reducing sedatives may improve function, while abrupt discontinuation could cause withdrawal. Make the expected absolute benefit, likely harms, time to benefit and patient preference explicit.

10 · Integrated prescribing maths and communication

Dimensional analysis catches errors: (mg/kg/dose) × (kg) = mg/dose; (mg/dose) ÷ (mg/mL) = mL/dose. For a fictional 18-kg patient at an authorised 10 mg/kg dose, 180 mg is the arithmetic result. If the stock is 100 mg/5 mL, concentration is 20 mg/mL and volume 9 mL. This is a training example, not an endorsed regimen. In an OSCE, always state that you would confirm age-specific dose caps, formulation, contraindications, double-check and documentation. Communication completes the calculation: ask the patient/carer to repeat the regimen and warning signs.

11 · What an excellent PLAB prescription answer looks like

The strongest answer identifies the immediate danger (e.g., respiratory depression, hyperkalaemia, possible serious drug error), chooses safe initial stabilisation, states the population-specific contraindication or monitoring change, explains how drug-specific guidance is checked, and closes the loop with documentation and reassessment. “Use the BNF” alone is incomplete unless you say which dosing issue needs checking and how you keep the patient safe while seeking advice. An answer that starts with the correct drug but ignores the patient’s pregnancy, renal function or medication list is not good prescribing.

In 60 seconds · ten rules that protect patients

  • Indication first. Define the diagnosis, intended benefit, alternatives, timescale, and measurable endpoint before prescribing.
  • Reconcile what is actually taken. Include OTC drugs, supplements, doses, last administrations, and reactions rather than copying an old list.
  • Children are not small adults. Check weight, age, formulation, mg/kg calculation, maximum dose and a BNF for Children reference.
  • Pregnancy demands two risk assessments. Weigh fetal exposure against the maternal and fetal risk of leaving disease untreated; seek UKTIS/BNF advice.
  • Frailty changes sensitivity. Reduced reserve, orthostatic hypotension, anticholinergic burden and falls may matter more than chronological age.
  • Renal function is method- and time-dependent. eGFR is not automatically the right dosing metric; use Cockcroft–Gault CrCl when specified (notably for DOACs).
  • In AKI, creatinine lags behind physiology. A formula calculated from unstable creatinine may overestimate current clearance.
  • High-risk medicines need a monitoring owner. Define baseline tests, repeat interval, toxicity symptoms, action thresholds and review date.
  • Stop carefully. Abrupt withdrawal of long-term steroids, benzodiazepines or some other medicines can cause severe harm.
  • Explain and document. Shared decisions, a clear prescription, safety-netting and a handover complete the pharmacology.

1 · A universal prescription check: indication to monitoring

Step 1 — identify the person and problem. Confirm identifiers, diagnosis, treatment objective and urgency. Is the symptom a drug adverse effect rather than a new disease? Consider severity, comorbidities, prognosis, allergy history (reaction and timing), prior response, capacity and what matters to the patient. A drug is not “safe” in isolation: it is safe enough for this person, this purpose, this period and this monitoring system.

Step 2 — build an accurate medication history. Prescriptions, discharge letters and electronic records can disagree with what is taken. Ask about pharmacies, medication packs, missed doses, injections, inhalers, OTC NSAIDs, herbal products, alcohol and pregnancy possibility where relevant. Document exact strength, formulation, route, frequency, indication, last dose and adverse reactions. NICE NG5 recommends medicines reconciliation on acute admission within 24 hours or sooner when necessary, then again at transfers and discharge.

Step 3 — choose and communicate. Check efficacy, first-line recommendation, interactions, organ function, dosing metric, duplicate therapy, high-risk combinations, formulation and adherence. Write unambiguous units and directions. Agree an initiation and monitoring plan: baseline bloods, change in symptoms or vital signs, reviews, red-flag toxicity and who acts on abnormal results. Shared decision-making is a clinical safety intervention, not an administrative extra.

2 · Paediatric pharmacology: age, weight and formulation

Development changes both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Neonates have immature renal filtration and tubular handling, age-dependent hepatic enzymes, different body-water/fat proportions and altered plasma protein binding. In early life, these processes mature at different rates. Therefore two patients with the same weight may require different dosing because postnatal age, gestation, diagnosis or organ function differs.

Weight-based arithmetic is a verification step, never the whole prescription. For a fictional calculation, if the authorised regimen is 10 mg/kg per dose and a child weighs 18 kg, the arithmetic gives 180 mg per dose. Before prescribing, independently confirm that the source states per-dose rather than per-day, the maximum single and daily dose, concentration (mg/mL), indication, interval, age band, route and any renal modification. A 100 mg/5 mL liquid contains 20 mg/mL; 180 mg would represent 9 mL. These numbers illustrate units only and are not a recommended drug regimen.

Prevent tenfold errors. Record a recent measured weight in kg, avoid confusing lb and kg, use standard concentrations and oral syringes, and have another trained clinician verify high-risk paediatric calculations. Never round to an unmeasurable volume or infer that an adult tablet may safely be crushed. Parental/carer teach-back should include how much, how often, why, what to avoid and when to seek help. Use the BNF for Children and neonatal service protocols rather than extrapolating adult information.

3 · Pregnancy: treat the mother while protecting fetal development

Avoid the false choice between “no medication” and “risk to the baby.” Untreated epilepsy, infection, severe hypertension, diabetes or psychiatric disease can harm parent and fetus. Exposure risk depends on drug, dose, timing, placental transfer and maternal disease control. The organogenesis period carries particular structural teratogenic concern; late-pregnancy exposure may cause neonatal toxicity, withdrawal or ductal/renal effects with some medicines.

Examples of clinically important cautions: ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated in pregnancy because of fetal toxicity concerns; methotrexate is teratogenic; valproate carries serious reproductive risks and stringent UK controls; NSAIDs have gestation-dependent fetal risks, notably later in pregnancy. Do not generalise that every medicine is harmful, and do not stop essential antiseizure therapy abruptly without urgent specialist advice. For contraception, preconception planning and antiepileptics, check current MHRA measures rather than relying on old exam tables.

UK workflow: confirm pregnancy status and gestation when relevant; clarify indication, severity and alternative treatments; consult BNF/UKTIS (including Bumps patient information) and the relevant obstetric or specialist team; explain known, uncertain and untreated-disease risks; document a shared plan and follow-up. A pregnancy alert is a prompt to assess, not a reason to deny lifesaving therapy.

4 · Breastfeeding, reproductive potential and contraception

Breast milk exposure is not synonymous with infant harm. Infant dose depends on maternal level, milk transfer, timing, oral bioavailability, infant age, prematurity and clinical condition. Some drugs are compatible with breastfeeding; others require alternative treatment, infant monitoring or temporary interruption. Use current SPS breastfeeding advice/BNF and specialist information rather than a blanket rule.

Contraception interactions require mechanism-specific advice. Enzyme-inducing antiseizure medicines and rifampicin-like strong inducers can reduce exposure to certain hormonal contraceptives; the degree and options differ by method. Discuss long-acting methods unaffected by relevant interactions when appropriate and arrange sexual-health/pharmacy advice. Some medicines require highly effective contraception and documented pregnancy-prevention measures.

Valproate in the UK: current MHRA measures restrict initiation in patients under 55 and require specialist oversight; controls differ according to age, sex, treatment status and pregnancy potential. People taking valproate must not abruptly stop their treatment because of seizure or relapse risk; contact the responsible specialist for an individualised review.

5 · Older adults, frailty and medication-related harm

Frailty represents reduced physiological reserve, not simply a birthday. With lower lean mass, serum creatinine may appear reassuring despite impaired renal function. Pharmacodynamic sensitivity to benzodiazepines, opioids, antihypertensives and anticholinergics may cause sedation, delirium, constipation, urinary retention, orthostatic symptoms and falls. Prioritise function, independence, goals of care and the patient’s treatment burden.

Medication review is active clinical reasoning. Ask of every medicine: “Why is it still prescribed? What outcome has it produced? What harms are occurring? What monitoring is overdue? Is the expected time to benefit consistent with the person’s priorities and prognosis?” Review anticholinergic load, sedatives, duplicate therapy, NSAIDs, antihypertensives and complex insulin regimens. A tool such as STOPP/START can help identify potentially inappropriate prescriptions or omitted treatments, but it does not replace judgement.

Examples: a patient with repeated falls and morning confusion taking zopiclone, oxybutynin and an opioid merits assessment for cumulative central and anticholinergic effects; a person with postural hypotension may need blood pressure targets and therapy revised; stopping an essential medicine without assessing the indication can also cause harm. Plan one or a few changes at a time, with monitoring and patient agreement.

6 · Renal impairment: eGFR, CrCl and unstable creatinine

Two commonly reported measures are not interchangeable. Lab eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) is standardised to body surface area and useful for CKD classification. Cockcroft–Gault estimates creatinine clearance (CrCl, mL/min) using age, sex, body weight and creatinine; some product dosing instructions, particularly adult DOACs, require CrCl by this method. For medicines other than DOACs, follow the specific product/BNF dosing guidance and seek pharmacy advice if the patient is unusually small, large or frail.

AKI breaks assumptions. Creatinine reflects a delayed steady-state marker. In abrupt dehydration, sepsis or rapidly falling filtration, a mathematically normal eGFR or CrCl may overstate present renal drug clearance. Examine urine output, fluid status, potassium, trends and likely reversibility. Consider temporarily withholding or modifying a potentially harmful medicine under local sick-day and acute-illness guidance, but never automatically withdraw essential treatment without considering the indication and hazards of interruption.

High-yield examples: lithium and digoxin can accumulate when renal clearance falls; aminoglycosides require drug-level-informed dosing; metformin use depends on renal function and clinical circumstances such as severe acute illness; DOAC suitability and dosing require the authorised CrCl calculation; ACE inhibitors/ARBs may require renal and potassium monitoring but are often kidney-protective when properly indicated. Ask a clinical pharmacist where dosing, dialysis and toxicity are complex.

7 · Hepatic dysfunction and altered drug handling

“Abnormal LFTs” is not a complete hepatic dose adjustment. The liver affects metabolism, first-pass extraction, albumin binding, bile excretion and clotting-factor synthesis. Advanced cirrhosis can alter hepatic blood flow and enzyme activity, increase free drug concentration and worsen encephalopathy. An isolated ALT elevation, cholestasis and decompensated cirrhosis have different implications.

Assess the phenotype. Consider bilirubin, albumin, INR (interpreted in context), ascites, encephalopathy, portal hypertension, comorbid renal impairment and active bleeding. Product information may use Child–Pugh categories for particular medicines, but these scores were not invented as universal drug clearance equations. Where a medicine has substantial hepatic metabolism, a narrow therapeutic index or sedation potential, check drug-specific guidance and monitor closely.

Illustrative risks: sedatives may precipitate encephalopathy; NSAIDs in decompensated cirrhosis can worsen kidney injury and bleeding risk; anticoagulant choice requires an individualised assessment of bleeding, thrombosis and drug-specific restrictions. The safest action is often to involve a senior clinician, hepatology team or pharmacist rather than guessing a “half dose”.

8 · Polypharmacy, interactions and prescription cascades

Polypharmacy may be appropriate: a patient with HFrEF often benefits from multiple complementary drug classes. The danger is problematic polypharmacy—unclear benefit, unsafe combinations, poor adherence, duplicate treatment or inconsistent monitoring. Interactions can be pharmacokinetic (enzyme induction/inhibition, absorption, transporters) or pharmacodynamic (additive bleeding, hypotension, sedation, hyperkalaemia).

Classic combinations to recognise: ACE inhibitor/ARB + diuretic + NSAID increases AKI risk in susceptible patients; anticoagulant + NSAID raises bleeding risk; opioid + benzodiazepine increases respiratory depression risk; multiple QT-prolonging medicines create additive arrhythmia risk in susceptible settings; lithium levels may rise with certain renal haemodynamic changes or interacting medicines. Do not apply blanket prohibitions without considering indication, degree of risk and monitoring.

A prescribing cascade occurs when a drug adverse effect is misdiagnosed as new illness, leading to another drug (for example, anticholinergic urinary retention treated as a primary bladder problem). Unwind the causal history, prioritise safety, check which changes are reversible and discuss each proposed deprescribing step with the patient.

9 · High-risk medicines: standardised monitoring and error prevention

Methotrexate for inflammatory disease is typically once weekly, not daily. The prescribing record should clearly state the day, dose, folic-acid plan and specialist monitoring; a daily-dose error can be fatal. Oral anticoagulants need indication-specific dosing, bleeding counselling, renal checks and adherence support. Insulin is prescribed in units with correct device and timing; never use ambiguous “U” abbreviations. Concentrated potassium and opioids require local high-risk safety procedures.

Monitoring is a closed loop, not merely an ordered test. Specify the indication, baseline investigations, repeat interval, review thresholds, named reviewer and plan when the result is abnormal. For lithium or other narrow-therapeutic-index drugs, timing of the sample, interactions, hydration and renal function affect interpretation. For biologics and immunosuppressants, screen for infections and follow the specific vaccination/monitoring programme.

When an error is discovered: protect the patient, assess exposure and symptoms, obtain senior/pharmacy advice, correct the immediate issue and arrange monitoring, inform the patient openly in line with professional duties and local duty of candour requirements, document and report through the appropriate safety system. Disclosure and learning are essential; do not alter records to hide an error.

10 · Deprescribing, withdrawal and medicines reconciliation at discharge

Deprescribing is planned treatment, not an abrupt cancellation. Rank medicines by risk, benefit, life expectancy, symptom control, withdrawal hazards and patient priorities. Steroids used long term can suppress the HPA axis; benzodiazepines, opioids, antidepressants and gabapentinoids can cause withdrawal symptoms in relevant contexts. NICE NG215 supports collaborative, individualised reductions for dependence-forming medicines and antidepressants, avoiding abrupt cessation except exceptional safety circumstances.

Discharge is a particularly dangerous interface. Reconcile preadmission, inpatient and new discharge medicines; identify what was stopped and why; document the final list, dose/formulation, newly introduced monitoring, last administration and follow-up owner. Explain changes directly with the patient and, where appropriate, carers and the community pharmacy. Ensure high-risk results and pending tests are handed over reliably.

Ask the teach-back question: “To check I explained it clearly, how will you take this medicine and what would make you call us?” This detects misunderstanding without blaming the patient. Provide accessible written directions, interpreter support, and reasonable adjustments where needed.

UK prescribing principle: The GMC advises prescribing only when you have adequate knowledge of the patient and are satisfied the proposed medicine serves their needs. Verify information and share relevant details across care settings; the electronic record is a starting point, not a substitute for a conversation.

11 · Integrated PLAB case strategy: choose the safest next step

Clinical SBA sequence: identify the immediate threat → confirm the key clinical facts → decide whether action must precede investigation → select therapy suitable for the population and organ function → identify monitoring and counselling. An answer that is first-line in a healthy adult can be incorrect in pregnancy, neonates, renal failure or a patient with major drug interactions.

Common traps: reading “allergy” without checking reaction; assuming all renal dosing uses eGFR; prescribing from a single stable creatinine during evolving AKI; forgetting weight-based paediatric maximums; using a nonpregnant pathway in pregnancy; interrupting long-term steroids or antiseizure medication without specialist consideration; treating an adverse effect by adding another drug.

In PLAB 2: explain indication, meaningful benefit and uncertainty; actively invite the patient’s concerns; check pregnancy and medication use when relevant; discuss side effects, urgent red flags, monitoring and follow-up; safety-net in language the patient understands. If uncertain, acknowledge uncertainty and involve a senior or pharmacist rather than guessing.

Five integrated clinical cases · reason before revealing the plan

Case A · Toddler with a liquid prescription

A 2-year-old weighing 12 kg is prescribed an oral medicine. The junior doctor calculates mg/kg correctly, but the chosen bottle has twice the concentration used on the ward last month. The label says “5 mL” with no mg dose.

Reveal clinical approach

Clinical plan: Pause dispensing/administering and check indication, current weight, authorised mg/kg per dose, daily maximum and exact product concentration against the BNFC/local policy. Recalculate the administered mL with independent high-risk checking, specify both mg and mL in an unambiguous order and counsel carers using an oral syringe.

Exam pearl: The same number of millilitres is not the same dose when formulation strength changes.

Case B · Pregnancy and established epilepsy

A 25-year-old taking a long-term antiseizure medicine reports a positive pregnancy test and is frightened by internet warnings. Her seizures have been well controlled; she asks whether to stop today.

Reveal clinical approach

Clinical plan: Assess pregnancy timing, regimen and recent seizure history; explain that stopping abruptly can provoke seizures. Arrange urgent review with the specialist epilepsy/obstetric team and check pregnancy-specific drug guidance and current MHRA restrictions. Discuss known risks, safer alternatives where appropriate and an individualised plan without an abrupt unsupervised change.

Exam pearl: Maternal seizure control and fetal exposure are both clinically important. Valproate measures are particularly strict.

Case C · Frailty, falls and anticholinergic burden

An 84-year-old has fallen twice, is constipated and complains of blurred vision. Medicines include oxybutynin, a sedating antihistamine, nightly zopiclone and an opioid. Blood pressure is low standing.

Reveal clinical approach

Clinical plan: Exclude injury and acute illness, review the indication and current benefit of each medicine, check cognition, postural BP, renal function and potential interactions. Discuss stepwise reduction or alternatives, particularly sedative and anticholinergic burden, with an individualised taper where needed; arrange falls assessment and follow-up.

Exam pearl: Avoid simply prescribing another drug for each adverse effect. Do not abruptly stop a chronic dependence-forming medicine.

Case D · Dehydration, AKI and interacting drugs

A 71-year-old with CKD takes ramipril, furosemide and lithium. After diarrhoea she develops tremor, unsteady gait, oliguria and a rising creatinine. She asks whether to take her usual tablets.

Reveal clinical approach

Clinical plan: Urgent same-day medical assessment for possible lithium toxicity, AKI and dehydration. Assess ABCDE, ECG, fluid and neurological status; obtain kidney function/electrolytes and correctly timed lithium levels as directed, seek senior/toxicology advice. Withhold or adjust implicated medicines under a supervised acute plan and monitor serially; do not rely on an automatically reported eGFR in active AKI.

Exam pearl: A normal or near-normal single lithium result cannot always exclude clinically important chronic toxicity; use history and serial clinical assessment.

Case E · Methotrexate discharge discrepancy

A patient with rheumatoid arthritis normally takes methotrexate weekly. The discharge list now says “10 mg daily”. There is no documented reason for the change.

Reveal clinical approach

Clinical plan: Treat this as a potentially serious medication error. Prevent further incorrect doses, clarify what was actually administered, immediately contact the prescriber/specialist pharmacist, assess for toxicity and obtain indicated blood tests; correct the prescription, explain the error openly and document/incident-report. Confirm the weekly day, folic acid regimen and monitoring handover.

Exam pearl: Never assume a daily methotrexate order in non-oncology inflammatory disease is intentional.

Communicate any changes to the GP and pharmacist.

    NICE / BNF · Four safety-first clinical pathways

    These are principles for a PLAB decision, not an individual treatment order. Review current medicine-specific BNF/BNFC, NICE NG5, NHS SPS renal advice and specialist guidance for exact eligibility, doses and monitoring.

    Safe prescribing from presentation to follow-up

    1. Verify

    Confirm patient, diagnosis, expected benefit, urgency, prior response, allergies and true current medicines; address immediate danger first.

    ↓
    2. Individualise

    Check age/weight, pregnancy, breastfeeding, frailty, kidney and liver status; review dose metric, formulation, interactions and alternatives.

    ↓
    3. Write clearly

    Document generic drug, strength, route, dose in unambiguous units, frequency, indication, duration/review and PRN maximum if needed.

    ↓
    4. Close the loop

    Explain benefit/risks, arrange baseline and repeat monitoring with an owner, provide written directions, record consent and safety-net.

    Prescribing for a child or during pregnancy

    1. Identify subgroup

    For a child: accurate kg weight, age and developmental stage. For pregnancy: gestation, disease severity, planned treatment and fetal risk.

    ↓
    2. Consult source

    Child: BNFC/neonatal protocol; independently confirm mg/kg and maximum doses. Pregnancy: BNF, UKTIS/Bumps and relevant obstetric/specialist guidance.

    ↓
    3. Choose and calculate

    Confirm a suitable drug and formulation; check interactions, licensed status, concentration and appropriate monitoring. Do not abruptly stop essential treatment.

    ↓
    4. Explain and document

    Agree a shared plan with patient/carer, clarify exact administration, monitor parent/infant/child as indicated and arrange follow-up.

    Prescribing in renal or hepatic dysfunction

    1. Recognise acuity

    Determine stable CKD versus AKI; assess volume, urine output, renal trend and indications. In liver disease evaluate synthetic failure and encephalopathy.

    ↓
    2. Use the correct rule

    For DOAC dosing use Cockcroft–Gault CrCl as instructed; for other medicines use agent-specific BNF/product guidance. Avoid blind equations during evolving AKI.

    ↓
    3. Review exposures

    Check narrow-therapeutic-index drugs, active metabolites, nephrotoxins, hepatically metabolised sedatives and interacting combinations.

    ↓
    4. Reassess

    Record dose/interval decision, repeat renal or liver tests and drug levels where indicated, identify who acts on results, and re-evaluate on recovery.

    Frailty, polypharmacy and safe deprescribing

    1. Establish priorities

    Ask about function, falls, cognition, treatment burden, life expectancy, adherence, patient goals and all medicines including OTC.

    ↓
    2. Balance risk

    For each item assess indication, measured benefit, time to benefit, ADRs, anticholinergic/sedative burden, interactions and omitted treatments.

    ↓
    3. Plan changes

    Reduce unnecessary medicines with shared agreement; taper dependence-forming or HPA-axis-suppressing drugs when indicated rather than stopping reflexively.

    ↓
    4. Communicate

    Give a reconciled current list, review date, monitoring and clear responsibility across GP, pharmacy, carers and specialist services.

    Clinical guardrails: Pregnancy and renal disease do not mandate automatic medication withdrawal. Do not guess paediatric doses, use eGFR for every renal dosing decision, or stop long-term steroids or antiseizure medicines abruptly. Escalate when uncertain.
