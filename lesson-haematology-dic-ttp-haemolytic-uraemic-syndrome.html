DIC, TTP & Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome

Emergency rule: Suspected TTP, rapidly progressive HUS or bleeding DIC requires urgent hospital and specialist management.

Overview

DIC, TTP and HUS can cause thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction. TTP and HUS are thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs); DIC is systemic activation of coagulation with consumption of platelets and clotting factors.

PLAB application: Recognise all three as potentially life-threatening.

Immediate ABCDE

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, consciousness and bleeding; establish IV access, monitor observations, seek senior and haematology input.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency escalation for a complete diagnostic panel.

DIC pathophysiology

Widespread coagulation activation causes microvascular fibrin deposition and consumption of platelets and coagulation factors, leading to thrombosis and bleeding.

PLAB application: DIC is secondary to another process, not a standalone disease.

DIC triggers

Sepsis, major trauma, obstetric emergencies, malignancy (especially acute promyelocytic leukaemia) and severe tissue injury can precipitate DIC.

PLAB application: Identify and treat the trigger urgently.

DIC clinical clues

Oozing from cannula sites, bruising, mucosal bleeding, organ failure, acral ischaemia or thrombosis may occur.

PLAB application: DIC can be predominantly thrombotic or haemorrhagic.

DIC investigations

Typically low platelets, prolonged PT and often APTT, raised D-dimer and low or falling fibrinogen; trends and clinical context are essential.

PLAB application: A normal fibrinogen early in inflammatory DIC does not exclude it.

DIC management

Treat the cause, provide organ support and discuss blood components with haematology. Platelets, FFP and cryoprecipitate are guided by bleeding, procedures and results rather than laboratory numbers alone.

PLAB application: Do not give prophylactic blood components reflexively.

TMA and MAHA

Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) results from red-cell fragmentation in small vessels; look for schistocytes, raised LDH, indirect bilirubin and low haptoglobin.

PLAB application: MAHA plus thrombocytopenia warrants urgent assessment.

TTP mechanism

Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency allows ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers to promote platelet-rich microthrombi. Acquired immune TTP is most common in adults.

PLAB application: TTP is an emergency even without the classic pentad.

TTP presentation

Thrombocytopenia and MAHA with neurological changes, abdominal pain, cardiac injury or renal dysfunction. Fever may be absent.

PLAB application: Do not require all five classic features to suspect TTP.

TTP tests

FBC, blood film, haemolysis markers, renal profile, coagulation tests, troponin and ADAMTS13 activity/inhibitor testing. PT/APTT are often relatively normal.

PLAB application: Draw ADAMTS13 samples before plasma therapy if feasible, without delaying treatment.

TTP emergency treatment

Urgent specialist-directed therapeutic plasma exchange and immunosuppression are central; caplacizumab may be used for acquired TTP under specialist protocols.

PLAB application: Do not await ADAMTS13 results before treating strongly suspected TTP.

HUS patterns

Shiga-toxin-associated HUS often follows bloody diarrhoea and causes MAHA, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury. Complement-mediated HUS may present without diarrhoea.

PLAB application: Assess hydration, blood pressure and renal function carefully.

HUS assessment and treatment

Send stool testing for Shiga toxin when indicated, monitor renal function and electrolytes, provide careful fluid and renal support; dialysis may be required. Complement-mediated HUS needs urgent specialist evaluation and may require complement inhibition.

PLAB application: Avoid assuming antibiotics or antimotility agents are appropriate in suspected STEC infection.

DIC versus TTP versus HUS

DIC commonly deranges PT/APTT and fibrinogen; TTP and HUS usually have MAHA and thrombocytopenia with relatively preserved clotting tests. TTP typically has severe ADAMTS13 deficiency; HUS often prominent renal injury.

PLAB application: Patterns guide urgency but are not definitive alone.

PLAB 2 communication

Explain suspected microvascular clotting and blood-cell destruction simply; ask about sepsis, diarrhoea, pregnancy, medicines, bruising, bleeding and neurological symptoms; discuss urgent hospital treatment.

PLAB application: Safety-net for confusion, reduced urine output, chest pain, breathlessness or bleeding.

PLAB 2 — Suspected TMA consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent. Assess bleeding, bruising, diarrhoea, infection, new drugs, pregnancy, confusion, chest pain and urine output. Explain urgent blood tests and the need for immediate specialist care; check understanding and communicate empathetically.

Deep Dive

Why DIC and TTP differ

DIC activates the coagulation cascade and consumes clotting factors, often prolonging PT/APTT. TTP primarily forms platelet-rich microthrombi due to ADAMTS13 deficiency, often leaving routine coagulation tests relatively normal.

Interpreting a blood film

Schistocytes support MAHA, but interpretation needs the clinical context and specialist microscopy. Raised LDH and low haptoglobin support haemolysis; reticulocyte responses vary.

Why TTP treatment cannot wait

Untreated TTP can cause rapid fatal organ ischaemia. Send ADAMTS13 before treatment when practical, but a high clinical suspicion requires immediate plasma-exchange pathway activation.

HUS and infection

STEC-HUS commonly follows gastroenteritis, sometimes bloody. Management is primarily supportive, including careful fluid and renal care. Antibiotic and antimotility decisions in suspected STEC infection require current specialist guidance.

Transfusion nuance

DIC blood components depend on bleeding, procedures and laboratory findings. In TTP, routine platelet transfusion is generally avoided because of thrombosis concern, unless severe bleeding or specialist-directed need.

Diagnostic overlap

Other TMAs include pregnancy-related syndromes, severe hypertension, medication-related TMA and transplant-associated TMA. Do not assume every schistocyte-positive sample is TTP.
