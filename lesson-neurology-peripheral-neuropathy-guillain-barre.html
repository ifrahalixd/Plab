Peripheral Neuropathy & Guillain–Barré Syndrome (GBS)

PLAB priority: Rapidly progressive weakness with reduced reflexes is a neurological emergency. Assess breathing and swallowing, admit urgently and involve specialists.

01. Peripheral nervous system overview

Peripheral neuropathy is damage to peripheral nerves. Classify by distribution (distal symmetric, focal, multifocal), fibre type (motor, sensory, autonomic), and time course (acute, subacute, chronic).

PLAB application: Localise before ordering investigations; acute progressive weakness can be an emergency.

02. Patterns of neuropathy

Length-dependent polyneuropathy usually starts with symmetrical numbness or tingling in feet, later hands (stocking-glove). Mononeuropathy affects one nerve; radiculopathy follows a root distribution.

PLAB application: Distinguish peripheral nerve, nerve root and central nervous system patterns.

03. Common causes

Diabetes, alcohol, vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, renal disease, medications including chemotherapy, hereditary disease and inflammatory neuropathies are important causes.

PLAB application: Ask about diabetes, diet, medicines, alcohol, family history and systemic symptoms.

04. Peripheral nerve examination

Inspect for wasting, deformity, foot ulcers and skin changes. Test tone, power, reflexes, pinprick, light touch, vibration, proprioception and gait. Distal reflexes, especially ankle jerks, may be reduced.

PLAB application: Check foot sensation and protective sensation; document asymmetry and proximal weakness.

05. Guillain–Barré syndrome: presentation

GBS is an acute immune-mediated polyradiculoneuropathy, often following respiratory or gastrointestinal infection. It commonly causes progressive, relatively symmetric limb weakness with diminished or absent reflexes, typically evolving over days to 4 weeks.

PLAB application: Progressive weakness plus hyporeflexia warrants immediate hospital assessment.

06. GBS danger signs

Respiratory muscle weakness, bulbar dysfunction, impaired cough, rapid progression and autonomic instability can be life-threatening. Pulse oximetry alone does not reliably exclude impending ventilatory failure.

PLAB application: Urgently involve neurology and critical care for breathing, swallowing or autonomic concerns.

07. GBS variants

Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP) is common; axonal forms (AMAN/AMSAN) occur. Miller Fisher syndrome classically causes ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia.

PLAB application: Recognise that GBS may not present with the classic ascending pattern.

08. GBS investigations

Diagnosis is primarily clinical. Nerve conduction studies/EMG support subtype and may be normal early. Lumbar puncture may show raised CSF protein with normal or low cell count (albuminocytologic dissociation), often absent in the first week.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency treatment while waiting for LP or nerve conduction studies.

09. GBS initial management

Admit urgently for repeated respiratory assessment including forced vital capacity where feasible, bulbar evaluation, ECG and autonomic monitoring. Arrange specialist-led escalation and VTE prevention as appropriate.

PLAB application: Check breathing, cough, swallowing, heart rate and blood pressure repeatedly.

10. GBS disease-modifying treatment

IV immunoglobulin or plasma exchange improves recovery in appropriately selected patients; treatment choice depends on severity, timing and specialist guidance. Corticosteroids alone are not effective treatment for typical GBS.

PLAB application: Refer urgently for specialist IVIg or plasma exchange; do not prescribe routine steroids for GBS.

11. GBS supportive care

Manage pain, nutrition, skin care, bladder/bowel problems, thrombosis risk and rehabilitation. Neuropathic pain may need specific treatment; psychological support is important.

PLAB application: Prevent avoidable complications during prolonged immobility.

12. Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy

CIDP typically progresses or relapses over more than 8 weeks, with proximal and distal weakness, sensory changes and reduced reflexes. Specialist assessment and neurophysiology guide diagnosis.

PLAB application: GBS progresses over days to weeks; consider CIDP if deterioration continues beyond 8 weeks.

13. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy

Distal symmetric sensory neuropathy may cause burning pain, numbness, loss of vibration and absent ankle reflexes. Assess feet, ulcer risk and glycaemic control; investigate other possible causes when indicated.

PLAB application: A painless foot ulcer may reflect loss of protective sensation.

14. Other neuropathy syndromes

Carpal tunnel syndrome affects median nerve sensation and thenar strength; ulnar neuropathy affects ring/little fingers; common fibular (peroneal) neuropathy can cause foot drop. B12 deficiency may involve spinal cord pathways as well as peripheral nerves.

PLAB application: Use sensory territory and reflex pattern to localise lesions.

15. Initial tests and referral

For persistent polyneuropathy consider HbA1c/glucose, B12, thyroid function, renal function, FBC and other tests guided by history. Rapid progression, significant weakness, asymmetry or systemic red flags need prompt referral.

PLAB application: Do not attribute every neuropathy to diabetes without evaluating the clinical pattern.

16. PLAB 2 communication and safety-netting

Explain the suspected nerve problem in plain language. In GBS, describe need for hospital monitoring because breathing and heart function can be affected. In chronic neuropathy discuss foot care, falls prevention and follow-up.

PLAB application: New rapid weakness, difficulty breathing, swallowing or walking requires emergency assessment.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Immune-mediated nerve injury

In GBS, post-infectious immune responses damage peripheral nerve myelin or axons. Conduction failure produces weakness and loss of reflexes.

Why breathing can fail

Diaphragmatic and accessory respiratory muscle weakness may progress before oxygen saturation falls. Repeated clinical assessment and respiratory mechanics are essential.

Demyelinating versus axonal GBS

AIDP involves demyelination; AMAN and AMSAN are axonal variants. Nerve conduction studies help characterise patterns but may be nondiagnostic early.

Why CSF can be normal initially

CSF protein elevation may take time to develop. A normal early lumbar puncture does not exclude GBS, and marked pleocytosis prompts consideration of alternatives.

GBS versus CIDP

GBS generally reaches maximum weakness within 4 weeks; CIDP usually progresses or relapses beyond 8 weeks. Specialist reassessment is required for atypical courses.

Neuropathy localisation

Length-dependent polyneuropathy affects distal sensory axons; focal entrapment follows named nerve territories, while radiculopathy follows root patterns and may include back/neck pain.
