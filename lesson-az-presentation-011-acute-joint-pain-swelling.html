Acute joint pain and swelling: exclude septic arthritis first

A hot, swollen, painful joint may be infected, crystal-related, traumatic, autoimmune or degenerative. Septic arthritis is a medical and surgical emergency: cartilage damage, joint destruction and sepsis can develop rapidly. A patient may be afebrile; normal early inflammatory markers do not safely exclude infection.

Article audio
Never miss: suspected septic arthritis, prosthetic joint infection, septic shock, neurovascular injury, compartment syndrome, and fracture/dislocation. Finding urate or CPP crystals does not exclude a simultaneous joint infection. Do not inject intra-articular corticosteroid into a possibly infected joint.

1. A systematic diagnostic framework

Start with stability and dangerous diagnoses. An acutely unwell patient needs ABCDE, observations, immediate senior review and a sepsis assessment. In a stable patient, establish whether the pain is articular (pain on passive and active movement, effusion, joint-line tenderness) or periarticular (tendon or bursa tenderness, usually more pain on active/resisted movement).

3. Examination: the hot-joint examination

Look: asymmetry, effusion, erythema (which may be less obvious in darker skin), wounds, scars, psoriatic plaques, tophi and deformity. Feel: temperature, tenderness at joint line or adjacent bursa, effusion. Move: assess active and gentle passive range; marked pain with passive movement suggests intra-articular inflammation. Function: walking or weight-bearing; document distal sensation, pulses and capillary refill after injury.

Beyond the joint: examine skin and nails (psoriasis), eyes (uveitis or conjunctivitis), other joints, cardiovascular system if bacteraemia/endocarditis is plausible, and look for a focal infectious source. Avoid repeatedly forcing a very painful joint through movement.

5. Investigations: identify the question first

Joint aspiration (arthrocentesis) is pivotal when septic arthritis is suspected. Obtain synovial fluid for cell count and differential, Gram stain, culture and sensitivity, and polarised crystal microscopy, with additional testing directed by microbiology or rheumatology. A high neutrophil count supports inflammation but does not distinguish infection reliably. Negative Gram stain or identified crystals cannot safely exclude infection. Ultrasound may identify or guide aspiration of deeper joints such as hip.

Send FBC, CRP/ESR, renal function, glucose and other tests as appropriate. Take blood cultures when infection is suspected; if stable, collect appropriate samples before antibiotics without creating dangerous delay. If septic or unstable, start urgent empiric IV antibiotics according to local sepsis and microbiology policy even if sampling is delayed. Serum urate can be checked, but repeat after the flare if the result is misleading. RF/anti-CCP support evaluation of persistent synovitis, not diagnosis by themselves.

Imaging: radiographs after injury or to evaluate fracture, degeneration or chondrocalcinosis; ultrasound for effusion, aspiration guidance and periarticular pathology; MRI for suspected osteomyelitis, occult injury or difficult cases under specialist advice. Imaging does not replace joint aspiration where infection is suspected.

6. Management by urgency and diagnosis

Suspected septic arthritis

Admit for urgent orthopaedic/rheumatology and microbiology input. Perform sepsis assessment, cultures and joint aspirate when safe, and begin appropriate empiric IV antibiotics promptly. Joint washout or repeated drainage may be needed; tailor antibiotics to organism and clinical response. Do not delay life-saving antibiotics in shock. Do not give intra-articular steroid until infection has been excluded.

Acute gout

After evaluating for infection, NICE NG219 supports a choice of NSAID, colchicine or short-course oral corticosteroid, considering renal function, gastrointestinal bleeding risk, cardiovascular disease, interactions and patient preference; provide suitable gastroprotection with NSAIDs when indicated. Ice and rest can help. For long-term prevention, discuss urate-lowering therapy when indicated, using treat-to-target serum urate monitoring and flare prophylaxis during initiation. New urate-lowering therapy is not emergency analgesia.

CPPD and inflammatory arthritis

CPPD flares may respond to NSAIDs, colchicine or corticosteroids after infection is excluded, with safety assessment. Persistent inflammatory synovitis should trigger prompt rheumatology referral; NICE NG100 advises referral even when inflammatory markers or RF/anti-CCP are normal. Early disease-modifying therapy is specialist-led.

Trauma, haemarthrosis and mechanical causes

Immobilise appropriately, provide analgesia, assess neurovascular status and image where needed. An acutely swollen joint after trauma may represent fracture, ligament injury or haemarthrosis. Significant bleeding, dislocation or neurovascular impairment needs urgent orthopaedics. Individualise anticoagulation decisions rather than stopping medicines automatically.

7. Special populations and complications

Children: fever, refusal to bear weight, limited hip motion or an irritable child may have septic arthritis or osteomyelitis; do not assume transient synovitis. Prosthetic joint: new painful swelling, erythema, wound drainage or systemic illness needs urgent prosthetic-joint infection evaluation. Pregnancy: medication and imaging selection differ; consider specialist advice. Immunocompromised adults: infection may have subtle systemic signs.

Escalate immediately for suspected septic shock, rapidly progressive pain, neurovascular deficits, tense compartment, widespread purpura or unexplained severe pain after recent surgery or procedure.

8. Worked clinical cases

Case A — Hot knee: A 71-year-old with diabetes has 24 hours of knee swelling and cannot flex the joint. Temperature 37.0°C. Reasoning: afebrile status does not exclude septic arthritis; arrange urgent joint aspiration, blood tests and specialist assessment.
Case B — First MTP: A 52-year-old has sudden nocturnal severe first-toe pain after a previous similar episode. Reasoning: gout is likely, but check for infection risk and atypical findings before choosing treatment; weigh CKD and drug interactions.
Case C — Polyarthritis: A 34-year-old has six weeks of swollen MCP/PIP joints and 90 minutes of morning stiffness. Reasoning: suspected RA; urgently refer for specialist assessment; do not defer referral pending RF results.
Case D — Prosthetic hip: A 75-year-old develops acute hip pain, inability to bear weight and fever four months after arthroplasty. Reasoning: urgent assessment for prosthetic joint infection; specialist sampling and treatment.
Case E — Post-diarrhoeal ankle: A 27-year-old develops asymmetric ankle swelling after a gastrointestinal illness, with eye redness. Reasoning: reactive arthritis is possible; assess for active joint infection, STI risks and uveitis red flags.

9. PLAB 2 OSCE: explain and safety-net

Introduce yourself, obtain consent and assess pain and vital signs. Explain: “A swollen joint can have several causes, including inflammation from crystals or infection. Infection can damage the joint quickly, so we need an urgent assessment and may take some joint fluid to test it.” Ask about antibiotic allergies, medicines and anticoagulants, explore the patient's concerns and give a clear handover using SBAR.

PLAB summary: hot monoarthritis = septic arthritis until suitably evaluated; crystals do not exclude infection; use aspiration and cultures wisely; treat sepsis promptly; refer persistent synovitis even with negative serology.

Bedside discrimination

Intra-articular: pain with passive movement, effusion and restricted motion. Periarticular: focal tendon/bursal tenderness, often preserved passive movement. Crystal and infection: may look identical on examination; aspirate when infection is a concern. Inflammatory polyarthritis: persistent swelling with prolonged stiffness requires early rheumatology referral.

Deep Dive: synovitis, crystals, infection and diagnostic errors

1. Why an infected joint deteriorates quickly

Normally synovial fluid lubricates cartilage. Bacterial invasion recruits neutrophils and increases proteolytic enzymes and cytokines; joint pressure rises, and cartilage can be damaged rapidly. The hip is especially difficult to examine and may need ultrasound-guided aspiration. A patient may lack fever, and inflammatory blood tests may be nondiagnostic early.

2. The crystal paradox

Monosodium urate crystals activate inflammasome-associated innate immune pathways and drive neutrophil recruitment. Calcium pyrophosphate crystals provoke a similar intense inflammatory reaction. Both can produce fever and raised CRP. Crystal detection supports the crystal diagnosis but cannot logically exclude a coexisting bacterial infection: the same fluid sample should be cultured when clinically indicated.

3. What synovial fluid can and cannot prove

Synovial leucocytes and neutrophil percentage generally rise in inflammatory and infective arthritis, but overlap is substantial. Gram stain is specific when positive but imperfectly sensitive. Culture remains central; negative cultures must be interpreted with prior antibiotics and the clinical picture. Sampling should be performed using appropriate asepsis and senior guidance, particularly near prosthetic joints.

4. Immunology of persistent inflammatory synovitis

RA involves autoimmunity and chronically activated synovium, which forms pannus and may erode cartilage and bone. ACPA (anti-CCP) can support diagnosis and prognosis, but seronegative RA exists. NICE advises specialist referral based on persistent suspected synovitis rather than waiting for antibody or CRP positivity.

5. Why monoarthritis differs from polyarthritis

A sudden single inflamed joint has a different pre-test probability profile from six weeks of symmetrical small-joint swelling. Distribution narrows the hypothesis but does not confirm the diagnosis. Polyarticular septic arthritis is possible, particularly in high-risk people.

6. Treatment trade-offs

NSAIDs relieve crystal and mechanical inflammation but can worsen kidney injury, fluid retention and GI bleeding. Colchicine has important renal and interaction considerations. Corticosteroids may mask infection; avoid intra-articular injection if septic arthritis has not been excluded. Antimicrobial choice for septic arthritis should reflect likely organisms, allergies, local resistance and culture results.

7. Five traps tested in PLAB

  1. Assuming absence of fever excludes septic arthritis.
  2. Calling an acute swollen knee gout because urate is high.
  3. Excluding infection after finding crystals.
  4. Delaying a suspected RA referral because RF is negative.
  5. Sending a child who refuses to weight-bear home with presumed transient synovitis without assessing for infection.
Integrate: identify threat → assess joint and patient → obtain relevant samples without delaying urgent treatment → match management to cause → safety-net and refer appropriately.
