Trauma Assessment: Primary Survey, Head Injury, Spinal Injury & Major Haemorrhage

Trauma priority: Control catastrophic bleeding, manage airway and breathing, recognise haemorrhagic shock, protect the brain and spine, and reassess continuously.

Trauma team preparation

Activate the major trauma pathway early, assign roles and prepare airway equipment, haemorrhage control, monitoring and blood products. Use structured handover with mechanism, injuries, signs and treatment.

PLAB application: Safety, PPE and clear team leadership come first.

The <C>ABCDE primary survey

In major trauma control catastrophic external bleeding before airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Identify and treat immediate threats during each step, then reassess.

PLAB application: The primary survey is an active treatment sequence, not just an examination.

Catastrophic haemorrhage control

Apply firm direct pressure and haemostatic dressings to compressible bleeding. Use a tourniquet for life-threatening limb haemorrhage when direct pressure is ineffective or impractical; record application time.

PLAB application: Never delay haemorrhage control while obtaining imaging.

Airway and cervical spine protection

Assess speech, obstruction, blood, vomit and facial injury; use jaw thrust and suction as indicated. Protect the spine while avoiding unnecessary prolonged immobilisation; expert airway support is essential when consciousness or ventilation is impaired.

PLAB application: Airway protection takes precedence over imaging.

Breathing and thoracic injuries

Check respiratory rate, saturation, chest movement, auscultation and signs of chest trauma. Immediately treat clinically suspected tension pneumothorax in an unstable patient; consider massive haemothorax and open pneumothorax.

PLAB application: Do not wait for chest X-ray in unstable tension pneumothorax.

Circulation and shock in trauma

Look for external and concealed haemorrhage in chest, abdomen, pelvis and long bones. Assess pulse, skin perfusion, mental state and blood pressure; establish IV/IO access and activate massive haemorrhage protocol when indicated.

PLAB application: A normal blood pressure does not rule out serious bleeding.

Pelvic fracture and limb haemorrhage

Suspect pelvic injury after high-energy trauma with pain, instability or shock. Apply a correctly positioned pelvic binder over the greater trochanters when indicated; avoid repeated pelvic springing. Splint long-bone fractures and reassess distal circulation.

PLAB application: A pelvic binder is not placed around the waist.

Damage-control resuscitation

Use local major haemorrhage protocol for balanced blood component therapy, warming, calcium monitoring and rapid definitive bleeding control. Avoid indiscriminate large-volume crystalloid. Consider tranexamic acid early in major trauma with active or suspected bleeding within recommended time windows.

PLAB application: Do not give TXA routinely after three hours from injury unless specialist indication.

Disability and neurological assessment

Record GCS components, pupils, limb movement, blood glucose and seizure activity. Look for lateralising signs and deterioration; treat hypoxia and hypotension promptly to reduce secondary brain injury.

PLAB application: Document GCS as E, V and M components, not just the total.

Head injury red flags

Reduced or falling GCS, focal deficit, seizure, signs of skull fracture, repeated vomiting and anticoagulant use raise concern for intracranial injury. Apply current NICE CT criteria and seek neurosurgical input when indicated.

PLAB application: A lucid interval does not exclude evolving intracranial bleeding.

Spinal trauma assessment

Assess midline pain, neurological deficit, mechanism, limb strength, sensation and sphincter symptoms. Use appropriate spinal movement restriction while prioritising ABCDE; follow validated imaging criteria and specialist advice.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary movement and document neurological findings before and after transfer.

Exposure and hypothermia prevention

Fully inspect for posterior injuries, penetrating wounds and hidden bleeding while maintaining dignity and temperature. Warm the environment, fluids and patient; hypothermia worsens coagulopathy.

PLAB application: Prevent heat loss throughout trauma resuscitation.

Secondary survey and AMPLE history

Once stabilised, perform head-to-toe examination including back, limbs, abdomen and neurovascular status. Obtain Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last meal and Events; reassess the primary survey if deterioration occurs.

PLAB application: Never let a secondary survey delay life-saving interventions.

Trauma imaging and transfer

Use bedside imaging or whole-body CT according to physiology and local major trauma pathway. Unstable patients may need immediate haemorrhage control rather than CT; coordinate rapid transfer to a major trauma centre when needed.

PLAB application: Physiology determines whether CT is safe.

Special populations and safeguarding

Older adults, children, pregnant patients and people taking anticoagulants can deteriorate despite subtle signs. Consider non-accidental injury where the history and injuries are inconsistent, document objectively and escalate safeguarding concerns.

PLAB application: Use age-appropriate assessment and avoid assumptions about injury mechanism.

PLAB 2 trauma communication and handover

Introduce yourself when feasible, call for senior help, give concise updates to patient or family and use SBAR or ATMIST for transfer. Document times, GCS, interventions, responses and ongoing risks.

PLAB application: State immediate threats, actions completed and what must happen next.

PLAB 2 — major trauma handover

Declare the mechanism and time of injury, suspected catastrophic bleeding, ABCDE findings, GCS components, interventions, physiological response, imaging and urgent needs. Use closed-loop communication and seek senior support early.

Deep Dive — Major trauma

The first five minutes

Simultaneously assign roles, obtain monitoring, activate massive haemorrhage pathways and treat immediately reversible threats. Reassessment is continuous.

Preventing secondary brain injury

Hypoxia, hypotension, seizures and temperature derangement can worsen traumatic brain injury. Prioritise oxygenation, perfusion, serial GCS and urgent specialist assessment.

Haemorrhage beyond what you can see

Blood may collect in the chest, abdomen, pelvis and long-bone compartments. Normal initial haemoglobin or blood pressure does not exclude acute bleeding.

Traumatic spinal injury

Neurological examination and safe transfers matter. Imaging decisions follow current NICE criteria and trauma team assessment; prolonged rigid immobilisation can cause harm.

Damage-control principles

Early haemorrhage control, warming, blood component resuscitation and correction of coagulopathy are integrated with surgery or interventional radiology.

Human factors and PLAB 2

Use ATMIST for trauma handover: Age, Time, Mechanism, Injuries, Signs, Treatment. Repeat observations, document intervention timing and explain uncertainty clearly.
