Breast Lumps, Breast Cancer & Breast Emergencies

Surgery · Lesson 15 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Breast presentations: a safe UK approach

Red flags: new hard irregular lump, skin tethering/dimpling, nipple inversion or bloody unilateral spontaneous discharge, axillary mass, peau d’orange or persistent focal changes need prompt assessment. A normal mammogram does not exclude cancer when examination remains suspicious.

1 · History and examination

Ask duration, growth, cyclical change, pain, nipple discharge (spontaneous? unilateral? single duct? bloody?), fever, lactation, trauma, previous breast disease, hormone exposure, family history and impact. Examine both breasts seated and supine, with consent and chaperone; assess skin, nipples, all quadrants and axillary/supraclavicular nodes. Document site using clock-face and distance from nipple, size, mobility, consistency and tenderness. Examine a male breast lump seriously too.

3 · Triple assessment

Breast clinic assessment integrates clinical examination + imaging + needle biopsy when indicated. Ultrasound is often first imaging in younger patients; mammography is commonly used in older adults, with targeted ultrasound as appropriate. Core needle biopsy provides histology and receptor testing. A clinically suspicious lesion requires specialist evaluation despite reassuring initial imaging. Do not rely on a negative test in isolation.

4 · Suspected breast cancer and referral

NICE NG12: consider a suspected-cancer pathway referral for people aged 30 and over with an unexplained breast lump with or without pain; consider referral for those aged 50 and over with unilateral nipple discharge, retraction or other concerning nipple changes. Other age groups and suspicious signs still warrant appropriate urgent specialist assessment. Breast pain alone is usually not cancer but focal persistent symptoms need assessment. Breast cancers include ductal and lobular types, DCIS (non-invasive), and inflammatory breast cancer (rapid erythema/oedema/peau d’orange). Staging considers tumour size, nodal involvement and metastasis; biopsy tests ER, PR and HER2.

5 · Treatment principles

MDT-led treatment may include breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy, mastectomy, sentinel lymph-node biopsy or axillary management, endocrine treatment for hormone receptor-positive disease, anti-HER2 treatment for HER2-positive cancer, chemotherapy and selected immunotherapy. Treatment is tailored to stage, receptor status, genetics and patient preference. Explain reconstruction and fertility issues where relevant. Do not promise a specific operation before staging and MDT discussion.

6 · Breast infection and emergencies

Lactational mastitis: painful red breast with systemic symptoms; continue effective milk removal/breastfeeding when feasible, supportive care and antibiotics if bacterial infection suspected or clinically indicated. Abscess: localised fluctuance, persistent fever or failure to improve; urgent breast ultrasound and drainage/aspiration plus antibiotics when indicated. Non-lactational mastitis: investigate persistent or recurrent symptoms and exclude malignancy. Inflammatory breast cancer: rapidly progressive diffuse erythema and peau d’orange, sometimes without a discrete lump; urgent breast assessment, especially if not responding as expected to infection treatment. Severe sepsis needs ABCDE and emergency escalation.

7 · Safety and communication

Discuss uncertainty clearly, avoid labelling a lump benign on palpation alone, arrange safety-netting and follow-up, and address anxiety sensitively. Family history suggesting hereditary risk may require genetic risk assessment. Screening mammography does not replace symptomatic breast assessment.

Decision frameworks

A · New breast lump

History + bilateral breast/nodal exam → assess red flags → appropriate breast clinic referral → triple assessment (exam, imaging, biopsy if indicated) → concordant results and MDT plan → communicate findings and follow-up.

B · Red, painful breast

Assess fever, sepsis and lactation → mastitis care and antibiotics when indicated → suspected abscess or poor response: ultrasound and drainage assessment → persistent skin changes or non-lactational symptoms: exclude inflammatory cancer.

C · Confirmed breast cancer

Core histology + ER/PR/HER2 → stage and assess nodes → MDT → surgery/systemic treatment/radiotherapy based on biology and stage → survivorship, surveillance and psychosocial support.

Exam distinctions

Fibroadenoma: smooth mobile mass; cyst: fluid-filled on ultrasound; cancer: may be hard/fixed/irregular but can appear deceptively benign; inflammatory cancer: rapidly progressive peau d’orange and erythema; abscess: focal collection requiring drainage.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Painless new lump

A 47-year-old notices a new painless, firm, irregular upper-outer-quadrant lump. Answer: suspected cancer referral and breast clinic triple assessment; do not reassure based on absence of pain.

Case 2 — Young adult with mobile lump

A 22-year-old has a well-defined mobile breast lump. Answer: fibroadenoma is possible, but perform age-appropriate clinical assessment and ultrasound/referral as indicated; avoid definitive diagnosis by palpation.

Case 3 — Breastfeeding and fever

A 30-year-old breastfeeding parent has fever and a tender red area. Symptoms persist with a fluctuant swelling. Answer: mastitis complicated by possible abscess; assess sepsis, ultrasound and urgent drainage pathway, with appropriate antibiotics and feeding support.

Case 4 — Persistent erythema

A 59-year-old has rapid unilateral breast enlargement, diffuse erythema and peau d’orange without a palpable lump. Answer: urgent specialist evaluation for inflammatory breast cancer; infection is a differential, not a reason to defer referral.

PLAB 2 — New breast lump consultation

Station: A 42-year-old reports a new breast lump and fears cancer.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and privacy; acknowledge concern without premature reassurance.
  2. Explore onset, size, change, pain, relation to periods, trauma and other lumps.
  3. Ask nipple discharge, inversion, skin changes, weight loss, fever and axillary swelling.
  4. Ask personal/family cancer history, prior biopsies, medicines/hormones, pregnancies and screening history.
  5. Explain and offer breast and nodal examination with chaperone.
  6. Say that many lumps are benign, but a new unexplained lump needs proper assessment.
  7. Explain triple assessment: specialist examination, ultrasound/mammogram and biopsy if needed.
  8. Arrange appropriate urgent referral, explain likely next steps and avoid predicting results.
  9. Safety-net for rapid changes, redness with fever, new nipple or skin changes; ensure results follow-up.
  10. Check understanding and provide emotional support.

Patient-friendly explanation: “A lump does not automatically mean cancer. The safest way to find out is a breast clinic assessment with examination, scans and sometimes a small tissue sample.”

Rapid revision

  1. Triple assessment = clinical examination, imaging and tissue sampling when indicated.
  2. A painless lump may be malignant; pain does not rule cancer in or out.
  3. Hard irregular mass, skin tethering, new nipple inversion, spontaneous bloody discharge or axillary nodes are concerning.
  4. Young age and mobility suggest fibroadenoma but cannot prove benignity.
  5. NICE NG12 includes suspected-cancer referral for unexplained breast lumps in people aged 30+.
  6. Inflammatory breast cancer may present with peau d’orange and no distinct mass.
  7. Core biopsy supports histology and ER/PR/HER2 testing.
  8. Sentinel node biopsy assesses axillary spread in selected patients.
  9. Mastitis may progress to abscess, requiring drainage.
  10. Persistent non-lactational inflammation requires malignancy exclusion.
  11. Management is MDT-led and tailored to stage and receptors.
  12. Symptomatic breast assessment is separate from routine screening.
