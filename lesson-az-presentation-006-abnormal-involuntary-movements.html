Abnormal involuntary movements: first recognise the movement, then localise and explain it

Involuntary movements are a presentation, not a diagnosis. A useful clinical sequence is to determine the phenomenology (rhythmic or non-rhythmic, sustained or brief, suppressible or not), identify associated neurological signs, ask about medications and systemic illness, and decide whether the onset requires immediate treatment.

Article audio
60-second PLAB summary: Rest tremor plus bradykinesia or rigidity suggests parkinsonism; symmetric action tremor without other signs suggests essential tremor. Irregular flowing movements are chorea; patterned twisting is dystonia; lightning-like jerks are myoclonus; brief repetitive potentially suppressible movements are tics. Sudden focal onset, altered consciousness, stridor after dopamine-blocking drugs, fever plus rigidity and autonomic instability, or severe metabolic disturbance require urgent escalation. In stable patients examine carefully, review medication timing, check targeted blood tests and refer according to NICE NG127/NG71.

Rest, posture, action: Ask the patient to rest hands fully supported, hold arms outstretched, then draw a spiral / write and perform finger–nose testing. The classification is a clue, not a definitive diagnosis. Cerebellar intention tremor often coexists with dysmetria or ataxia.

2. Structured history: TIME–DRUGS–SYSTEMS

  1. Onset and evolution: sudden (vascular, seizure, toxicity), days/weeks (medicine, metabolic, inflammatory), or months/years (degenerative/genetic); episodic versus constant; unilateral versus bilateral.
  2. Phenomenology: ask when it appears (rest, action, sleep, stress), whether awareness is affected, whether it can be suppressed, and if an urge precedes it. Video from home can be valuable if consented.
  3. Drugs and exposures: recent initiation/dose changes in metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, antipsychotics, lithium, antidepressants, valproate, stimulants and levodopa; alcohol use/withdrawal, caffeine, illicit stimulants; environmental exposures where relevant.
  4. Associated symptoms: bradykinesia, rigidity, falls, altered gait, dysarthria, ataxia, weakness, numbness, headache, fever, weight loss, confusion, seizures or psychiatric/cognitive change.
  5. Family and developmental history: movement disorders, Huntington disease, age of onset, childhood tics, previous perinatal brain injury; be sensitive about genetic implications.
  6. Impact and safety: walking, eating, writing, driving, medication adherence, swallowing, falls, employment and distress; assess mental health, especially with akathisia or progressive neurodegeneration.

3. Examination: observe before touching

Record vital signs, temperature, oxygen saturation, glucose and conscious state in any acutely unwell patient. Watch movement at rest and during conversation, then with distraction, posture and goal-directed actions. Describe distribution, frequency, rhythm, amplitude, pattern, triggers, suppressibility and associated signs. Assess tone, bradykinesia (finger tapping), power, reflexes, plantar responses, eye movements, cerebellar signs, gait, facial expression and cognition. A functional movement disorder requires positive examination findings such as variability, distractibility or entrainment when appropriate—not simply normal investigations.

At the bedside: bilateral postural tremor with normal tapping and gait → essential/enhanced physiological tremor possible; asymmetric rest tremor with bradykinesia → suspected Parkinson disease; acute abnormal neck/eye posture after an antiemetic → drug-induced dystonia; irregular jerks plus confusion or renal failure → toxic-metabolic myoclonus possible.

4. Recognise time-critical causes

ABCDE and immediate senior help: new focal movement disorder with weakness, facial droop or speech disturbance (stroke pathway); continuous convulsions or repeated seizures without recovery (status epilepticus pathway); acute laryngeal dystonia (stridor, difficulty breathing or swallowing) after dopamine-blocking medicines; fever, severe rigidity, altered mental state and autonomic instability after antipsychotics (possible neuroleptic malignant syndrome); severe hyperthermia/clonus after serotonergic drugs (possible serotonin syndrome); severe hypoglycaemia or electrolyte disturbance. The need for emergency care is determined clinically, not by waiting for a scan.

Children: NICE NG127 advises immediate neurological referral for sudden-onset chorea, ataxia or dystonia. Be vigilant for post-infectious, inflammatory and other acute causes; a child with abrupt neurological change requires urgent examination.

Brain MRI is not routinely required for every long-standing benign-appearing tremor. Use it for focal, rapidly progressive, atypical or otherwise concerning neurological features as judged by a clinician. Do not order genetic testing casually without counselling around implications.

6. Management and referral: treat mechanism, not movement alone

  1. Stabilise emergencies: treat underlying life threat, correct glucose/electrolytes as appropriate, and escalate to emergency, stroke or seizure teams.
  2. Stop/review causative medicines safely: urgent suspected drug reactions need appropriate medicine review; do not abruptly stop longstanding antipsychotics or Parkinson medicines without specialist input unless emergency management requires it.
  3. Essential tremor: if troublesome, discuss function, medication/caffeine effects and specialist/primary care management. Propranolol or primidone may be considered based on comorbidities and contraindications; do not prescribe automatically.
  4. Suspected Parkinson disease: NICE NG71 advises prompt referral before treatment to a specialist with expertise in differential diagnosis. Consider physiotherapy, occupational and speech support after confirmation.
  5. Dystonia: focal forms may benefit from specialist botulinum toxin treatment; acute drug-induced forms need prompt recognition, airway assessment and locally recommended anticholinergic treatment, e.g. procyclidine, after clinical evaluation.
  6. Chorea and myoclonus: manage cause first; symptomatic agents and specialist input depend on mechanism, comorbidities, cognitive / psychiatric status and adverse effects.
  7. Tics: psychoeducation and behavioural approaches where troublesome; uncomplicated tics do not always require a neurology referral or medication (NG127).

8. Five worked clinical cases

Case A — asymmetric tremor: A 69-year-old has a 7-month right-hand rest tremor, reduced right arm swing and slow finger tapping. Likely syndrome: parkinsonism. Next: refer untreated to movement disorder specialist; review atypical signs (early falls, gaze palsy, autonomic symptoms).
Case B — sudden neck spasm: A 22-year-old develops painful torticollis and upward eye deviation after IV metoclopramide. Diagnosis: acute dystonic reaction. Next: check airway and observations, urgently treat per local protocol and document medicine reaction.
Case C — progressive chorea: A 43-year-old has involuntary dance-like movements, disinhibition and worsening executive functioning; parent had a similar illness. Concern: Huntington disease. Next: neurology/genetics referral with counselling, psychological risk assessment and family support.
Case D — shaking and systemic symptoms: A 34-year-old has new fine postural tremor, palpitations, sweating and weight loss. DDx: thyrotoxicosis, stimulant/caffeine and medication effects. Next: examine heart rate, thyroid and medicines; arrange TFTs and other clinically indicated tests.
Case E — jerks plus confusion: A 77-year-old with advanced kidney dysfunction develops multifocal myoclonus and altered attention after a medicine change. Concern: toxic-metabolic encephalopathy or drug accumulation. Next: urgent medical assessment, glucose/renal/electrolytes and medicine reconciliation; evaluate for seizure or sepsis as indicated.

9. PLAB 2 OSCE: tremor assessment and explanation

Opening: “Could you tell me when you first noticed the shaking, and when it is worst?” Obtain consent for focused neurologic assessment. Ask about functional impairment, falls, medication list, alcohol, caffeine, family history and neurological symptoms. Observe rest/posture/action, handwriting or spiral, finger taps, tone and gait. Explain uncertainty without prematurely diagnosing Parkinson disease. Safety-net: sudden weakness, new speech disturbance, altered awareness, severe headache or seizure requires emergency care; progressive symptoms warrant planned specialist follow-up.

Pattern-to-priority algorithm

  1. Sudden onset, impaired awareness, new focal signs, fever or airway symptoms? ABCDE and immediate pathway escalation.
  2. Rhythmic oscillation? Test rest, posture and intention; examine for bradykinesia and cerebellar signs.
  3. Irregular flowing movement? Consider chorea and vascular, drug or genetic causes.
  4. Twisting posturing? Consider dystonia; review recent dopamine blockers.
  5. Shock-like jerks? Consider myoclonus, seizures, metabolic and toxic processes.
  6. Suppressible with premonitory urge? Tics become more likely, but assess function and comorbidity.
PLAB traps: Do not call every shaking movement a seizure. Do not label all tremor as Parkinson disease. A normal glucose or absent family history does not settle every differential. Do not mistake akathisia for treatment-resistant agitation. Airway involvement in acute dystonia must be assessed immediately. NMS and serotonin syndrome can both cause hyperthermia but differ in neurological signs and medication exposure.

Deep Dive: why abnormal movement phenotypes arise

1. Basal ganglia direct and indirect pathways

Motor cortex communicates with striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus and thalamus in recurrent loops. The direct pathway facilitates selected movements by reducing inhibitory output; the indirect pathway helps suppress competing motor programs. Dopamine from substantia nigra pars compacta modulates these circuits, promoting useful movement through D1 and D2 receptor effects. Clinical signs are network effects rather than a single isolated anatomical lesion.

2. Parkinsonism and loss of dopaminergic input

Loss of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons changes thalamocortical motor activation. Bradykinesia and rigidity are core bedside signs; rest tremor reflects complex oscillatory circuits and is not obligatory. Non-motor symptoms can include constipation, anosmia, sleep disturbance and autonomic dysfunction. Importantly, tremor alone does not diagnose Parkinson disease.

3. Why chorea and hemiballismus look different

Chorea reflects excessive irregular release of motor programs across basal ganglia–thalamocortical circuits. Huntington disease involves degeneration, including striatal neuronal populations, together with cortical and other network changes. Hemiballismus can occur with contralateral subthalamic/basal ganglia circuit disruption; a sudden presentation should raise vascular concern even without the textbook lesion location.

4. Tremor oscillators

Tremor is rhythmical alternating or synchronous muscle activation involving oscillatory neural circuits. Essential tremor implicates cerebello-thalamo-cortical networks; cerebellar lesions can cause intention tremor with dysmetria. Beta-agonists, caffeine, hyperthyroidism and anxiety may enhance physiological tremor through increased adrenergic drive.

5. Dystonia and sensorimotor processing

Dystonia involves impaired inhibition and altered sensorimotor integration, producing patterned co-contractions and sustained postures. A task-specific dystonia such as writer's cramp differs from a simple painful muscle spasm. Focal botulinum toxin reduces acetylcholine release at neuromuscular junctions and is a specialist treatment for certain patterns.

6. Myoclonus by generator

Myoclonus can be cortical, subcortical, spinal or peripheral, and may be epileptic or non-epileptic. Abrupt cortical discharges can produce rapid muscle jerks; negative myoclonus represents a brief interruption of ongoing muscle activity. Toxic-metabolic encephalopathy may produce multifocal jerks and asterixis; check kidney, liver and electrolyte function in the appropriate context.

7. D2 antagonism explains acute dystonia and parkinsonism

Dopamine receptor blockade in nigrostriatal circuitry disturbs dopaminergic–cholinergic balance. Acute dystonia can follow metoclopramide, prochlorperazine or antipsychotics, sometimes within hours. Antimuscarinic medication such as procyclidine can relieve acute dystonia under local emergency protocols. Chronic exposure can lead to tardive dyskinesia, which requires a different long-term management strategy.

8. Akathisia is not simply anxiety

Akathisia includes intense subjective inner restlessness and a drive to move, often after a dopamine-blocker dose change. It can be profoundly distressing and is associated with risk of self-harm in some patients. Taking a careful drug chronology is essential to avoid inadvertently escalating the causative medication.

9. NMS versus serotonin syndrome

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome typically features severe rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability and fever after dopamine blockade; serotonin syndrome often develops faster after serotonergic exposure, with hyperreflexia, inducible/spontaneous clonus and autonomic excitation. Both can cause life-threatening complications and require urgent medical treatment; presentations may overlap.

10. Why the clinical examination comes before sophisticated testing

Movement classification depends on what the examiner sees, whether the movement changes with rest/task/distraction, and which neurological signs accompany it. This guides use of targeted thyroid/metabolic tests, EEG, MRI and selected specialist dopamine-transporter imaging. Unfocused testing without phenotyping can produce misleading results.

11. Genetics and patient autonomy

Huntington disease is associated with pathogenic CAG expansion in HTT and autosomal dominant inheritance. Testing an at-risk asymptomatic person has important personal and family implications. Arrange specialist genetics counselling and avoid promising or pressuring predictive testing during an initial general medical encounter.

12. Integrated examiner example

A 64-year-old with new asymmetric rest tremor and slowed right finger tapping should be examined for rigidity, gait changes, medication exposures and atypical signs; if parkinsonism is suspected, arrange prompt expert neurological assessment before treatment (NG71). In contrast, a 24-year-old with abrupt oculogyric crisis after an antiemetic requires emergency assessment for acute dystonia and possible airway involvement. The movement phenotype determines the clinical priority.
