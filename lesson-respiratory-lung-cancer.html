Lung Cancer: diagnosis, airway clearance and exacerbations

Emergency reminder: Major haemoptysis, severe breathlessness, confusion or sepsis requires emergency assessment and escalation. This lesson is educational, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

Overview and types

Lung cancer is a malignant tumour arising in the lung. Main groups are non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), including adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma, and small-cell lung cancer (SCLC).

Clinical application: Histology and stage guide treatment; SCLC often grows and spreads rapidly.

Risk factors

Smoking is the most important modifiable risk factor. Other factors include passive smoke, radon, asbestos, air pollution, occupational carcinogens and family history.

Clinical application: Ask about pack-years, exposures and smoking cessation without blame.

Symptoms and signs

Persistent or changing cough, haemoptysis, breathlessness, chest pain, recurrent pneumonia, weight loss and fatigue may occur.

Clinical application: Examine for clubbing, lymphadenopathy, focal chest signs and features of metastatic disease.

Urgent referral

NICE NG12 outlines suspected cancer referral and urgent chest X-ray criteria, based on age, smoking history and symptoms.

Clinical application: Haemoptysis in an adult aged 40 or over is a key suspected-cancer pathway trigger; apply full current NG12 criteria and local pathways.

Initial imaging

Chest X-ray is often an initial investigation, but a normal film does not exclude lung cancer when suspicion remains.

Clinical application: Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and upper abdomen helps define the lesion, lymph nodes and possible spread.

Obtaining tissue

Histological or cytological confirmation is usually needed. Methods include bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), CT-guided biopsy and sampling metastatic sites.

Clinical application: Choose the safest route that provides sufficient tissue for histology and molecular testing.

Staging

NSCLC uses TNM staging to describe tumour extent, nodal involvement and metastases. PET-CT, brain imaging and invasive mediastinal staging are used selectively.

Clinical application: SCLC is commonly described as limited or extensive stage alongside TNM where relevant.

Molecular and biomarker testing

Advanced NSCLC may require testing for actionable alterations and PD-L1 expression to guide targeted or immune therapies.

Clinical application: Testing pathways vary by histology and current NHS commissioning; ensure adequate biopsy tissue.

Early-stage NSCLC

Surgery can be curative in suitable early-stage NSCLC; stereotactic ablative radiotherapy may be an option if surgery is unsuitable.

Clinical application: Assess operability using lung function, fitness, comorbidities and specialist MDT review.

Locally advanced NSCLC

Selected patients may receive combinations of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery or immunotherapy depending on stage and fitness.

Clinical application: Management should be individualised by a lung cancer multidisciplinary team.

Metastatic NSCLC

Systemic options include immunotherapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapy where biomarkers support them.

Clinical application: Treatment goals, expected benefit and toxicity should be discussed through shared decision-making.

Small-cell lung cancer

SCLC is strongly associated with smoking, tends to progress quickly and is usually treated with systemic therapy, sometimes with radiotherapy.

Clinical application: Urgent specialist staging and treatment planning are important.

Complications and emergencies

Superior vena cava obstruction may cause facial swelling, distended neck veins and dyspnoea. Malignant spinal cord compression may cause back pain, weakness or bladder dysfunction.

Clinical application: Suspected cord compression is an oncological emergency: urgent escalation and follow local MSCC guidance.

Paraneoplastic syndromes

SCLC can cause SIADH or ectopic ACTH production. Squamous cancers may cause PTHrP-mediated hypercalcaemia.

Clinical application: Check for unexplained hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia or neurological syndromes.

Supportive and palliative care

Symptom control includes analgesia, breathlessness support, psychological care, smoking cessation, nutritional support and early palliative care when appropriate.

Clinical application: Discuss patient priorities, prognosis sensitively and involve specialist nurses and multidisciplinary services.

PLAB 2 communication

Explore concerns, explain why tests are needed without prematurely confirming cancer, and use clear safety-netting.

Clinical application: Use a structured approach for breaking bad news, offer written information and arrange timely follow-up.

PLAB 2: suspected lung cancer counselling

  1. Explore symptoms, smoking/exposure history and concerns.
  2. Explain that symptoms need urgent investigation but do not confirm cancer.
  3. Describe chest imaging, CT, biopsy and specialist review in clear language.
  4. Discuss smoking cessation, support and likely next steps.
  5. Safety-net haemoptysis, worsening breathlessness, neurological deficits and severe pain.

Deep Dive

Why histology matters

NSCLC and SCLC differ in growth, staging and therapeutic strategies.

Molecular oncology

Actionable driver alterations and PD-L1 expression can determine eligibility for targeted or immune treatment in advanced NSCLC.

TNM staging

T describes primary tumour extent, N regional lymph nodes and M distant metastases; staging guides prognosis and management.

Paraneoplastic physiology

SIADH causes dilutional hyponatraemia; PTHrP can raise serum calcium.

Why EBUS is useful

Minimally invasive ultrasound-guided needle sampling can provide tissue from mediastinal and hilar nodes.

Palliative care alongside treatment

Early supportive care helps manage symptoms, psychological distress and treatment decisions.
