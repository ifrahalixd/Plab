Drug Eruptions, Stevens–Johnson Syndrome & Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis

PLAB priority: Recognise skin pain, mucosal erosions and blistering as severe drug-reaction red flags. Suspected SJS/TEN requires immediate emergency admission.

01. Why drug eruptions matter

Cutaneous adverse drug reactions range from self-limiting maculopapular rashes to life-threatening severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCAR), including Stevens–Johnson syndrome (SJS), toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), DRESS and AGEP.

PLAB application: A new rash after starting a medicine needs a drug timeline and assessment for systemic danger.

02. First assessment: ABCDE and severity

Check airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, temperature, hydration and pain. Ask about skin pain, blisters, detachment, mouth or eye symptoms, fever and systemic illness.

PLAB application: Mucosal involvement, skin tenderness or blistering warrants immediate emergency assessment.

03. Constructing the medication timeline

Record all prescription, OTC, herbal and recently stopped drugs; dates started and stopped, previous exposures, dose changes and concurrent illness. Many uncomplicated exanthems begin 4–14 days after a new drug, sooner after re-exposure.

PLAB application: Do not assume the newest medicine is the culprit without checking plausible latency.

04. Morbilliform drug eruption

A symmetric erythematous maculopapular rash commonly begins on the trunk and spreads to limbs, often itchy. Patients are usually otherwise well, without mucosal erosion or epidermal detachment.

PLAB application: Distinguish uncomplicated exanthem from early SCAR; reassess if red flags develop.

05. Urticaria and immediate allergy

Transient itchy wheals can occur after drugs; associated airway, breathing or circulation compromise indicates anaphylaxis, not isolated urticaria.

PLAB application: Treat anaphylaxis immediately with intramuscular adrenaline according to resuscitation guidance.

06. Fixed drug eruption

A sharply demarcated round or oval dusky patch recurs at the same site on re-exposure and can leave post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation; occasionally bullous.

PLAB application: Ask whether the same spot reacted after a previous course.

07. SJS and TEN: defining features

SJS/TEN are severe mucocutaneous reactions with painful dusky or targetoid lesions, blistering and epidermal detachment, usually with oral, ocular or genital erosions. SJS involves <10% body surface area detachment; overlap 10–30%; TEN >30%.

PLAB application: A painful rash with erosive mucositis is an emergency even before extensive detachment.

08. SJS/TEN triggers and latency

Common triggers include allopurinol, sulfonamide antibiotics, certain anticonvulsants such as carbamazepine and lamotrigine, and some NSAIDs. Onset is often within 1–4 weeks of exposure, although timing varies.

PLAB application: Stop suspected causative and nonessential medicines promptly under senior supervision.

09. Emergency management of suspected SJS/TEN

Arrange immediate hospital admission and urgent dermatology, critical care/burns and relevant specialty input. Stabilise ABCDE, give analgesia, carefully manage fluids and temperature, protect skin, avoid adhesive trauma, monitor infection and electrolytes.

PLAB application: Do not manage suspected SJS/TEN as a routine outpatient rash or delay transfer for tests.

10. Eyes and mucosal protection

Ocular involvement may cause lasting scarring and vision loss. Seek urgent ophthalmology review; assess mouth, genitals, urinary symptoms and ability to swallow.

PLAB application: Ophthalmology should be involved early even when eye symptoms initially appear mild.

11. SCORTEN and prognostic assessment

SCORTEN is a validated mortality risk score used by specialists in SJS/TEN; it includes age, malignancy, heart rate, detached area, urea, glucose and bicarbonate.

PLAB application: Know its purpose; urgent stabilisation and referral come before scoring.

12. DRESS syndrome

Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms usually presents weeks after drug initiation with widespread rash, fever, facial oedema, lymphadenopathy and possible hepatitis, nephritis or other organ injury. Eosinophilia may be absent early.

PLAB application: Request FBC, liver and renal tests and assess organ involvement; urgent specialist care is needed.

13. Acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis

AGEP causes rapid-onset widespread tiny non-follicular sterile pustules on erythematous skin, often with fever and neutrophilia, typically after a drug exposure.

PLAB application: Do not confuse AGEP with ordinary acne or bacterial folliculitis.

14. Investigations and differential diagnosis

Investigate according to severity: FBC, renal function, electrolytes, liver tests, glucose and cultures if infection suspected; biopsy may support diagnosis. Consider viral exanthem, erythema multiforme, staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome and autoimmune blistering disease.

PLAB application: Clinical severity and drug history guide initial actions; do not await biopsy to escalate.

15. Recovery and long-term prevention

Document suspected culprit drugs, reaction phenotype and dates clearly; communicate to patient, GP and pharmacy. Consider specialist allergy/dermatology advice and report serious suspected adverse drug reactions via the MHRA Yellow Card scheme.

PLAB application: Give explicit written avoidance advice; never casually re-challenge after SJS/TEN.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and safety-netting

Explain uncertainty, examine respectfully, assess pain and distress, ask about medication access and understanding, and provide clear escalation advice. For mild rashes, consider medication review and symptom relief with follow-up.

PLAB application: Use teach-back: worsening pain, fever, blistering, mouth sores or eye symptoms need urgent assessment.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

T-cell-mediated injury in SJS/TEN

Drug-specific immune responses can trigger keratinocyte apoptosis and widespread epidermal necrosis. The key danger is loss of skin-barrier integrity plus mucosal damage, with fluid loss, infection risk and systemic instability.

Why skin pain matters

Itch is common in benign exanthems, whereas skin tenderness or pain can reflect epidermal injury. Pain plus dusky lesions should prompt urgent examination for blistering and mucosal erosion.

Distinguishing erythema multiforme

Erythema multiforme often has classic three-zone target lesions, frequently acral and associated with HSV; SJS/TEN more often has atypical targets, widespread painful dusky lesions, prominent mucosal disease and detachment.

DRESS as a systemic syndrome

The skin eruption may be only one component. Delayed immune activation can cause hepatitis, kidney injury, pneumonitis or myocarditis; normal early eosinophil count does not exclude the diagnosis.

Supportive care is central

SJS/TEN patients need careful fluid balance, nutrition, analgesia, thermoregulation, gentle dressings, infection surveillance and multidisciplinary mucosal care. Decisions about immunomodulatory therapies are specialist-led.

Prescribing and allergy documentation

A vague label of “rash” can cause inappropriate avoidance or dangerous re-exposure. Record phenotype, onset, suspected drug, organ involvement and whether SJS/TEN was confirmed; involve specialists for uncertain histories.
