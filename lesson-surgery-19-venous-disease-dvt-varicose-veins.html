Venous Disease, Deep Vein Thrombosis & Varicose Veins

Surgery · Lesson 19 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Venous disorders: PLAB clinical approach

Emergency red flags: sudden breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, syncope or hypoxia in suspected DVT → assess for pulmonary embolism (PE) urgently. Severe painful swollen cyanotic limb (phlegmasia cerulea dolens) can threaten the limb: emergency vascular referral. Uncontrolled bleeding from a varicose vein requires direct pressure and urgent assessment.

1 · Venous anatomy and pathophysiology

Deep veins return most leg blood; superficial great and small saphenous veins connect through perforators. Venous valves and calf-muscle pump prevent reflux. DVT arises through Virchow's triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability. Chronic venous insufficiency results from reflux or obstruction and sustained venous hypertension.

2 · DVT: risk factors and presentation

Risk factors include recent surgery or immobility, active cancer, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen therapy, previous VTE, thrombophilia, obesity and long travel. Typical symptoms are unilateral swelling, calf pain/tenderness, warmth and erythema; signs are nonspecific, and DVT may be asymptomatic. Examine both legs, measure calf circumference where appropriate, assess pulses and look for PE symptoms. Differentiate cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst, muscle injury and superficial thrombophlebitis.

3 · NICE NG158 diagnostic pathway

Use the two-level DVT Wells score for suspected lower-limb DVT. A score ≥2 = DVT likely; ≤1 = DVT unlikely. If likely, offer proximal leg vein ultrasound with result within 4 hours if possible; if unavailable, obtain D-dimer, start interim therapeutic anticoagulation where appropriate and ultrasound within 24 hours. If initial ultrasound negative but D-dimer positive, repeat proximal ultrasound in 6–8 days. If unlikely, obtain D-dimer first; negative can rule out DVT in appropriate patients, positive leads to ultrasound. Follow the exact NICE algorithm including age-adjusted D-dimer where applicable and safety-netting. In patients receiving anticoagulants, test interpretation differs.

4 · DVT treatment and prevention

For confirmed proximal DVT, offer anticoagulation for at least 3 months and review whether to continue based on provoked/unprovoked status, recurrence and bleeding risk. NICE commonly recommends apixaban or rivaroxaban first-line if suitable; renal impairment, pregnancy, active cancer, antiphospholipid syndrome and drug interactions alter choice. Pregnancy usually requires LMWH under specialist guidance; DOACs are avoided. Check FBC, renal/liver function and coagulation baseline, but do not unnecessarily delay interim treatment. Advise on bleeding signs, adherence, PE red flags and follow-up. Mechanical and pharmacological VTE prophylaxis in hospital depends on individual risk and contraindications (NICE NG89).

5 · Pulmonary embolism and limb-threatening venous thrombosis

Suspected PE: ABCDE, oxygen if hypoxaemic, urgent senior review and NICE PE diagnostic pathway (Wells, D-dimer, CTPA or V/Q as indicated). Haemodynamic instability is an emergency and may require reperfusion therapy. Phlegmasia cerulea dolens causes massive painful swelling, cyanosis and possible loss of arterial inflow from venous congestion: immediate vascular/IR assessment, anticoagulation if appropriate and consideration of urgent intervention.

6 · Superficial vein thrombosis (SVT)

Localized tender, red, palpable cord over a superficial vein; assess for proximal extension and associated DVT. Duplex ultrasound is important for extensive SVT, near the saphenofemoral junction, or when DVT is suspected. Management may include mobilisation, symptom relief and anticoagulation in higher-risk or extensive disease per specialist/local guidance. Avoid assuming all superficial thrombophlebitis is harmless.

7 · Varicose veins and chronic venous insufficiency

Varicose veins are dilated, tortuous superficial veins caused by valve incompetence. Symptoms include aching/heaviness after standing, itching, ankle swelling and skin changes. Complications: eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, haemosiderin staining, venous ulceration, SVT and bleeding. Assess standing, inspect skin and ulcers, check peripheral pulses and obtain duplex ultrasound to confirm reflux and plan treatment.

8 · NICE CG168 referral and treatment

Refer to vascular service for symptomatic primary/recurrent varicose veins, lower-limb skin changes attributed to venous insufficiency, superficial vein thrombosis with suspected venous incompetence, and venous leg ulcer (active ulcer below knee not healed within 2 weeks or healed ulcer). Bleeding varicose veins: immediate referral. Confirm truncal reflux with duplex. Treatment sequence: endothermal ablation (radiofrequency/laser) first if suitable, then ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy, then surgery if other options unsuitable. Do not use compression hosiery as definitive treatment if intervention is suitable, though it may aid symptoms and ulcer care.

9 · Venous ulcers and compression safety

Venous ulcers typically occur in the gaiter area near the medial malleolus, with oedema, pigmentation and shallow irregular wound. Arterial ulcers are often painful, distal and associated with poor pulses. Assess ABPI before high compression to exclude significant arterial disease; ABPI can be falsely elevated with calcification. Compression, leg elevation, exercise, wound care and venous intervention may promote healing; refer to specialist services for mixed disease or non-healing wounds.

10 · Patient communication

Explain DVT as a clot in a deep vein with potential to travel to lungs; provide clear safety-net advice. For varicose veins, discuss symptoms, duplex confirmation, options, recurrence and procedural risks. Encourage activity, weight management and skin care; avoid prolonged immobility when possible.

Clinical algorithms

Suspected leg DVT

History and examination → 2-level DVT Wells → likely (≥2): proximal ultrasound promptly; if delayed, D-dimer and interim therapeutic anticoagulation as appropriate → unlikely (≤1): D-dimer first → ultrasound if positive. Negative initial scan plus positive D-dimer may require repeat scan at 6–8 days under NICE NG158.

Confirmed proximal DVT

Baseline bloods and bleeding assessment → appropriate therapeutic anticoagulant (often apixaban/rivaroxaban if suitable) → at least 3 months → reassess provoking factors, recurrence and bleeding risk → safety-net for PE and bleeding.

Symptomatic varicose veins

Examine and check complications → vascular referral → duplex-confirmed truncal reflux → endothermal ablation first if suitable → foam sclerotherapy → surgery if other treatments unsuitable.

Venous leg ulcer

Assess pulses, ABPI and wound → compression only after arterial assessment → wound care and venous specialist input → consider reflux treatment and review healing.

Venous emergencies

PE symptoms or shock: emergency PE assessment. Blue massively swollen painful leg: suspect phlegmasia and urgent vascular escalation. Bleeding varicosity: elevate leg, direct pressure and immediate specialist referral.

Four applied cases

Case 1 — Postoperative DVT

A 64-year-old develops unilateral calf swelling and tenderness 8 days after hip surgery. Wells score suggests DVT likely. Action: urgent proximal leg vein ultrasound per NICE pathway; interim anticoagulation if scan delayed and clinically appropriate; assess for PE.

Case 2 — Possible PE

A patient being investigated for DVT develops sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic pain and tachycardia. Action: ABCDE, urgent senior assessment, oxygen if needed and PE diagnostic/treatment pathway; do not focus solely on the leg.

Case 3 — Symptomatic varicosities

A 42-year-old has aching visible leg veins and ankle swelling after standing; no ulcer. Action: refer for duplex and discuss endothermal ablation as first-line intervention if truncal reflux and suitable anatomy.

Case 4 — Venous ulcer

A 71-year-old has an irregular shallow medial ankle ulcer, brown pigmentation and oedema. Action: assess arterial supply/ABPI before compression; provide wound care and refer for venous assessment and treatment.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Swollen painful leg

Station: A 58-year-old presents with a swollen left calf after a long period of immobility.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, ask permission and assess discomfort.
  2. Ask onset, unilateral swelling, pain, redness, recent surgery, immobility, cancer, pregnancy/oestrogen use, prior DVT and bleeding risk.
  3. Screen urgently for PE: breathlessness, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, syncope.
  4. Examine both legs for asymmetry, oedema, warmth, tenderness and superficial veins; check pulses and vital signs.
  5. Calculate the two-level DVT Wells score; explain need for ultrasound and possibly D-dimer according to the score.
  6. Explain: “A clot in a deep leg vein is one possible cause. We need to check promptly because a clot can sometimes travel to the lungs.”
  7. Discuss interim anticoagulation if imaging is delayed and appropriate; ask about bleeding, kidney disease, pregnancy and medication interactions.
  8. Safety-net: call emergency services for sudden breathlessness, chest pain, collapse or significant bleeding.
  9. Check understanding, document findings and arrange timely follow-up of test results.

Examiner focus: Wells scoring, correct NICE sequencing, PE red flags, anticoagulation safety and empathetic explanation.
