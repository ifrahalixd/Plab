Dermatological Examination, Lesion Recognition & PLAB 2 OSCE

PLAB priority: Describe lesions accurately, examine respectfully, identify emergency red flags and communicate a safe plan.

01. Preparation, consent and dignity

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, explain the examination and obtain consent. Ask about pain; offer a chaperone where appropriate. Wash hands, ensure adequate lighting and expose only the area required.

PLAB application: For PLAB 2, narrate what you are doing and maintain privacy throughout.

02. Focused dermatology history

Clarify onset, duration, progression, itch, pain, discharge, bleeding, systemic symptoms, exposures, medicines, allergies, travel, contacts, atopy and family history. Ask how the condition affects sleep, work and mood.

PLAB application: New medication plus painful blistering or mucosal symptoms requires emergency assessment.

03. General inspection

Inspect the patient from an appropriate distance before close examination. Note distribution, symmetry, number, colour, morphology, secondary changes and evidence of scratching. Consider temperature and systemic observations if unwell.

PLAB application: Describe the rash before naming a diagnosis.

04. Primary lesions: macules and patches

A macule is a flat, circumscribed colour change under 1 cm; a patch is a larger flat colour change. Neither is palpable. Examples include freckles, vitiligo and some drug eruptions.

PLAB application: A flat lesion is not a papule, even if its colour is striking.

05. Primary lesions: papules, plaques and nodules

A papule is a small raised solid lesion; a plaque is a broad raised lesion, often formed by coalescing papules; a nodule is a deeper solid lesion. Describe surface, edge and palpation.

PLAB application: Well-demarcated scaly plaques on extensor surfaces suggest psoriasis.

06. Primary lesions: vesicles, bullae and pustules

A vesicle is a small fluid-filled blister; a bulla is larger. A pustule contains purulent-looking material, which may be sterile. Describe whether blisters are tense or flaccid and whether mucosa is affected.

PLAB application: Tense itchy bullae in an older adult suggest bullous pemphigoid; painful mucosal erosions suggest pemphigus or a severe drug reaction.

07. Wheals and vascular lesions

Wheals are transient raised oedematous lesions that usually resolve within 24 hours at a given site. Purpura is non-blanching blood extravasation; petechiae are small pinpoint lesions.

PLAB application: Non-blanching rash with fever or shock needs urgent assessment for serious infection.

08. Secondary changes

Scale, crust, excoriation, fissure, erosion, ulcer, lichenification, atrophy and scarring reflect evolution or scratching. An erosion is superficial; an ulcer extends more deeply.

PLAB application: Honey-coloured crust suggests impetigo, but appearances alone do not establish systemic illness.

09. Distribution and pattern

Record flexural, extensor, acral, photo-exposed, dermatomal, intertriginous, seborrhoeic or generalised involvement. Note linear, annular, targetoid or grouped arrangement.

PLAB application: A unilateral dermatomal painful vesicular eruption supports shingles; annular scaly lesions may suggest tinea.

10. Recognising findings in darker skin

Erythema may look violaceous, brown or grey rather than bright red. Palpate for warmth, oedema and texture changes, and compare with adjacent skin. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and hypopigmentation can be prominent.

PLAB application: Do not rule out cellulitis or eczema solely because obvious redness is absent.

11. Palpation and bedside assessment

With consent, assess tenderness, warmth, induration, fluctuance, scale and blanching. Check relevant lymph nodes and joints where indicated. Consider dermoscopy if trained, but do not delay referral for a suspicious lesion.

PLAB application: Pain out of proportion and rapid progression suggest necrotising infection.

12. Full skin, scalp, nails and mucosa

Examine additional sites guided by symptoms and suspected disease, including scalp, palms, soles, nails and oral mucosa; obtain specific consent for intimate areas. Look for nail pitting, onycholysis and mucosal erosions.

PLAB application: Psoriasis may have nail changes and associated inflammatory arthritis.

13. Recognising common PLAB conditions

Atopic eczema often itches with flexural involvement; psoriasis causes well-defined scaly plaques; scabies causes nocturnal itch with contact history; tinea is often annular and scaly; urticaria produces fleeting wheals.

PLAB application: Use morphology, site, time course and history together rather than one feature alone.

14. Skin cancer recognition and referral

Assess evolution, asymmetry, border irregularity, colour variation, diameter and change for melanoma; note pearly rolled-edge lesions for basal cell carcinoma and growing keratotic or ulcerated lesions for squamous cell carcinoma. Follow current UK referral pathways.

PLAB application: A changing lesion can be suspicious even if small or non-pigmented.

15. Emergency dermatology red flags

Escalate urgently for suspected SJS/TEN, eczema herpeticum, necrotising fasciitis, anaphylaxis, meningococcal sepsis or severe erythroderma. Assess ABCDE and vital signs; initiate emergency management within competence.

PLAB application: A painful widespread rash with mucosal involvement after a new drug is an emergency.

16. PLAB 2 presentation and safety-netting

Summarise site, distribution, morphology, secondary changes, relevant negatives and leading differential. Explain your plan in plain language, check understanding, discuss treatment adherence and provide clear red-flag safety-netting.

PLAB application: A structured presentation scores better than an unqualified diagnostic guess.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Morphology as a diagnostic language

Primary lesions reflect the underlying process: epidermal change creates scale; dermal oedema creates wheals; vascular leakage creates purpura. Precise description allows safer handover even before a diagnosis is certain.

Why distribution matters

Flexural versus extensor, dermatomal versus generalised, and photo-exposed versus covered sites narrow differentials. Distribution is useful only when combined with symptoms and chronology.

Inflammation in diverse skin tones

Inflammation is not synonymous with bright redness. Pigment changes, induration, heat, swelling and patient-reported symptoms may be more informative in deeply pigmented skin.

Blister plane and diagnosis

Intraepidermal blistering tends to produce fragile flaccid blisters, while subepidermal cleavage produces more tense blisters. Biopsy and immunofluorescence are often needed for confirmation.

Clinical safety versus pattern recognition

A familiar-looking rash can still be dangerous if there is fever, haemodynamic compromise, mucosal involvement, rapidly progressing skin pain or disproportionate tenderness. ABCDE assessment takes priority.

OSCE reasoning and communication

The examiner assesses safe, respectful clinical process: consent, systematic examination, meaningful differential, appropriate escalation and understandable patient explanation. Avoid overclaiming diagnostic certainty.
