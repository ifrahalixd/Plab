Heart failure · Deep Dive

Cardiac anatomy and haemodynamics

Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. Stroke volume depends on preload, afterload and contractility. The left ventricle ejects into the systemic circulation; the right ventricle ejects into the low-pressure pulmonary circulation. LV dysfunction raises pulmonary venous pressure; RV dysfunction raises systemic venous pressure.

Frank–Starling and remodelling

Increasing ventricular filling initially increases contraction strength, but beyond an optimal range raises filling pressures and congestion. Persistent overload causes dilation, hypertrophy and fibrosis. An enlarged heart can generate a lower EF and an S3.

RAAS, SNS and natriuretic peptides

Reduced renal perfusion increases renin, angiotensin II and aldosterone. Vasoconstriction increases afterload; salt and water retention increase preload. Chronic sympathetic activation raises heart rate and arrhythmic risk. BNP and NT-proBNP are released with wall stress, aiding diagnosis but requiring clinical interpretation.

Cellular and metabolic changes

Abnormal calcium handling, impaired mitochondrial energy production, oxidative stress and fibrosis reduce myocardial efficiency. Ischaemic injury can leave non-contractile scar. The kidneys may retain sodium despite total-body fluid overload because effective arterial perfusion is reduced.

Pharmacology: mechanism to outcome

RAAS blockers and MRAs counter harmful neurohormonal signalling; beta-blockers counter chronic sympathetic stimulation; SGLT2 inhibitors improve HF outcomes by multiple mechanisms not fully explained by glucose lowering. Diuretics primarily relieve congestion. Do not confuse symptomatic relief with proven survival benefit.

Causes, genetics and investigations

Ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, valvular lesions, myocarditis, alcohol, tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy and infiltrative disease can cause HF. Family history of unexplained cardiomyopathy or sudden death may prompt specialist genetic assessment. ECG, echo, cardiac MRI and coronary evaluation are selected by suspected aetiology.

Bedside assessment and OSCE

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent, assess distress, take focused history and examine pulse, BP, JVP, apex, heart sounds, lungs, abdomen and legs. Explain investigations and treatment in plain language; safety-net severe breathlessness, syncope, chest pain or rapid deterioration.

Four worked cases

Post-MI low EF: assess for HFrEF, congestion and disease-modifying treatment. Rapid AF with pulmonary oedema: stabilise and treat rhythm/rate trigger. HFpEF with hypertension: confirm objective evidence and manage comorbidities. Hypotension with cold extremities: suspect shock and urgently escalate.

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome caused by structural or functional cardiac abnormalities that impair cardiac filling or output. It can develop after myocardial infarction, from longstanding hypertension, valve disease, arrhythmia or cardiomyopathy.

In 60 seconds: essentials

  • Think heart failure with breathlessness, orthopnoea, ankle swelling, fatigue and raised jugular venous pressure; signs can be absent.
  • Differentiate acute decompensation from stable chronic disease; pulmonary oedema and shock need emergency escalation.
  • Measure NT-proBNP and arrange echocardiography to assess structure and ejection fraction; a low peptide makes heart failure less likely, but interpret with context.
  • HFrEF and HFpEF are not managed identically. Congestion needs diuretics; disease-modifying treatment matters particularly in HFrEF.
  • Always look for a cause and a trigger: ACS, arrhythmia, infection, uncontrolled BP, renal dysfunction, medication non-adherence.

1. What is heart failure?

Heart failure does not mean the heart has stopped. It means cardiac function cannot meet the body's needs without abnormal filling pressures. Left-sided congestion commonly causes pulmonary symptoms; right-sided congestion causes peripheral oedema, ascites and elevated JVP. Both often coexist.

PhenotypeDefinition and significance
HFrEFReduced left ventricular ejection fraction, typically ≤40%. Strong evidence supports several disease-modifying drug classes.
HFmrEFMildly reduced EF, typically 41–49%. Investigate causes and consider phenotype-appropriate treatment.
HFpEFPreserved EF, typically ≥50%, with objective evidence of increased filling pressures or structural/functional abnormalities; preserved EF alone does not establish HF.
Acute HFNew-onset or rapidly worsening symptoms/signs requiring urgent evaluation, often due to congestion and sometimes poor perfusion.
Ejection fraction is the proportion of end-diastolic ventricular volume ejected per beat, not a direct measurement of total cardiac output. A patient with HFpEF may have severe symptoms despite a normal EF.

2. Why heart failure happens

Falling effective cardiac output stimulates the sympathetic nervous system and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS). Initially this supports blood pressure, but persistent vasoconstriction, sodium retention, tachycardia and ventricular remodelling worsen symptoms and outcomes. Natriuretic peptides are released in response to wall stretch and counteract fluid retention.

In HFrEF, impaired contraction often follows myocardial injury or dilated cardiomyopathy. In HFpEF, impaired relaxation, stiffness and elevated filling pressures are common; hypertension, obesity, atrial fibrillation and ageing frequently contribute.

3. History and examination

History

Ask about exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea (number of pillows), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, weight gain and ankle swelling. Ask about chest pain, palpitations, syncope, recent infection, alcohol, cardiotoxic drugs, prior MI, hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, sleep apnoea and family history of cardiomyopathy.

Examination

Record pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature, weight and perfusion. Look for elevated JVP, bibasal crackles, displaced apex beat, third heart sound, murmurs, hepatomegaly and pitting oedema. A patient can have HF without crackles or ankle swelling.

Red flags: severe breathlessness at rest, hypoxaemia, hypotension, altered consciousness, cold clammy extremities, chest pain, dangerous arrhythmia or rapidly worsening pulmonary oedema require urgent emergency assessment.

4. Investigations and diagnosis

Clinical suspicion

Assess symptoms, signs, comorbidities and alternative causes (COPD, pneumonia, anaemia, renal disease, venous insufficiency).

↓
NT-proBNP in suspected chronic HF

NICE NG106: refer for specialist assessment and transthoracic echo within 2 weeks if NT-proBNP >2,000 ng/L, or within 6 weeks if 400–2,000 ng/L. A value below 400 ng/L in an untreated person makes HF less likely, but does not absolutely exclude it.

↓
Echocardiography

Evaluate EF, chamber dimensions, diastolic function, valve abnormalities, right ventricular function and pulmonary pressures. Further imaging may be needed if echo is limited or cardiomyopathy is suspected.

Order ECG, FBC, renal function/electrolytes, liver function, thyroid tests, HbA1c/glucose, lipids and other tests as indicated. Chest X-ray can reveal congestion or an alternative diagnosis. BNP/NT-proBNP can be raised by age, renal impairment, atrial fibrillation and other illnesses, and lowered by obesity; treatment may also alter results.

5. Acute decompensated heart failure

Use ABCDE, monitoring, IV access and early senior involvement. Seek precipitants including ACS, rapid AF, severe hypertension, infection, pulmonary embolism and medication-related fluid retention. Check ECG, blood gases when indicated, renal function, electrolytes, troponin when ACS is suspected, and chest imaging or bedside ultrasound where appropriate.

Congested / pulmonary oedema

NICE CG187 recommends intravenous diuretic therapy; monitor urine output, weight, kidney function and electrolytes. Give oxygen for hypoxaemia, not simply for breathlessness. Consider non-invasive ventilation for severe dyspnoea with acidaemia or failure to respond to medical treatment, with experienced monitoring.

Shock or severe hypoperfusion

Immediate critical care and cardiology review; investigate cardiogenic shock and potentially reversible causes. Inotropes/vasopressors are not routine and require specialist supervision.

Avoid treating all breathlessness as fluid overload. Hypotension, sepsis, acute valvular disease or right ventricular failure may change the safest strategy. Routine nitrates and opioids are not recommended for every acute HF patient.

6. Chronic heart failure: long-term plan

Confirm phenotype and cause, relieve congestion, reduce hospitalisation and mortality where evidence supports it, treat comorbidities and arrange coordinated follow-up. For HFrEF, NICE NG106 recommends an ACE inhibitor, beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) and SGLT2 inhibitor, with individualisation for tolerability and contraindications. ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan) may be used in eligible patients according to NICE criteria and specialist advice.

For HFpEF, address blood pressure, atrial fibrillation, obesity, diabetes, sleep apnoea and congestion; consider an SGLT2 inhibitor in line with current NICE technology appraisals and patient eligibility. Avoid assuming the entire HFrEF regimen has identical evidence in HFpEF.

Verify specific doses, titration schedules, renal cut-offs and contraindications in the current BNF and relevant NICE guidance before prescribing. Review interactions with NSAIDs and other medicines that may worsen fluid retention or kidney function.

8. Monitoring, devices and prevention

Reassess symptoms, weight, volume status, BP, pulse, kidney function and electrolytes after medication initiation or dose changes. Offer cardiac rehabilitation, vaccination advice, smoking cessation, exercise guidance and patient education on worsening symptoms. Discuss a personalised action plan and when to seek urgent help.

Some patients with persistent low EF or electrical dyssynchrony benefit from implantable cardioverter-defibrillators or cardiac resynchronisation therapy after appropriate optimisation and specialist evaluation. Consider iron deficiency, sleep-disordered breathing, renal disease and palliative/supportive needs where relevant.

9. Clinical reasoning and PLAB practice

Case A · Breathlessness and ankle swelling

A 69-year-old with previous MI has progressive exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea and bilateral oedema. Assess ABCDE and stability, investigate with NT-proBNP and echo, check ECG, renal function and causes, then treat congestion and initiate phenotype-appropriate therapy after confirmation.

Case B · Sudden severe breathlessness

A patient is hypoxaemic, tachypnoeic and has widespread crackles. Treat as an emergency: ABCDE, oxygen for hypoxaemia, monitoring, IV diuretic if congested, search for ACS/arrhythmia, and escalate; consider NIV if indicated.

Case C · Normal ejection fraction

A patient with obesity, hypertension and AF has exertional dyspnoea and a preserved EF. HFpEF remains possible; seek objective evidence of elevated filling pressures and assess alternatives. Treat congestion and comorbidities.

    Heart failure · Clinical notes

    Heart failure causes congestion and/or reduced cardiac output. Distinguish HFrEF, HFmrEF, HFpEF and acute decompensation. Chronic suspected HF: NT-proBNP >2,000 ng/L merits specialist review and echo within 2 weeks; 400–2,000 ng/L within 6 weeks. Echo confirms phenotype and helps identify cause. Acute pulmonary oedema needs ABCDE, senior escalation, oxygen if hypoxaemic and IV diuretics when congested. Chronic HFrEF treatment combines symptom control with evidence-based neurohormonal and SGLT2 therapy. Monitor renal function, potassium, BP and pulse. Do not assume a normal EF excludes HF.
