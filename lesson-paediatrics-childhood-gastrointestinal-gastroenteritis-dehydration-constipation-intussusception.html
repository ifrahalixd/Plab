Childhood Gastrointestinal Conditions: Gastroenteritis, Dehydration, Constipation & Intussusception

Red flags: Shock, reduced consciousness, bilious vomiting, peritonism, severe dehydration or bloody diarrhoea with reduced urine output require urgent assessment.

Initial assessment

Assess ABCDE, vital signs, capillary refill, mental state, hydration, weight and glucose if unwell. Ask about onset, stool frequency, blood or mucus, vomiting colour, urine output, fever, pain and contacts.

PLAB application: Shock, altered consciousness, bilious vomiting and peritonism require urgent paediatric assessment.

Acute gastroenteritis

Usually viral in young children; causes diarrhoea with or without vomiting. Norovirus and rotavirus are common causes. Consider bacterial infection if bloody diarrhoea, high fever or travel exposure.

PLAB application: A child with bloody diarrhoea needs consideration of invasive infection and STEC-associated HUS.

Dehydration assessment

Clinical dehydration may cause reduced urine, dry mucosa, sunken eyes, tachycardia, reduced skin turgor and irritability. Shock causes poor perfusion, weak pulses, prolonged capillary refill, hypotension (late) or reduced consciousness.

PLAB application: Normal blood pressure does not exclude serious dehydration in children.

Oral rehydration

Use reduced-osmolarity oral rehydration solution (ORS) in clinical dehydration, offered frequently in small volumes. Continue breastfeeding and usual milk feeds. Replace ongoing stool losses as appropriate.

PLAB application: NICE CG84 advises ORS 50 mL/kg over 4 hours for clinical dehydration, plus maintenance and losses; tailor to assessment.

IV or nasogastric fluids

Consider nasogastric ORS if a child cannot drink enough or persistently vomits; IV fluids are indicated for shock, deterioration despite ORS or persistent vomiting of ORS by oral/NG route. Follow local paediatric fluid protocols and monitor electrolytes.

PLAB application: In shock, urgent IV isotonic fluid resuscitation with reassessment and senior help; do not apply routine oral plans to shock.

Feeding and medications

Continue breastfeeding. After rehydration, resume normal diet. Avoid fruit juice and fizzy drinks during diarrhoea. Antibiotics are not routine for uncomplicated gastroenteritis; antidiarrhoeal medicines are not recommended.

PLAB application: Do not routinely prescribe loperamide for young children with gastroenteritis.

Investigations and infection control

Stool testing is selective: bloody/mucoid stool, immunocompromise, suspected septicaemia, travel or persistent illness may warrant testing. Blood tests are not routine in mild cases; check glucose/electrolytes if IV fluids or severe illness.

PLAB application: Use hand hygiene and infection-control advice; return to school/nursery at least 48 hours after last diarrhoea or vomiting.

Safety-netting gastroenteritis

Advise urgent reassessment for lethargy, reduced responsiveness, very low urine output, persistent vomiting, blood in stool, worsening pain, bilious vomit or signs of dehydration.

PLAB application: Explain expected course: diarrhoea often 5–7 days, vomiting usually 1–2 days; prolonged symptoms need review.

Functional constipation

Common in childhood. Clues include infrequent hard stools, painful defecation, withholding, large stools and overflow soiling. Examine growth, abdomen and perianal area where appropriate.

PLAB application: Overflow soiling can be constipation, not true diarrhoea.

Constipation red flags

Delayed passage of meconium beyond 48 hours in term neonates, ribbon stools, severe distension with vomiting, faltering growth, neurological signs or abnormal anus suggest organic pathology.

PLAB application: Do not treat suspected Hirschsprung disease or bowel obstruction as simple functional constipation.

Disimpaction and maintenance

If faecal impaction is present, use an escalating-dose oral macrogol regimen according to age and BNFC/NICE CG99. After disimpaction, give maintenance macrogol with gradual adjustment and regular review.

PLAB application: Laxative treatment often continues for months; do not stop abruptly once stooling improves.

Behaviour and diet in constipation

Use scheduled relaxed toilet sitting after meals, foot support, positive reinforcement and a stool diary. Encourage age-appropriate fluid, fibre and activity, but diet alone is not sufficient treatment for established constipation.

PLAB application: Avoid blaming or punishing children for soiling.

Intussusception

Telescoping of bowel, usually ileocolic, commonly in infants and toddlers. Causes intermittent severe colicky pain with leg drawing, pallor, vomiting and episodes of lethargy; a sausage-shaped mass may be present.

PLAB application: Redcurrant jelly stool is a late and unreliable sign: do not wait for it.

Intussusception diagnosis and treatment

Urgent ultrasound is the preferred diagnostic test. Stabilise with IV access, fluids, analgesia and surgical/radiology involvement. Air or contrast enema reduction is often first-line when stable and no perforation/peritonitis; surgery if contraindicated or unsuccessful.

PLAB application: Bilious vomiting, peritonism or shock demands immediate surgical assessment.

Other surgical differentials

Consider appendicitis (migratory right iliac fossa pain), malrotation/volvulus (bilious vomiting), pyloric stenosis (progressive non-bilious projectile vomiting in young infants), incarcerated hernia and testicular torsion.

PLAB application: Green bilious vomiting in an infant is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise.

PLAB 2 communication

Ask about intake, wet nappies, stool characteristics, vomiting, fever, travel, medicines, weight and parental concerns. Explain hydration plans clearly, demonstrate ORS administration and give specific safety-net advice.

PLAB application: Use teach-back: ask the caregiver to describe when they would seek urgent help.

PLAB 2 — Parent consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm child and caregiver details, assess immediate stability, take a focused hydration and bowel history, explore concerns, explain likely diagnosis and management, check understanding and safety-net. Escalate urgently if the child is unwell.

Deep Dive

Dehydration versus shock

Assess overall appearance, responsiveness, pulse quality, capillary refill, respiratory pattern, mucosa and urine output. Hypotension is a late sign in paediatric shock.

ORS dosing and escalation

NICE CG84 recommends 50 mL/kg ORS over 4 hours for clinical dehydration in children under 5, alongside maintenance needs; re-evaluate regularly. Shock requires immediate emergency resuscitation.

Constipation disimpaction

Macrogol with electrolytes is first-line for faecal impaction and maintenance; dosing varies by age and formulation. Continue maintenance for weeks to months and adjust against symptoms.

Intussusception mechanism

Venous congestion and bowel oedema can progress to ischaemia and perforation. Episodic pain and lethargy may precede bloody stools; ultrasound supports diagnosis.

HUS after bloody diarrhoea

Shiga toxin-associated HUS can cause microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury. Check FBC, renal function and urine output urgently if suspected.

Shared decision-making

Provide practical ORS instructions, written red flags, school/nursery infection advice and a clear follow-up plan; account for caregiver ability to provide fluids and observe the child.
