Consent: what the GMC expects

Consent is a fundamental legal and ethical principle. Patients have a right to participate in decisions about their care and receive the information and support needed to decide. Meaningful dialogue is more important than simply completing a form.

The seven GMC principles

  1. Patients have a right to be involved and supported to make informed decisions.
  2. Decision-making is an ongoing, individualised dialogue.
  3. Patients must be listened to and given appropriate information, time and support.
  4. Explore what matters to each patient; explain benefits, harms, reasonable alternatives and no treatment.
  5. Presume adults have capacity; assess capacity for a particular decision at a particular time when needed.
  6. For patients lacking capacity, decisions must be of overall benefit, with consultation of those close to them or advocating for them.
  7. Where law affects the right to consent, involve the patient and support choice as far as possible.

What to explain

Diagnosis and prognosis (including uncertainty); available options; what each option involves; expected benefits and likelihood of success; common and serious harms; reasonable alternatives; the option of no action; and any patient-specific consequences. Explore the patient's own goals, concerns and priorities.

PLAB anchor: A risk can be material because the patient would consider it important, even if its statistical likelihood is low. The GMC asks clinicians to discuss serious harms however unlikely, alongside patient-specific concerns.

Consent in practice

Verbal consent is often sufficient; non-verbal consent can be appropriate for some simple examinations, provided you explain what you are doing and stop if the patient objects. Record consent appropriately. More invasive interventions may require formal documentation, but a signature alone does not prove informed consent.

Refusal and changing one's mind

Respect an adult patient's capacitous choice, even if you consider it unwise. Check understanding, offer information and support without coercion, and document the discussion. Revisit consent before treatment, especially when circumstances or risks change.

Emergency exception: A conscious patient is presumed to have capacity and consent should be sought. If the patient lacks capacity and immediate treatment is necessary to save life or prevent serious deterioration, treatment may be given when their wishes cannot be established, using the least restrictive appropriate option.

A practical consent framework

  1. Identify the decision: What procedure, investigation or treatment is proposed?
  2. Check the patient's understanding: What do they already know?
  3. Presume capacity: If there is a genuine concern, arrange a decision-specific assessment under applicable law.
  4. Explore priorities: What outcomes, fears, beliefs or daily activities matter to them?
  5. Explain options: Benefits, risks, uncertainty, reasonable alternatives and no treatment.
  6. Support understanding: Use plain language, interpreter, accessible materials and time as needed.
  7. Check voluntariness: Ask privately if family or others appear to be pressuring the patient.
  8. Invite a decision: Avoid pressure; clarify the scope of agreement.
  9. Record and revisit: Document key information, preferences and decisions; check again before treatment.
Key distinction: Capacity, adequate information and voluntariness are separate questions. Do not assume that agreement automatically establishes all three.

Common exam traps

  • “The family signed, so the patient need not be asked” — incorrect for an adult who has capacity.
  • “The patient refused, therefore lacks capacity” — incorrect.
  • “The consent form is signed, so discussion is unnecessary” — incorrect.
  • “All possible risks must be listed” — incorrect; tailor discussion to material and serious risks.
  • “Consent obtained last month is always enough today” — incorrect; review where appropriate.

Clinical reasoning cases

Case 1 · The pianist

A 42-year-old concert pianist is offered a procedure with a small risk of permanent finger weakness. The surgeon describes only common complications.

Best approach: Discuss the finger weakness risk because of its importance to this patient's occupation and priorities, alongside alternatives and no intervention.

Case 2 · Refusing admission

A patient with a serious infection understands the diagnosis and consequences but declines admission.

Best approach: Explore the reasons, assess capacity if there is a genuine concern, explain risks and alternatives, offer safety-netting and document. Do not equate an unwise choice with incapacity.

Case 3 · Family pressure

A patient agrees to surgery while their relative answers every question and insists they proceed.

Best approach: Speak with the patient privately, assess whether their choice is free, provide information and support, and avoid proceeding without valid consent.

Case 4 · Deteriorating unconscious patient

An unconscious patient needs immediate lifesaving treatment and their wishes cannot be established in time.

Best approach: Provide immediately necessary treatment under the applicable emergency framework; consider any known advance wishes, choose the least restrictive appropriate option and explain later if the patient regains capacity.

PLAB 2 simulated station

8-minute consultation

Station: Patient declines a recommended operation

Candidate brief: You are an FY2 doctor. A 55-year-old patient with symptomatic gallstones has been advised to consider elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy but is reluctant. Discuss their decision and agree a safe plan. You are not expected to perform a procedure.

Suggested consultation flow

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, establish the patient's understanding.
  2. Ask open questions: “What worries you most about the operation?”
  3. Explore ideas, concerns, expectations, daily priorities and relevant circumstances.
  4. Explain the recommended option, benefits, common and serious risks, uncertainty, alternatives and no surgery.
  5. Check understanding and allow questions; avoid pressuring the patient.
  6. Respect an informed decision, agree follow-up and safety-net for fever, jaundice, persistent severe pain or deterioration.
  7. Document the discussion and communicate with the supervising team.

Useful phrases

“It is your decision. My role is to explain the options and help you decide what is right for you.”
“Would you tell me what matters most to you when weighing the benefits and risks?”
“Could you tell me in your own words what you understand about the options?”
