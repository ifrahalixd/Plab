Renal Examination, Urinalysis & Kidney Function Interpretation

UKMLA / PLAB-focused renal bedside examination, urine testing and interpretation.

01. Clinical priorities

Assess ABCDE if unwell; ask about urine output, pain, fever, swelling, breathlessness, haematuria and drug exposures. AKI, sepsis, hyperkalaemia and obstruction are time-critical.

02. Focused renal history

Ask about dysuria, frequency, loin pain, frothy urine, visible blood, oliguria, nocturia, thirst, fluid loss, weight change, diabetes, hypertension and previous renal disease.

PLAB application: Link findings to safe investigation and escalation.

03. Risk factors and medicines

Review NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics, aminoglycosides, contrast, lithium and herbal remedies; consider dehydration, diabetes, vascular disease, family history and systemic illness.

PLAB application: Link findings to safe investigation and escalation.

04. General inspection

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent and check pain. Observe distress, pallor, scratch marks, bruising, oedema, fluid balance charts and any dialysis access.

PLAB application: Link findings to safe investigation and escalation.

05. Observations and fluid status

Measure blood pressure, pulse, temperature, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation and weight. Assess mucous membranes, capillary refill, postural symptoms, JVP, lung crackles and peripheral oedema; interpret together.

06. Hands, face and chest

Look for pallor, nail changes, excoriations, periorbital oedema and evidence of systemic disease. Examine heart and lungs for fluid overload; inspect fistula and check thrill/bruit when appropriate.

07. Abdominal and renal examination

Inspect abdomen for scars, distension and masses. Palpate gently for tenderness, bladder distension and ballotable kidneys; assess renal angle tenderness. Auscultate for bruits when indicated.

08. Completing the OSCE

Thank the patient, ensure comfort, wash hands and report findings. Propose urine dipstick, ACR, blood tests, ECG when potassium abnormal, imaging for obstruction and specialist assessment when indicated.

09. Urine dipstick interpretation

Leucocyte esterase and nitrites support possible bacterial UTI in the right context; blood may reflect RBCs, haemoglobin or myoglobin; protein needs quantification. Glucose and ketones suggest metabolic abnormalities.

PLAB application: Link findings to safe investigation and escalation.

10. Urine microscopy and culture

Culture is useful when pyelonephritis, complicated infection, pregnancy or treatment failure is suspected. Red-cell casts suggest glomerular bleeding; white-cell casts can occur in renal inflammation.

PLAB application: Link findings to safe investigation and escalation.

11. Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio

Use urine ACR to quantify albuminuria. A1 <3 mg/mmol, A2 3–30 mg/mmol, A3 >30 mg/mmol. Confirm a new ACR of 3–70 mg/mmol with an early-morning sample when clinically appropriate.

12. Creatinine, eGFR and urea

Creatinine depends on muscle mass and changes slowly after acute injury. eGFR is less reliable when kidney function is rapidly changing. Urea rises with reduced filtration but also dehydration, GI bleeding and catabolism.

13. AKI recognition and staging

AKI criteria include creatinine rise ≥26 micromol/L in 48 hours, ≥50% within 7 days or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for >6 hours in adults. Check previous values, volume, drugs and obstruction.

14. CKD classification

CKD requires kidney abnormality for at least 3 months. Classify by cause, GFR category G1–G5 and albuminuria A1–A3. G1–G2 require evidence of kidney damage; assess cardiovascular risk.

15. Imaging and further tests

Renal ultrasound assesses hydronephrosis and anatomy. CT may be appropriate for stones; immunology tests and biopsy are specialist-led for suspected glomerulonephritis. Choose tests by clinical question.

16. Red flags and escalation

Urgent escalation for severe hyperkalaemia, pulmonary oedema, anuria, obstructed infected kidney, rapidly progressive renal impairment or nephritic features with systemic illness. Safety-net worsening urine output, breathlessness and fever.

Deep Dive

Filtration and creatinine

Glomerular filtration depends on renal perfusion and the integrity of the filtration barrier. Serum creatinine lags behind acute changes and is affected by muscle mass.

Why ACR matters

Albuminuria is a marker of kidney damage and predicts renal and cardiovascular outcomes; ACR helps classify CKD risk.

Dipstick limitations

Dipsticks are screening tests, not diagnoses. Concentrated urine, contamination and timing affect results; interpret with microscopy and quantitative tests.

Oedema mechanisms

Salt and water retention and low plasma oncotic pressure can contribute to swelling. Distinguish overload from intravascular depletion.

Urine sediment

Dysmorphic RBCs and red-cell casts suggest glomerular bleeding; crystals, leukocytes and bacteria need clinical interpretation.

Clinical reasoning

A stable isolated low eGFR differs from an acutely rising creatinine with oliguria. Trends, urine findings and patient condition determine urgency.
