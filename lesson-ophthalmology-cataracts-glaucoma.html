Cataracts & Glaucoma

Emergency red flag: severe painful red eye with haloes, nausea/vomiting and reduced vision suggests acute angle-closure glaucoma. Immediate ophthalmology assessment.

Overview

Cataract is opacity of the crystalline lens causing gradual painless visual impairment. Glaucoma is a group of optic neuropathies with characteristic optic nerve damage and visual field loss; intraocular pressure is an important modifiable risk factor.

PLAB takeaway: Do not confuse gradual open-angle glaucoma with acute angle closure.

Lens and aqueous anatomy

The lens focuses light onto the retina. Aqueous humour is produced by the ciliary body and drains largely through the trabecular meshwork at the anterior chamber angle.

PLAB takeaway: Angle closure impedes aqueous drainage and can rapidly elevate pressure.

Cataract symptoms

Progressive painless blurred vision, glare from headlights, reduced contrast, altered colour perception and frequent spectacle changes are common.

PLAB takeaway: A cataract does not typically cause a painful red eye.

Cataract risk factors

Age, diabetes, smoking, ultraviolet exposure, ocular trauma and prolonged corticosteroid use can increase cataract risk.

PLAB takeaway: Ask how vision affects reading, driving, falls and independence.

Cataract examination

Assess visual acuity and red reflex; lens opacity may be seen with slit-lamp examination. Examine for other causes of visual loss.

PLAB takeaway: A reduced red reflex is not specific to cataract; urgent referral may be needed in children.

Cataract management

Update spectacles and optimise lighting when helpful. Definitive treatment is surgical lens extraction with intraocular lens implantation when visual impairment affects quality of life.

PLAB takeaway: NICE NG77 advises not restricting access to cataract surgery solely by visual-acuity thresholds.

Cataract surgery counselling

Explain benefits, realistic expectations and risks including infection, inflammation, retinal detachment, posterior capsule opacification and refractive surprise.

PLAB takeaway: A painful red eye or worsening vision after surgery needs urgent assessment.

Primary open-angle glaucoma

Usually chronic, painless and asymptomatic until peripheral visual field loss develops. Optic nerve damage may occur even with pressures in the statistically normal range.

PLAB takeaway: Screening solely by symptoms misses early disease.

Glaucoma risk factors

Increasing age, family history, higher intraocular pressure, certain ancestries, myopia and corticosteroid exposure can increase risk.

PLAB takeaway: A first-degree relative with glaucoma is important history.

Glaucoma assessment

Comprehensive assessment includes Goldmann-type applanation tonometry, optic nerve evaluation/OCT, visual fields, anterior chamber angle assessment and central corneal thickness where appropriate.

PLAB takeaway: NICE NG81 outlines specialist diagnostic assessment and monitoring.

Open-angle glaucoma treatment

Pressure-lowering eye drops are common; NICE NG81 recommends selective laser trabeculoplasty as initial treatment for many eligible patients with newly diagnosed non-advanced chronic open-angle glaucoma.

PLAB takeaway: Treatment is individualised by severity, target pressure, preference and contraindications.

Eye-drop counselling

Demonstrate correct technique, avoid touching bottle tip, use punctal occlusion if appropriate and space different drops. Discuss adherence and adverse effects.

PLAB takeaway: Topical beta-blockers may be unsuitable in asthma or significant bradycardia.

Acute angle-closure glaucoma

Severe unilateral ocular pain, red eye, blurred vision/haloes, headache, nausea or vomiting, corneal haze and a mid-dilated poorly reactive pupil suggest acute angle closure.

PLAB takeaway: Immediate emergency ophthalmology referral is essential; do not delay for routine tests.

Acute angle closure treatment

Emergency specialist management lowers intraocular pressure using appropriate medicines, followed by definitive laser peripheral iridotomy when suitable.

PLAB takeaway: Avoid attempting unsupervised definitive treatment in primary care.

Differentials and emergencies

Sudden painless visual loss suggests retinal vascular occlusion, retinal detachment or vitreous haemorrhage. Painful visual loss suggests keratitis, uveitis or angle closure.

PLAB takeaway: New flashes, floaters or curtain-like field loss warrant urgent retinal assessment.

PLAB 2 consultation

Take onset, pain, glare, haloes, family history, steroid use, driving and daily impact. Check visual acuity, pupils and red reflex, and refer appropriately.

PLAB takeaway: Explain cataract surgery versus long-term glaucoma monitoring and safety-net clearly.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Visual loss

  1. Introduce yourself, check identity, consent and assess immediate visual threats.
  2. Ask onset, pain, glare, haloes, medication/steroid use, family history and effect on driving or daily living.
  3. Check visual acuity separately, pupils and red reflex; explain further eye assessment.
  4. For cataract, discuss functional impact and surgery choices; for glaucoma, discuss long-term monitoring and adherence.
  5. Safety-net: sudden painful red eye, rapid loss of vision or new postoperative pain needs emergency review.

Deep Dive

Why cataracts cause glare

Lens opacities scatter incoming light, causing halos and loss of contrast even when measured visual acuity seems acceptable.

Why glaucoma is often missed

Peripheral visual field loss may progress unnoticed until optic nerve damage is advanced.

IOP is not the whole diagnosis

Some people with high pressure do not have glaucoma; some with glaucoma have pressures within the normal range.

Trabecular outflow

Reduced aqueous outflow raises intraocular pressure; laser and medicines aim to improve outflow or reduce production.

SLT versus drops

Selective laser trabeculoplasty can reduce pressure and treatment burden for eligible patients; monitoring remains essential.

Angle closure mechanism

A narrowed or obstructed anterior chamber angle abruptly prevents aqueous drainage and can injure the optic nerve.

Postoperative endophthalmitis

Pain, redness and vision deterioration after surgery may signal intraocular infection and need emergency assessment.
