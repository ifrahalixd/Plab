Mental Health Law: UK clinical practice

Learning outcomes: Distinguish Mental Health Act (MHA) detention from Mental Capacity Act (MCA) decisions; recognise emergency assessment and detention pathways; know patient safeguards, treatment rights and documentation; apply these principles in PLAB scenarios.

1. Two different legal questions

Mental Health Act 1983 (England and Wales): provides powers for assessment, detention and, in defined circumstances, treatment of mental disorder without consent, subject to safeguards. Mental Capacity Act 2005 (England and Wales): provides a framework for decision-specific capacity assessment and decisions for adults who lack capacity. A person may have capacity yet meet MHA detention criteria; a person may lack capacity without needing MHA detention.

Legislative update: The Mental Health Act 2025 reforms the law in England and Wales, with provisions commencing in stages. Always check the commencement and current Code of Practice before applying a specific rule. This lesson teaches the established section framework and flags reforms rather than assuming every change is in force.

3. Admission under Sections 2 and 3

Ordinarily, applications involve an Approved Mental Health Professional (AMHP) or nearest relative and recommendations from two doctors, one with appropriate specialist approval where required. The statutory criteria and procedural safeguards must be met; detention is not automatic because someone is suicidal, psychotic or refuses care. Consider necessity, risk, less restrictive alternatives and appropriate treatment.

4. Capacity, consent and treatment

Assess capacity for the particular decision; do not equate a psychiatric diagnosis with incapacity. Under the MHA, specified treatment for mental disorder may be authorised despite refusal, subject to statutory rules and safeguards. Treatment for an unrelated physical illness generally requires consent or another lawful basis, such as MCA best-interests provisions when capacity is absent.

5. Rights and safeguards

Patients should receive information about detention and rights, have access to advocacy (including IMHA where eligible), and may have routes to challenge detention through a tribunal. Consider nearest-relative provisions, appropriate review and careful documentation. Restrictive interventions must be necessary, proportionate and least restrictive.

6. Across the UK

Scotland uses the Mental Health (Care and Treatment) (Scotland) Act 2003; Northern Ireland uses separate mental health and capacity legislation. Do not automatically apply England-and-Wales section numbers throughout the UK.

UK mental health decision pathways

  1. Immediate danger? Assess physical safety, self-harm, violence, intoxication, delirium and urgent medical causes. Call senior/psychiatric help and emergency services as needed.
  2. Assess the clinical picture. Mental state examination, collateral history when appropriate, risk assessment and alternatives to detention.
  3. Can the person consent to proposed care? Capacity is decision-specific; support decision-making. A capacitous refusal is not by itself grounds for detention.
  4. Is MHA assessment necessary? Request appropriate statutory assessment (often via AMHP/crisis team) rather than independently 'sectioning' someone as a junior doctor.
  5. Already an inpatient and trying to leave? Consider urgent senior review and whether a valid holding power applies; Section 5(2) is not for someone who has not been admitted.
  6. After any restriction: Explain rights, document legal authority and clinical reasoning, review frequently and use least restrictive options.

PLAB discriminator: MCA best-interests treatment ≠ MHA detention. Neither can be used as a shortcut to avoid the other's safeguards.

Four clinical reasoning cases

Case 1 · Voluntary patient requests discharge

A patient admitted voluntarily for severe depression asks to leave and is at immediate high risk of suicide. Approach: Do not assume voluntary status can be ignored. Arrange urgent senior/psychiatric assessment, consider legal holding powers only if eligibility and criteria are met, and use safe de-escalation.

Case 2 · Psychosis with capacity

A patient with psychosis understands the proposed antipsychotic treatment and refuses. Approach: Capacity alone does not determine whether MHA criteria are met. Evaluate risk, statutory criteria, alternatives and rights; do not automatically force medication.

Case 3 · Delirium and pneumonia

An older adult with sepsis is delirious and lacks capacity to decide about IV antibiotics. Approach: Treat the medical emergency and use the relevant capacity/best-interests framework; a psychiatric detention section is not automatically indicated.

Case 4 · Police bring patient to place of safety

Police use Section 136 following concerning behaviour in a public place. Approach: Ensure medical assessment, monitor physical health and intoxication, and arrange timely statutory mental health assessment. Section 136 does not itself authorise ongoing hospital detention or blanket treatment.

PLAB 2 simulated station · Request to leave hospital

Station: You are a junior doctor. A voluntary inpatient with depression says, “I'm leaving now,” after expressing suicidal intent.

Suggested communication

“I can hear that you want to leave, and I want to understand what's making staying difficult. I'm worried about your safety after what you've told us. Could we talk about what you might do if you leave? I'd like to involve the senior mental health team urgently and explain your options and rights.”

Examiner priorities

Empathy; immediate risk assessment; assess medical and psychiatric urgency; explain rather than threaten; involve senior staff and appropriate legal professionals; do not claim an automatic power to detain; document and arrange safe observation according to local policy.
