Vertigo: BPPV, Ménière Disease & Vestibular Neuritis

PLAB emergency: Acute continuous vertigo with central signs, severe gait ataxia or new neurological deficits may be posterior circulation stroke.

Approach to dizziness

Clarify vertigo (illusion of movement), presyncope, imbalance and nonspecific dizziness. Establish timing, triggers, duration and associated symptoms.

Clinical application: Do not assume all dizziness is vestibular; check vital signs, medication, cardiovascular and neurological features.

Peripheral versus central vertigo

Peripheral disorders often produce positional or episodic vertigo; central causes include posterior circulation stroke. Neither symptom severity nor nausea reliably excludes stroke.

Clinical application: New focal neurology, severe gait ataxia or concerning acute vestibular syndrome needs emergency assessment.

BPPV mechanism

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo occurs when displaced otoconia enter a semicircular canal, usually the posterior canal.

Clinical application: Brief spinning triggered by rolling in bed, looking up or bending over is typical.

BPPV presentation

Episodes usually last seconds, generally less than a minute, and are triggered by specific head movements. Between attacks patients may feel unsteady.

Clinical application: Persistent continuous vertigo is not typical of uncomplicated BPPV.

Dix–Hallpike test

With consent, move from sitting to supine with the head turned and extended; observe for characteristic delayed, transient torsional/upbeating nystagmus in posterior canal BPPV.

Clinical application: Consider cervical instability or other contraindications before testing; atypical findings need further assessment.

Epley manoeuvre

Canalith repositioning (Epley) is first-line for confirmed posterior canal BPPV when suitable, performed by a trained clinician.

Clinical application: Reassess symptoms and provide fall precautions; recurrent or atypical symptoms warrant review.

Ménière disease

A disorder characterised by spontaneous recurrent vertigo, fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness.

Clinical application: Vertigo episodes typically last 20 minutes to 12 hours in definite Ménière disease.

Ménière assessment

Arrange audiometry and specialist ENT/audiovestibular assessment. Consider vestibular migraine and other causes.

Clinical application: No single blood test confirms Ménière disease; document hearing symptoms and episode duration.

Ménière management

Specialist-led care may include education, individual trigger management, hearing support and selected preventive treatments; manage acute attacks symptomatically where appropriate.

Clinical application: Betahistine may be considered in specialist practice, but evidence is uncertain; do not present it as a guaranteed cure.

Vestibular neuritis

Usually causes sudden sustained vertigo lasting days with nausea, spontaneous nystagmus and gait unsteadiness, but without new hearing loss.

Clinical application: Acute vestibular syndrome can also be a stroke: assess carefully rather than diagnosing neuritis by symptoms alone.

Labyrinthitis

Acute vestibular symptoms with hearing loss suggest labyrinthitis or another cochleovestibular process.

Clinical application: New sudden hearing loss needs urgent specialist assessment; do not casually label it viral labyrinthitis.

HINTS examination

Head impulse, nystagmus and test of skew can help distinguish peripheral from central causes in selected patients with ongoing acute vestibular syndrome and spontaneous nystagmus.

Clinical application: HINTS should be used only by clinicians specifically trained in it; it is not a screening test for episodic BPPV.

Posterior circulation stroke

Look for new diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, limb weakness, sensory loss, severe truncal ataxia or other central signs. Stroke can occasionally present without obvious focal deficits.

Clinical application: Suspected stroke: emergency stroke pathway and urgent imaging/assessment; do not delay for bedside manoeuvres.

Vestibular migraine

Recurrent vertigo or dizziness associated with migraine history and migraine features, sometimes without headache during attacks.

Clinical application: Consider when episodic vertigo does not fit BPPV or Ménière disease.

Treatment principles

Short courses of vestibular suppressants/antiemetics may be used for severe acute symptoms when appropriate; prolonged use can impede vestibular compensation. Vestibular rehabilitation can help persistent imbalance.

Clinical application: Falls assessment, hydration and driving/work safety advice are important.

PLAB 2 counselling and safety-net

Explore attack triggers, duration, hearing changes, migraine symptoms, neurological symptoms and impact. Explain positional manoeuvres and follow-up in plain language.

Clinical application: Seek emergency help for new weakness, speech difficulty, double vision, inability to stand or walk, severe new headache or sudden hearing loss.

PLAB 2 OSCE: dizziness and vertigo

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and assess stability.
  2. Clarify timing, triggers, duration, hearing symptoms, headache, migraine and neurological symptoms.
  3. Examine vital signs, ears, cranial nerves, eye movements, coordination and gait; choose positional tests safely.
  4. Explain the working diagnosis, uncertainty, management and expected course.
  5. Safety-net for new focal symptoms, inability to walk, severe headache or sudden hearing loss.

Deep Dive

Vestibulo-ocular reflex

The vestibular system stabilises gaze during head movement. Dysfunction can cause nystagmus and oscillopsia.

Canalithiasis

Free-floating otoconia create abnormal endolymph movement with positional changes, causing transient vertigo and nystagmus.

Endolymphatic hydrops

Ménière disease is associated with altered inner-ear fluid homeostasis, but mechanisms and treatment response vary.

Acute vestibular syndrome

A continuous syndrome of vertigo, nystagmus, nausea and gait instability can arise from vestibular neuritis or posterior circulation stroke.

HINTS limitations

HINTS accuracy depends on patient selection and specialist training; misuse can miss strokes.

Vestibular compensation

Central adaptation after peripheral vestibular injury is supported by mobilisation and tailored rehabilitation.
