Adolescent Medicine: Puberty, Eating Disorders, Mental Health & Confidentiality

Red flags: Imminent suicide risk, severe self-harm, eating-disorder medical instability, sexual exploitation, acute intoxication or safeguarding danger require urgent escalation.

Adolescent-centred consultation

Begin by introducing yourself, establishing rapport and asking how the young person prefers to be addressed. Offer time alone as appropriate, explain confidentiality and its limits, and use non-judgemental language. Assess capacity for each decision rather than assuming age determines understanding.

PLAB application: Ask permission before sensitive questions; make clear when safety concerns require sharing information.

HEADSSS psychosocial history

Explore Home, Education or employment, Activities, Drugs and alcohol, Sexual health and relationships, Suicide or mood, and Safety. Adapt the order to the person and the presenting concern. Ask about online harms, bullying, exploitation, identity and support networks without assumptions.

PLAB application: A private HEADSSS history can uncover risks missed in a parent-led consultation.

Normal puberty and Tanner staging

Puberty follows activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. Girls usually begin with breast budding and boys with testicular enlargement. Tanner stages describe physical development, but normal timing varies. Discuss growth velocity, menstrual history, family patterns and psychosocial impact.

PLAB application: Avoid diagnosing delayed puberty from height alone; examine growth trajectory and pubertal development.

Early and delayed puberty

Consider precocious puberty when secondary sexual characteristics start before age 8 in girls or 9 in boys. Delayed puberty may be suspected if no breast development by 13 in girls or no testicular enlargement by 14 in boys. Review growth, nutrition, chronic illness, medication and family history; arrange specialist assessment where indicated.

PLAB application: Neurological symptoms with early puberty or significant growth failure need timely specialist review.

Menstrual health and adolescent gynaecology

Ask about menarche, cycle regularity, bleeding volume, pain, pregnancy possibility and functional impact. Early cycles can be irregular, but severe bleeding, anaemia, persistent amenorrhoea or marked pain warrants investigation. Respect confidentiality while considering safeguarding and sexual health needs.

PLAB application: Always consider pregnancy sensitively when clinically relevant, with consent and appropriate safeguarding.

Eating disorders: recognition

Anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder and ARFID can occur at any body size. Look for rapid weight loss, restrictive intake, purging, compulsive exercise, fainting, menstrual changes, dental erosion and family concern. A normal BMI does not exclude severe illness.

PLAB application: Use NICE NG69 principles; avoid reassuring on BMI alone.

Eating disorder medical instability

Check pulse, postural symptoms, blood pressure, temperature, hydration, ECG and electrolytes when clinically indicated. Bradycardia, arrhythmia, syncope, significant electrolyte disturbance, dehydration, rapid weight loss or acute food refusal can require urgent hospital assessment. Refeeding carries electrolyte risks and needs specialist planning.

PLAB application: Escalate suspected medical instability immediately; do not wait for routine outpatient review.

Eating disorder management

Provide a compassionate, non-stigmatising assessment and refer promptly to a specialist children and young people eating-disorder service. Family-based approaches are often central for younger patients; psychological therapy, physical monitoring and nutritional rehabilitation are coordinated. Avoid presenting diet advice as a substitute for specialist treatment.

PLAB application: Ask directly about purging, laxatives, exercise and self-harm while preserving dignity.

Depression and anxiety

Symptoms can include irritability, low mood, withdrawal, declining school performance, sleep change, somatic complaints and loss of enjoyment. Assess duration, severity, functioning, substance use, neurodevelopmental needs and psychosocial stressors. Use developmentally appropriate communication and shared planning.

PLAB application: Ask about self-harm and suicide directly; doing so does not implant the idea.

Self-harm and suicide risk

Ask about thoughts of death, intent, plans, access to means, previous attempts, recent self-harm, protective factors and immediate safety. A risk score must not replace a comprehensive psychosocial assessment. Urgent mental-health and emergency services are needed when there is imminent risk or serious injury.

PLAB application: Do not promise secrecy when someone is at immediate risk; explain what information must be shared and why.

Substance use and vaping

Ask neutrally about alcohol, nicotine or vaping, cannabis and other substances, frequency, dependence, intoxication and social context. Consider interactions with prescribed medicines, safeguarding, driving and school or family impacts. Offer brief intervention and specialist support as indicated.

PLAB application: Assess acute intoxication or overdose using ABCDE first; discuss confidentiality after stabilisation.

Sexual health, consent and safeguarding

Use inclusive, non-assumptive questions about relationships, contraception, STI symptoms, coercion and safety. Sexual activity in a person under 16 does not automatically mean every disclosure must be reported, but clinicians must assess exploitation, power imbalance and risk; sexual activity involving a child under 13 is a serious safeguarding concern. Follow local policies.

PLAB application: Never equate contraception requests with automatic parental notification; assess competence and safeguarding.

Gillick competence and Fraser guidelines

Under-16s may consent if they have sufficient understanding and intelligence to understand the proposed intervention (Gillick competence). Fraser guidelines are specific considerations for contraceptive advice or treatment without parental involvement. Encourage trusted adult involvement where safe, but it is not an absolute prerequisite.

PLAB application: Explain confidentiality limits and document capacity, benefits, risks and safeguarding assessment.

Confidentiality and information sharing

Confidentiality applies to young people as well as adults. Share information with consent where possible; without consent, sharing may be justified or required for significant harm, serious risk or legal duties. Share only necessary information, document reasons and explain the decision when safe.

PLAB application: Use GMC 0–18 years guidance; do not promise absolute secrecy.

Neurodiversity, school and transition

Consider autism, ADHD, learning disability, sensory needs and communication preferences when assessing distress or behaviour. Ask about bullying, attendance, educational support, sleep and family strengths. Plan transition from paediatric to adult services early, involving the young person in decisions.

PLAB application: Offer accessible information and reasonable adjustments; do not attribute all symptoms to neurodivergence.

PLAB 2 consultation and safety-netting

Start with the young person’s agenda, clarify who should be present, assess confidentiality and capacity, obtain a private psychosocial history, check immediate risk and agree a collaborative plan. Give specific safety-net advice for deterioration, self-harm, syncope, dehydration or inability to eat and identify urgent contact routes.

PLAB application: Summarise the plan back to the adolescent and check understanding, consent and follow-up.

PLAB 2 — Confidential adolescent consultation

Introduce yourself, offer private discussion, explain confidentiality and limits, assess Gillick competence for the specific decision, screen for immediate risk, seek consent for appropriate family involvement and document your shared plan.

Deep Dive — Clinical reasoning

Gillick versus Fraser

Gillick competence is a general legal principle for consent by under-16s. Fraser guidelines are a narrower framework for contraception. Neither eliminates the duty to assess safeguarding.

Eating-disorder emergency

Physical instability can be present without low BMI. Assess rapid weight change, observations, ECG, hydration, electrolytes and risk of refeeding complications. Escalate urgently if unstable.

Suicide and self-harm

Explore thoughts, intent, plan, means, recent behaviour and support. Formulate needs and safety collaboratively rather than using a score to predict suicide.

Pubertal differential

Constitutional delay, chronic disease, undernutrition, hypothalamic-pituitary disorders and gonadal conditions may cause delayed puberty. Growth chart and specialist evaluation guide investigation.

Confidentiality scenarios

Explain at the start that private information stays private unless sharing is necessary to protect from significant harm or fulfil another lawful obligation. Discuss disclosures and document rationale.

OSCE structure

Introduce and build rapport → consent and private time → HEADSSS → targeted examination and investigations → safeguarding and risk formulation → shared plan and safety net.
