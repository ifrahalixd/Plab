Acute Appendicitis, Peritonitis & Perforated Viscus

Surgery · Lesson 02 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Recognise surgical infection and perforation

Safety first: Shock, diffuse rigidity, sepsis, or suspected perforation requires immediate ABCDE assessment, IV access, analgesia, urgent senior surgical review and resuscitation. Do not delay definitive care for routine tests.

1 · Acute appendicitis

Appendiceal luminal obstruction and inflammation may progress to gangrene, perforation and abscess. Classically, vague periumbilical pain migrates to the right iliac fossa with anorexia, nausea and fever. Presentations vary, particularly in children, pregnancy and older adults.

  • History: onset and migration, anorexia, vomiting, bowel/urinary symptoms, pregnancy possibility and gynaecological symptoms.
  • Examination: vital signs, RIF tenderness, percussion tenderness or guarding; assess for generalised peritonitis. Rovsing, psoas and obturator signs can support but not confirm the diagnosis.
  • Differentials: ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, PID, mesenteric adenitis, gastroenteritis, Crohn disease, renal colic and UTI.
  • Tests: FBC/CRP, U&Es, urinalysis and pregnancy test where relevant. Normal inflammatory markers do not always exclude early appendicitis.
  • Imaging: ultrasound is useful in children and selected pregnant patients; CT is commonly used in non-pregnant adults when diagnosis is uncertain, considering radiation and local pathways. MRI may be used in pregnancy if ultrasound is inconclusive.
  • Treatment: analgesia, fluids if needed, nil by mouth when operation likely, urgent surgical decision. Laparoscopic appendicectomy is a common definitive option; selected uncomplicated cases may be offered antibiotics following shared decision-making and local protocol. Perforation/abscess requires tailored source control and antibiotics.

2 · Peritonitis

Inflammation of the peritoneum may be localised or generalised. Involuntary guarding, rebound/percussion pain and board-like rigidity are concerning. Causes include perforated peptic ulcer, perforated diverticulitis, appendiceal perforation, bowel ischaemia and anastomotic leak. Sepsis and shock can develop rapidly.

3 · Perforated viscus

Sudden severe abdominal pain, a rigid abdomen, tachycardia and signs of shock raise suspicion. Erect chest radiography may show subdiaphragmatic free air but a normal film does not exclude perforation. In a stable patient, contrast-enhanced CT can help locate perforation and plan treatment. Unstable patients need immediate senior surgical/anaesthetic involvement and a decision about urgent source control.

4 · Initial management

  1. ABCDE, NEWS2 and repeated observations; call senior surgery early.
  2. IV access, FBC, U&Es, CRP, VBG/lactate, blood cultures if sepsis suspected, coagulation and group-and-save/crossmatch when indicated.
  3. Analgesia, antiemetic, judicious IV crystalloid with reassessment, nil by mouth if operation anticipated.
  4. Prompt IV antibiotics for suspected perforation, intra-abdominal infection or sepsis according to local policy; antibiotics are not automatic for every undifferentiated abdominal pain.
  5. Urgent source control where indicated: surgery, drainage or other specialist intervention. Document and communicate deterioration.

Clinical pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Suspected appendicitis

RIF pain ± migration → ABCDE and assess for sepsis/peritonitis → focused history/examination → pregnancy test where relevant, urine, FBC/CRP → surgical review ± risk-appropriate imaging → appendicectomy or selected non-operative management after shared decision-making.

Pathway B — Suspected perforation

Sudden severe pain ± rigidity → resuscitate and urgently escalate → IV access, bloods/crossmatch, appropriate antibiotics and nil by mouth → CT if stable and useful → urgent source-control decision. Do not delay escalation for a normal X-ray.

Slide 1 — Appendicitis clues

Migrating pain • anorexia • RIF tenderness • nausea • low fever. Atypical symptoms do not exclude it.

Slide 2 — Peritonitis red flags

Involuntary guarding • rigidity • marked percussion tenderness • tachycardia • hypotension • confusion.

Slide 3 — Imaging selection

Children/pregnancy: often ultrasound first. Non-pregnant adults with diagnostic uncertainty: consider CT. Suspected perforation in stable adults: CT often defines the source.

Slide 4 — PLAB pitfalls

Do not withhold analgesia. Do not exclude appendicitis on a normal WCC. Do not rely on absence of free air on plain films. Always consider ectopic pregnancy where relevant.

Four integrated surgical cases

Case 1 — Typical appendicitis

A 21-year-old has periumbilical pain migrating to the RIF, anorexia and focal tenderness. Reasoning: appendicitis is likely. Check observations, urine and pregnancy status when relevant; give analgesia, obtain labs and arrange surgical assessment/imaging as indicated.

Case 2 — Appendicitis mimic

A 26-year-old has RIF pain, dizziness and vaginal spotting. Reasoning: do not assume appendicitis. Assess stability and urgently test for pregnancy; ectopic pregnancy may be life-threatening.

Case 3 — Perforated ulcer

A 59-year-old with NSAID use develops abrupt epigastric pain, then a rigid abdomen and tachycardia. Reasoning: suspect perforated peptic ulcer. Resuscitate, urgent surgical/anaesthetic review, appropriate IV antibiotics and source-control planning.

Case 4 — Complicated appendicitis

A 72-year-old presents late with fever, confusion, diffuse tenderness and hypotension. Reasoning: suspect sepsis and perforation. Begin ABCDE resuscitation, obtain cultures when feasible without delaying antibiotics, and arrange urgent surgical management.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Right iliac fossa pain

Station: You are an FY2 in ED. A 25-year-old reports 18 hours of abdominal pain. Take a focused history, explain concerns and outline the plan.

Eight-minute checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent; assess immediate distress and observations.
  2. Clarify onset, migration, location, severity, progression and pain characteristics.
  3. Ask about anorexia, vomiting, fever, bowel, urinary and gynaecological symptoms.
  4. Ask about pregnancy possibility, menstrual history and vaginal bleeding where appropriate and sensitively.
  5. Ask previous episodes, surgery, comorbidities, medication, allergies and last oral intake.
  6. Offer analgesia and antiemetic; explain examination, chaperone and dignity.
  7. Explain blood tests, urine/pregnancy testing and imaging depending on clinical context.
  8. State suspected appendicitis, differentials, surgical review, nil by mouth if needed and safety-netting.

Example explanation: “Your symptoms could be due to an inflamed appendix, but there are other causes we need to rule out. We will treat your pain now, check your observations and arrange urgent assessment by the surgical team.”

Rapid revision and guideline references

  1. Migration from umbilicus to RIF is characteristic but not universal for appendicitis.
  2. Pregnancy-related emergencies and ovarian torsion can mimic appendicitis.
  3. Generalised rigidity suggests peritonitis and needs urgent escalation.
  4. Normal WCC or normal plain X-ray cannot reliably exclude appendicitis or perforation.
  5. Antibiotics and source control are central to complicated intra-abdominal infection.
