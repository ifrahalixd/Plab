Paediatric Haematology: Anaemia, Sickle Cell Disease, Thalassaemia & Leukaemia

Emergency red flags: Fever in sickle cell disease; chest pain or hypoxia in HbSS; focal neurological deficits; sudden pallor with splenomegaly; bruising with bone pain; fever during chemotherapy.

Approach to the pale or bruising child

Assess ABCDE, vital signs, perfusion and signs of bleeding or infection. Ask about onset, diet, pica, jaundice, dark urine, recurrent infections, pain, weight loss, family history, ethnicity, medication and newborn screening. Examine pallor, scleral icterus, lymph nodes, liver, spleen, petechiae and bone tenderness.

PLAB application: A child with pallor plus fever, bruising or bone pain needs urgent investigation; do not assume iron deficiency.

Full blood count and blood film

Interpret haemoglobin against age-specific ranges; review MCV, MCH, RDW, reticulocytes, white cell differential and platelets. A film may reveal target cells, sickle cells, blasts or fragmented red cells. Reticulocytes indicate marrow response; low counts suggest impaired production.

PLAB application: Use all three cell lines and the blood film, not haemoglobin alone.

Microcytic anaemia differential

Iron deficiency is common; consider dietary insufficiency, excessive cow’s milk, chronic blood loss and coeliac disease. Thalassaemia trait can cause marked microcytosis despite relatively preserved haemoglobin and a normal/high RBC count. Inflammation may alter iron studies.

PLAB application: Ferritin can rise with inflammation; interpret alongside CRP and clinical context.

Iron deficiency anaemia

Features include fatigue, pallor, pica and poor concentration. Investigate dietary history and underlying causes. Treat with oral iron at an age/weight-appropriate BNFC dose and dietary advice; monitor reticulocyte and haemoglobin response and continue treatment to replenish stores according to local guidance.

PLAB application: Avoid routine iron treatment without considering haemoglobinopathy or alternative causes.

Macrocytic and haemolytic anaemia

Macrocytosis suggests B12 or folate deficiency, marrow disease or hypothyroidism; check clinical context. Haemolysis may produce jaundice, reticulocytosis, raised LDH and bilirubin, reduced haptoglobin and splenomegaly. Consider G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis and autoimmune haemolysis.

PLAB application: Sudden pallor and dark urine after an oxidative trigger can suggest G6PD-associated haemolysis.

Sickle cell disease: fundamentals

HbSS and other sickling genotypes cause chronic haemolysis and vaso-occlusion. Complications include pain episodes, infection from functional asplenia, acute chest syndrome, splenic sequestration, stroke, avascular necrosis and chronic organ injury. UK newborn blood spot screening identifies affected infants.

PLAB application: Fever in a child with sickle cell disease is a medical emergency because of invasive bacterial infection risk.

Vaso-occlusive painful crisis

Assess pain, hydration, oxygen saturation, fever and red flags. Provide rapid age-appropriate analgesia, reassessment and fluids guided by clinical hydration; avoid fluid overload. Look for acute chest syndrome, osteomyelitis or other causes when symptoms are atypical.

PLAB application: Treat pain promptly; do not delay analgesia for investigations.

Acute chest syndrome

Suspect with new respiratory symptoms, hypoxia, chest pain or fever and a new pulmonary infiltrate. It can deteriorate quickly. Admit urgently, give oxygen if hypoxic, analgesia, antibiotics and specialist-directed respiratory support and transfusion assessment.

PLAB application: A sickle cell patient with fever and chest symptoms requires immediate hospital evaluation.

Splenic sequestration and aplastic crisis

Splenic sequestration causes rapid splenic enlargement, acute haemoglobin fall and possible hypovolaemic shock. Parvovirus B19 can cause transient red-cell aplasia with low reticulocytes and profound anaemia. Both require urgent haematology-led management.

PLAB application: Sudden pallor, tachycardia and enlarging spleen are an emergency.

Sickle cell stroke and prevention

New focal weakness, seizure, speech disturbance or severe headache warrants immediate stroke pathway and specialist haematology input. Preventive care includes vaccination, penicillin prophylaxis in appropriate children, transcranial Doppler screening and hydroxycarbamide when indicated.

PLAB application: Do not attribute focal neurology to a simple painful crisis.

Thalassaemia trait and major

Alpha- and beta-thalassaemia reduce globin synthesis. Trait often causes asymptomatic microcytosis; transfusion-dependent beta-thalassaemia can cause severe anaemia, growth failure, hepatosplenomegaly and iron overload. Diagnosis involves haemoglobin analysis and sometimes genetic testing.

PLAB application: Do not prescribe iron solely for microcytosis when iron stores are normal.

Thalassaemia long-term care

Specialist care may include regular transfusions, iron chelation, monitoring cardiac/liver iron and endocrine complications, and discussion of curative options such as stem-cell transplantation in selected patients. Counsel families about inheritance and carrier testing.

PLAB application: Repeated transfusions can cause iron overload even when haemoglobin improves.

Leukaemia presentation

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia is the most common childhood leukaemia. Look for persistent fever, pallor, bruising, petechiae, recurrent infection, fatigue, bone or joint pain, lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. FBC may show cytopenias or blasts, but blasts are not always visible.

PLAB application: Unexplained bone pain plus bruising or cytopenias requires urgent paediatric referral.

Suspected leukaemia: investigations and referral

Arrange urgent FBC and blood film with specialist discussion; definitive diagnosis requires bone marrow assessment, immunophenotyping and cytogenetic/molecular tests. Avoid unnecessary corticosteroids before specialist assessment as they can alter diagnostic findings.

PLAB application: A normal or near-normal white cell count does not exclude leukaemia.

Neutropenic sepsis and bleeding emergencies

A child on chemotherapy with fever or appearing unwell needs immediate assessment, cultures if feasible and prompt IV broad-spectrum antibiotics per local neutropenic sepsis pathway. Severe thrombocytopenic bleeding or symptomatic anaemia needs urgent specialist advice and supportive treatment.

PLAB application: Do not wait for neutrophil results if a chemotherapy patient is acutely unwell.

PLAB 2: family communication and safety-netting

Explain results clearly without prematurely labelling cancer; acknowledge uncertainty, arrange urgent paediatric review, use interpreters when needed and involve parents and the child appropriately. For haemoglobinopathies discuss inherited risk, routine preventive care and emergency triggers.

PLAB application: Give clear return precautions for fever, breathing difficulty, neurological symptoms, rapidly increasing pallor or uncontrolled bleeding.

PLAB 2 OSCE: Parent of a child with unexplained bruising

Introduce yourself, establish the timeline and associated fever, pain and bleeding. Explore parental concerns and examine for petechiae, pallor, lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly. Explain that urgent blood tests and paediatric assessment are needed without making an unsupported diagnosis. Confirm understanding and give emergency return precautions.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Paediatric Haematology

Interpreting reticulocytes

A high reticulocyte count indicates a compensatory marrow response to red-cell loss or destruction. A low reticulocyte count with severe anaemia suggests impaired production, including marrow suppression or aplastic crisis.

Sickle cell red flags

Acute chest syndrome, invasive bacterial infection, stroke and splenic sequestration can rapidly become life-threatening. A child can have more than one complication at once.

Iron deficiency versus thalassaemia trait

Both can cause low MCV. Iron deficiency often lowers ferritin and raises RDW; thalassaemia trait may have a relatively high RBC count and normal iron stores. Confirm with appropriate tests rather than indices alone.

Haematological malignancy

Leukaemia may infiltrate marrow and other organs. Fever, bone pain and cytopenias can precede obvious circulating blasts. Rapid specialist referral is more important than exhaustive primary-care testing.

Long-term haemoglobinopathy care

Preventive measures include vaccinations, infection prophylaxis, monitoring for stroke risk and organ complications, and appropriate genetic counselling. Individual plans must be specialist-led.

Communication and safeguarding

Explain inherited disorders without blame. Offer genetic counselling where appropriate. In suspected malignancy, avoid definitive statements before confirmation and ensure rapid follow-up.
