Genetic Disorders & Congenital Syndromes: Down Syndrome, Turner Syndrome, Cystic Fibrosis & Inherited Conditions

Red flags: Bilious vomiting in a newborn, cyanosis, severe respiratory distress, acute weakness, failure to thrive with dehydration, or new neurological symptoms require urgent assessment.

Approach to a child with a suspected genetic condition

Take a three-generation family history, pregnancy and birth history, growth and developmental history, and ask about congenital anomalies, recurrent illness and relatives with similar findings. Examine systematically and avoid drawing conclusions from appearance alone.

PLAB application: Use respectful, non-stigmatising language and discuss findings with the family before proposing tests.

Inheritance patterns

Autosomal dominant disorders often show vertical transmission; autosomal recessive disorders can affect siblings with unaffected carrier parents; X-linked recessive conditions predominantly affect males, although females can be affected. De novo variants and variable penetrance complicate patterns.

PLAB application: For two carrier parents of an autosomal recessive condition, each pregnancy has a 25% affected risk.

Chromosomal abnormalities and testing

Aneuploidy changes chromosome number, while deletions, duplications and rearrangements change structure. Karyotype identifies large chromosomal abnormalities; chromosomal microarray detects many copy-number variants; targeted gene tests or genomic sequencing assess specific conditions. Genetic counselling and consent matter.

PLAB application: Select tests with clinical genetics support; a normal karyotype does not exclude a single-gene disorder.

Down syndrome: genetics and recognition

Trisomy 21 is usually caused by meiotic nondisjunction; translocation and mosaicism are less common. Features can include hypotonia, characteristic facial appearance, single transverse palmar crease, congenital heart disease and developmental differences. Phenotype is variable.

PLAB application: Confirm with chromosome testing and assess immediate feeding, cardiac and gastrointestinal concerns.

Down syndrome: associated conditions

Consider atrioventricular septal defect, duodenal atresia, hearing and visual impairment, thyroid disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, coeliac disease and increased leukaemia risk. Arrange condition-specific surveillance and multidisciplinary developmental support.

PLAB application: A newborn with bilious vomiting needs urgent surgical evaluation regardless of suspected syndrome.

Down syndrome: communicating the diagnosis

Share the diagnosis privately and clearly, allowing time for questions. Describe both strengths and potential medical needs, offer written resources and support groups, and avoid predicting an individual child’s abilities from the diagnosis alone.

PLAB application: Explain that care is individualised and connect parents with paediatrics, genetics and community support.

Turner syndrome: recognition

Turner syndrome involves complete or partial loss of one X chromosome, sometimes with mosaicism. Features may include short stature, neonatal hand and foot lymphoedema, webbed neck, broad chest and delayed puberty due to ovarian insufficiency.

PLAB application: A girl with unexplained short stature or delayed puberty merits consideration of Turner syndrome.

Turner syndrome: screening and treatment

Associated risks include bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, aortic disease, renal anomalies, hearing problems, hypertension and thyroid dysfunction. Specialist care may include growth hormone and planned pubertal induction, with cardiac surveillance throughout life.

PLAB application: Check blood pressure and arrange appropriate cardiac evaluation; pregnancy counselling requires specialist input.

Cystic fibrosis: pathophysiology and diagnosis

CFTR dysfunction causes thick secretions affecting airways, pancreas and other organs. UK newborn screening may identify affected infants, but a negative screen does not fully exclude disease when clinical suspicion is strong. Sweat chloride testing and CFTR genetic testing support diagnosis.

PLAB application: Think of cystic fibrosis in recurrent chest infections, poor weight gain, steatorrhoea or meconium ileus.

Cystic fibrosis: respiratory disease

Chronic airway infection, bronchiectasis and inflammation cause cough, sputum and progressive lung disease. Management involves specialist airway clearance, infection surveillance, vaccination, antibiotics when indicated and mutation-eligible CFTR modulator therapy.

PLAB application: A child with cystic fibrosis and new respiratory deterioration needs prompt specialist assessment.

Cystic fibrosis: nutrition and complications

Pancreatic insufficiency can cause malabsorption, bulky greasy stools and failure to thrive. Pancreatic enzyme replacement, fat-soluble vitamins and dietetic support are common. Other issues include CF-related diabetes, liver disease and salt-loss dehydration.

PLAB application: Ask about growth, stools, enzyme adherence and respiratory symptoms at each review.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy

An X-linked dystrophin disorder usually presents in early childhood with delayed walking, proximal weakness, frequent falls, calf pseudohypertrophy and Gowers sign. Creatine kinase is often markedly elevated; molecular testing confirms diagnosis.

PLAB application: Assess cardiac and respiratory involvement and refer urgently to neuromuscular specialists.

Fragile X syndrome

An FMR1 repeat expansion can cause intellectual disability, speech and language difficulties, behavioural differences and autistic features. Physical features vary and may become more apparent with age. Family history can include affected relatives and reproductive implications.

PLAB application: Consider targeted testing with genetics input in unexplained developmental disability.

Neurofibromatosis type 1

An autosomal dominant condition with café-au-lait macules, axillary or inguinal freckling, neurofibromas and possible optic pathway glioma or learning difficulties. Diagnostic criteria are age dependent; some signs develop later.

PLAB application: Vision changes, progressive neurological symptoms or concerning masses need prompt assessment.

Genetic counselling, testing and family planning

Explain what testing can and cannot establish, possible uncertain findings, confidentiality, implications for relatives and the right to decline. Discuss recurrence risk only when the specific molecular or chromosomal mechanism is known.

PLAB application: Do not assume all cases of Down syndrome have the same recurrence risk; translocation requires parental assessment.

PLAB 2: explaining an inherited condition

Start by eliciting parents’ understanding and concerns. Use plain language to explain diagnosis, uncertainty, next tests, multidisciplinary care and follow-up. Offer written information, an interpreter if needed, and a clear safety-net for acute symptoms.

PLAB application: Avoid blame, deterministic predictions and unsupported promises about prognosis.

PLAB 2 — Counselling station

A parent has been told that their child may have a genetic condition. Introduce yourself, check what they have heard, acknowledge uncertainty, explain the proposed investigations and specialist follow-up, explore support needs, and agree on next steps without making assumptions about prognosis.

Deep Dive — Clinical reasoning

Phenotype is not a diagnosis

Overlapping features occur across many syndromes. A careful history, examination and appropriate molecular or chromosome testing prevent overconfident labelling.

Why recurrence risks differ

Nondisjunction trisomy 21, Robertsonian translocation, mosaicism and single-gene disorders have different recurrence implications. Refer for tailored counselling.

Down syndrome neonatal priorities

Assess feeding, oxygenation, cardiac findings and signs of gastrointestinal obstruction. Coordinate echocardiography and ongoing hearing, vision, thyroid and developmental surveillance.

Turner syndrome beyond stature

Aortic and renal anomalies can be clinically silent. Cardiology review and longitudinal surveillance are important even when growth concerns dominate.

Cystic fibrosis and newborn screening

A positive screen requires confirmatory assessment; persistent symptoms can justify testing despite a negative screen. Care is delivered by a multidisciplinary specialist centre.

Communicating uncertain genetic results

A variant of uncertain significance is not equivalent to a confirmed disease-causing variant. Explain limitations, possible reclassification and family implications.
