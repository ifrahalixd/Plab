Fever of Unknown Origin, Infection Control & Antimicrobial Stewardship

PLAB priority: Distinguish stable prolonged fever from life-threatening sepsis. Investigate systematically, apply appropriate infection precautions and review antibiotic prescriptions regularly.

01. Definitions and first priorities

Fever of unknown origin (FUO) classically describes temperature at least 38.3°C on several occasions, lasting over three weeks without diagnosis despite appropriate evaluation. In acute illness, recognise sepsis first rather than waiting for FUO criteria.

PLAB application: A stable patient with prolonged fever needs structured investigation; an unstable patient needs immediate ABCDE assessment.

02. Focused history

Ask about duration, fever pattern, night sweats, weight loss, travel, animal and tick exposure, medications, surgery, implants, sexual exposure, immunosuppression, occupation, TB contact and family history.

PLAB application: Repeatedly review the timeline and previous test results rather than ordering every test indiscriminately.

03. Physical examination

Check observations and hydration; inspect skin, mouth, teeth, lymph nodes, heart murmurs, abdomen, joints, temporal arteries and neurological status. Repeat examination as new signs develop.

PLAB application: A new murmur, lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly may change the diagnostic direction.

04. Main diagnostic categories

Consider infections such as TB, endocarditis and occult abscess; inflammatory disease such as vasculitis and adult-onset Still disease; malignancy such as lymphoma; and miscellaneous causes including drug fever.

PLAB application: Do not assume all persistent fever is bacterial.

05. Initial investigations

Guided tests may include FBC with differential, CRP/ESR, U&Es, LFTs, urinalysis and culture, blood cultures, chest X-ray and HIV testing with consent. Add pregnancy testing, malaria films or TB investigations when indicated.

PLAB application: Take cultures before antibiotics when safe, but never delay treatment of sepsis.

06. Targeted second-line testing

Use echocardiography for suspected endocarditis; ultrasound or CT for focal occult infection or malignancy; autoimmune serology only with supportive clinical findings; biopsy when tissue diagnosis is needed.

PLAB application: A careful exposure history guides tests better than a broad unselected autoimmune panel.

07. Drug fever and non-infectious mimics

Medication-associated fever may occur with antibiotics, anticonvulsants and other agents. Consider thromboembolism, inflammatory disorders, malignancy and factitious fever in the right context.

PLAB application: Review the full drug list, including recently stopped medicines.

08. When antibiotics are urgent

Suspected sepsis with high-risk features, bacterial meningitis, neutropenic sepsis and other life-threatening bacterial syndromes need immediate guideline-directed therapy. Seek senior support and follow local sepsis pathways.

PLAB application: Never withhold urgent antibiotics solely to preserve culture yield.

09. When empiric antibiotics may harm

In stable FUO without a likely bacterial source, blind broad-spectrum antibiotics may obscure diagnosis, cause adverse effects and select resistance. Discuss the investigation plan with infection specialists.

PLAB application: Reassess daily and use microbiological evidence whenever possible.

10. Standard infection prevention

Hand hygiene, respiratory hygiene, safe sharps handling, appropriate PPE, equipment cleaning and environmental decontamination are core measures for all patients.

PLAB application: Perform hand hygiene before and after patient contact, even when gloves are used.

11. Transmission-based precautions

Contact precautions may be needed for infectious diarrhoea and resistant organisms; droplet precautions for selected respiratory infections; airborne precautions for pulmonary TB, measles and varicella, according to local policy.

PLAB application: Use the correct room, mask and isolation advice for the suspected pathogen.

12. Clostridioides difficile control

Suspect C. difficile in compatible diarrhoea after antibiotic or healthcare exposure. Use contact precautions, soap-and-water handwashing when appropriate, sporicidal cleaning and antimicrobial review.

PLAB application: Alcohol hand rub is not reliably sporicidal; follow local C. difficile policy.

13. Antimicrobial stewardship principles

Prescribe only when indicated; document indication, allergies, dose, route, duration and review date. Use local guidance and microbiology; consider renal function, pregnancy and interactions.

PLAB application: Obtain samples promptly, then narrow or stop antibiotics when evidence supports it.

14. The 48–72 hour antibiotic review

Reassess clinical progress, cultures, imaging and source control. Decide to stop, switch IV to oral, change to a narrower agent or continue with a documented plan.

PLAB application: Do not automatically continue IV broad-spectrum therapy when the patient is improving.

15. Source control and device review

Drain abscesses, relieve infected obstruction, remove infected lines when appropriate and assess implanted devices. Antibiotics alone may be inadequate without source control.

PLAB application: A deteriorating patient with an infected obstructed kidney requires urgent urology input.

16. PLAB 2 explanation and safety-net

Explain uncertainty, planned tests, isolation requirements and antibiotic decisions without alarming patients. Safety-net for confusion, breathlessness, rash, collapse, reduced urine output or rapidly worsening symptoms.

PLAB application: Use SBAR for escalation and explain why infection precautions protect other patients.

Deep Dive

FUO as a diagnostic syndrome

The classic definition is a framework, not a reason to delay care. Immunocompromised patients and healthcare-associated fever require tailored assessment.

Inflammatory and malignant fever

Vasculitis, connective tissue disease, lymphoma and other malignancies may present with persistent fever; clues include inflammatory symptoms, lymphadenopathy and weight loss.

Interpreting cultures and inflammatory markers

A negative culture does not exclude infection, especially after antibiotics. CRP and ESR are nonspecific; trends help but must be interpreted with clinical findings.

Contact, droplet and airborne spread

Transmission depends on particle behaviour and organism. PPE and room requirements vary; local infection prevention teams should guide implementation.

Antibiotic resistance mechanisms

Selection pressure favours organisms with resistance genes, target changes, drug-inactivating enzymes or efflux pumps. Unnecessary broad therapy increases this pressure.

Source control and antibiotic duration

Drainage or device removal can be decisive. Duration depends on syndrome, organism, clinical response and adequacy of source control; avoid one-size-fits-all courses.
