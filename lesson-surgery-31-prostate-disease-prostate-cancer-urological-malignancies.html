Prostate and urological malignancies — UK PLAB approach

Never miss: urinary retention with renal impairment, febrile acute prostatitis/sepsis, visible haematuria, testicular tumour, and suspected metastatic spinal cord compression (new back pain with neurological or bladder/bowel symptoms).

1 · LUTS and benign prostatic enlargement (BPE)

Storage symptoms: frequency, urgency, nocturia; voiding symptoms: hesitancy, weak stream, straining, intermittency; post-micturition dribbling and incomplete emptying. Ask about infection, haematuria, neurological symptoms, drugs, diabetes and impact on quality of life. Assess abdomen, external genitalia and DRE when indicated with consent/chaperone. Urinalysis, frequency-volume chart and symptom scoring (IPSS) can help. Consider PSA after informed discussion when appropriate; PSA is not a standalone diagnostic test for cancer.

2 · BPE treatment

Offer lifestyle advice: adjust evening fluids, caffeine/alcohol and medication review. Alpha-1 blockers such as tamsulosin improve symptoms; discuss postural hypotension and ejaculatory adverse effects. 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (finasteride/dutasteride) may help enlarged prostates but take months, affect PSA interpretation and have sexual adverse effects. Consider combination treatment when indicated. Persistent troublesome symptoms, recurrent retention, recurrent UTI, stones or renal impairment warrant specialist assessment for procedural options (e.g. TURP, HoLEP). Acute retention needs prompt drainage, not just tablets.

3 · Prostatitis

Acute bacterial prostatitis presents with fever, systemic illness, dysuria, perineal pain and a tender prostate; assess sepsis, send urine culture and start antibiotics following NICE NG110/local guidance. Avoid vigorous prostate massage. Escalate for retention, severe illness or failure to improve. Chronic pelvic pain symptoms have different management and should not be assumed to require prolonged antibiotics.

4 · Prostate cancer presentation and assessment

Often asymptomatic early; may present with LUTS, haematuria, bone pain, weight loss or neurological complications. Discuss benefits and limitations of PSA testing, including false positives, false negatives and overdiagnosis. DRE may reveal a hard irregular prostate. NICE NG12 supports suspected-cancer referral when the prostate feels malignant on DRE, and considering referral with PSA above age-specific reference ranges. Do not dismiss suspicious DRE because PSA is normal.

5 · Prostate cancer investigations

Specialist assessment generally includes multiparametric MRI before biopsy where appropriate; targeted and/or systematic biopsy according to MRI and risk. Histology gives Gleason grade group. PSA, clinical T stage, MRI and staging imaging (e.g. PSMA PET/CT where available and indicated) inform risk and extent. Shared decisions account for age, comorbidity, stage, preferences and treatment harms.

6 · Prostate cancer management

Options include active surveillance for selected localised low-risk cancers; radical prostatectomy or radiotherapy for suitable localised disease; androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) with other systemic agents for advanced disease according to specialist MDT decisions. Discuss urinary incontinence, erectile dysfunction, bowel effects and ADT-associated hot flushes, bone loss and metabolic effects. Suspected metastatic spinal cord compression is an emergency requiring immediate assessment and local pathway activation.

7 · Bladder cancer

Painless visible haematuria is a cancer red flag. Risk factors include smoking and occupational aromatic amine exposure. Assess urine for infection but ensure unexplained haematuria receives appropriate referral. NICE NG12 recommends suspected-cancer referral for people aged 45+ with unexplained visible haematuria without UTI, or persisting/recurring after successful UTI treatment; aged 60+ with unexplained non-visible haematuria plus dysuria or raised WBC count. Cystoscopy and imaging establish diagnosis; TURBT provides histology/staging. Non-muscle-invasive disease may need intravesical therapy; muscle-invasive disease may need cystectomy or bladder-preserving chemoradiation.

8 · Renal cell carcinoma (RCC)

Often incidental on imaging; may present with haematuria, flank pain or mass (classic triad uncommon), weight loss, fever or paraneoplastic erythrocytosis/hypercalcaemia. Contrast CT characterises and stages lesions when appropriate. Localised disease may undergo partial or radical nephrectomy; advanced disease is managed by specialist MDT with systemic therapies. Consider differential renal stones and infection.

9 · Testicular cancer

Typically a painless, firm intratesticular lump in younger adults. Examine with consent/chaperone and arrange urgent scrotal ultrasound and urology referral. NICE NG12 recommends suspected-cancer referral for non-painful enlargement or change in shape/texture of a testis. Tumour markers AFP, beta-hCG and LDH help staging/monitoring; normal markers do not exclude malignancy. Suspected cancer is usually managed with radical inguinal orchidectomy; do not perform trans-scrotal biopsy. Discuss fertility preservation before gonadotoxic treatment.

10 · Penile and upper-tract urothelial cancers

Persistent penile lesion, ulcer or bleeding requires examination and urgent specialist assessment; risk factors include HPV and smoking. Upper-tract urothelial cancer may cause haematuria and flank pain; CT urography and specialist endoscopic evaluation may be used. Consider risk of synchronous urothelial disease.

11 · Emergencies and escalation

Clot retention needs urgent urological drainage/irrigation. Obstructed infected urinary tract needs antibiotics plus urgent decompression. Suspected cord compression needs emergency pathway; new leg weakness, sensory level or bladder/bowel dysfunction is time-critical. Evaluate AKI/hyperkalaemia in obstructive malignancy.

12 · Communication and safety-netting

Explain that PSA alone does not diagnose cancer, discuss testing preferences, use shared decision-making, assess psychological impact and safety-net persistent haematuria, progressive symptoms, fever, retention or neurological red flags. Follow current NICE and local cancer pathways.

Clinical decision frameworks

Male lower urinary tract symptoms

History + medication review → urine dip and focused exam → symptom burden/IPSS → discuss PSA when appropriate → lifestyle measures → alpha blocker or 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor where indicated → refer for complications or refractory symptoms.

Suspicious prostate cancer

Shared PSA discussion + DRE → apply NG12 referral criteria → specialist MRI → biopsy when indicated → grade/stage → MDT: surveillance, surgery, radiotherapy or systemic treatment.

Visible haematuria

Check instability/clot retention → urine infection assessment → treat proven UTI but ensure reassessment → apply age/symptom NG12 criteria → cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging by urology.

Testicular lump

Examination with consent → urgent scrotal ultrasound and referral → tumour markers → inguinal orchidectomy/staging as indicated → fertility discussion.

Neurological red flags in cancer

New severe back pain + weakness/sensory/bladder symptoms → suspect metastatic spinal cord compression → emergency specialist pathway and MRI, without delay.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Troublesome LUTS

A 69-year-old reports weak stream, hesitancy and nocturia without red flags. DRE is smoothly enlarged. Approach: urine dip, symptom assessment, shared PSA discussion; lifestyle measures and consider tamsulosin, review and refer if complications.

Case 2 — Suspicious prostate

A 72-year-old has a hard irregular prostate on DRE and a PSA that is not markedly elevated. Approach: suspected cancer referral; normal or modest PSA does not negate suspicious DRE.

Case 3 — Painless visible haematuria

A 63-year-old smoker has painless visible haematuria and no UTI. Approach: suspected bladder cancer referral for cystoscopy and imaging; assess for clots and retention.

Case 4 — Testicular lump

A 28-year-old finds a painless firm intratesticular mass. Approach: urgent urology referral and ultrasound, AFP/beta-hCG/LDH; discuss likely inguinal orchidectomy and fertility preservation.

PLAB 2 OSCE — PSA and prostate cancer discussion

Station: A 60-year-old requests a PSA test after a friend's prostate cancer diagnosis. You have 8 minutes.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent and explore ideas, concerns, expectations and family history.
  2. Ask about LUTS, haematuria, bone pain, weight loss, infection symptoms and prior PSA testing.
  3. Explain PSA is a blood marker produced by the prostate, raised by cancer but also BPE/inflammation; normal values do not entirely exclude cancer.
  4. Explain potential benefits of earlier detection and harms of false alarms, biopsies, overdiagnosis and treatment adverse effects.
  5. Discuss informed choice, DRE if clinically indicated, and local testing/referral pathways; do not promise a cancer diagnosis from PSA.
  6. Explain that suspicious examination or raised age-specific PSA may lead to specialist MRI and possibly biopsy.
  7. Safety-net visible haematuria, unexplained weight loss, bone pain, urinary retention or neurological symptoms.
  8. Summarise, check understanding and agree next steps with shared decision-making.

Examiner focus: non-directive informed consent, balanced PSA counselling, appropriate referral and empathy.

Rapid review — 12 key facts

  1. BPE causes storage and voiding LUTS; assess severity and red flags.
  2. Alpha blockers improve LUTS; 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors act slowly and affect PSA.
  3. Acute urinary retention needs prompt catheter drainage when safe.
  4. Acute bacterial prostatitis may cause sepsis; avoid prostatic massage.
  5. PSA is not diagnostic for cancer and requires informed discussion.
  6. Hard irregular prostate on DRE warrants suspected-cancer referral.
  7. Prostate cancer work-up often uses MRI before biopsy.
  8. Active surveillance suits selected low-risk localised prostate cancers.
  9. Painless visible haematuria is a key bladder cancer warning sign.
  10. Bladder cancer diagnosis involves cystoscopy and TURBT histology.
  11. Firm painless testicular lump needs urgent ultrasound and urology review.
  12. New neurological deficits with cancer and back pain suggest spinal cord compression.
