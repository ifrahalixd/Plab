Venous Disease: Deep Vein Thrombosis, Varicose Veins & Venous Ulcers

Surgery · Lesson 39 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Venous disease — UK PLAB high-yield lesson

Never miss: pulmonary embolism in suspected DVT, phlegmasia cerulea dolens (limb-threatening extensive venous thrombosis), or severe infection in a leg ulcer. Check for arterial disease before compression.

1 · Venous anatomy and pathophysiology

Superficial veins (great and small saphenous) connect to deep veins via perforators. Valves and calf muscle pump maintain venous return. Venous reflux causes ambulatory venous hypertension, varicosities, oedema, skin inflammation and ulcers. Virchow triad for thrombosis: venous stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability.

2 · Deep vein thrombosis (DVT): risk and presentation

Risk factors: surgery, immobilisation, malignancy, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen exposure, previous VTE and inherited/acquired thrombophilia. Typical features: unilateral swelling, calf pain, tenderness along deep veins, warmth and pitting oedema. Signs are nonspecific; cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst, superficial thrombophlebitis and lymphoedema can mimic DVT. Ask about pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, syncope or hypoxia suggesting PE.

3 · NICE NG158 diagnostic pathway

For suspected leg DVT use two-level DVT Wells score: ≥2 = DVT likely; ≤1 = DVT unlikely. Likely: offer proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours if possible; if not, obtain D-dimer and give interim therapeutic anticoagulation while arranging ultrasound within 24 hours, subject to contraindications and local pathway. Unlikely: D-dimer first; if positive, arrange ultrasound. If initial proximal ultrasound negative but D-dimer positive, repeat proximal ultrasound in 6–8 days. Follow the full NICE algorithm, including age-adjusted D-dimer where applicable.

4 · DVT treatment

Confirm DVT and assess bleeding risk, renal/liver function, weight, pregnancy, interactions and cancer status. NICE commonly recommends apixaban or rivaroxaban for confirmed proximal DVT when suitable; alternatives include LMWH followed by dabigatran/edoxaban or LMWH with a vitamin K antagonist in selected cases. At least 3 months anticoagulation for confirmed proximal DVT, then reassess recurrence and bleeding risks. Pregnancy generally uses LMWH rather than DOACs. Suspected PE with haemodynamic instability is an emergency. Anticoagulation decisions require senior/local protocol guidance.

5 · Complications and emergencies

PE may present with sudden breathlessness, chest pain, tachycardia, syncope or shock. Phlegmasia cerulea dolens causes severe painful swollen cyanotic limb from extensive venous outflow obstruction; emergency vascular assessment is required. Post-thrombotic syndrome causes chronic pain, swelling and skin change. Routine elastic stockings are not recommended solely to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome after DVT, though may be used for symptom relief if appropriate.

6 · Superficial vein thrombosis

Localised painful, tender, palpable cord with erythema along a superficial vein. Consider duplex ultrasound if extensive, near deep venous junction, or DVT suspected. Management depends on extent and location: mobilisation, analgesia and selected anticoagulation according to protocol. Do not assume superficial thrombosis is harmless, especially near the saphenofemoral junction.

7 · Varicose veins

Dilated tortuous superficial veins from reflux. Symptoms include aching/heaviness after standing, itching, ankle swelling, eczema and sometimes bleeding. NICE CG168 referral indications include symptomatic primary or recurrent varicose veins, lower-limb skin changes attributable to venous insufficiency, superficial vein thrombosis with suspected venous incompetence, and a venous leg ulcer or healed venous ulcer. Bleeding varicose veins require immediate referral. Duplex ultrasound maps reflux.

8 · Varicose vein treatment hierarchy

For confirmed truncal reflux NICE CG168 recommends endothermal ablation first where suitable; if unsuitable, ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy; if unsuitable, surgery. Do not offer compression hosiery as definitive treatment for varicose veins unless interventional treatment is unsuitable. Walking, weight management and leg elevation can relieve symptoms.

9 · Chronic venous insufficiency and ulcers

Features: gaiter-area oedema, haemosiderin pigmentation, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis and shallow irregular exudative ulcer usually around the medial malleolus. Arterial ulcers tend to be painful, punched-out and distal with reduced pulses; neuropathic ulcers occur at pressure points with sensory loss. Evaluate pulses and ABPI before high-strength compression; diabetes may give falsely high ABPI and require specialist vascular testing.

10 · Venous leg ulcer management

Assess wound size, exudate, infection, pain, mobility, arterial supply and comorbidities. Compression is the cornerstone of venous ulcer healing when arterial perfusion is adequate, applied by trained staff; choose compression level based on vascular assessment and local guidance. Provide appropriate dressings, leg elevation, exercise/calf pump activity and skin care. Antibiotics only if clinical infection (spreading redness/warmth, increasing pain, fever), not mere colonisation. NICE NG152 covers leg ulcer infection. Refer for venous reflux evaluation/intervention, especially recurrent or non-healing ulcers.

11 · DVT prevention in surgical patients

Assess VTE and bleeding risk on admission and after clinical changes (NICE NG89). Encourage early mobilisation, hydration as appropriate, and prescribe pharmacological/mechanical prophylaxis according to procedure, risk and contraindications. Avoid compression devices in significant peripheral arterial disease or other contraindications. Document decisions and counsel patients about VTE warning signs.

Decision pathways

Suspected DVT

Check PE symptoms and stability → two-level Wells score → likely (≥2): ultrasound promptly, interim anticoagulation if imaging delayed as per NICE → unlikely (≤1): D-dimer first → image if positive → repeat scan in 6–8 days if initial proximal scan negative and D-dimer positive.

Confirmed proximal DVT

Review contraindications, renal function, pregnancy, bleeding risk and interactions → select appropriate therapeutic anticoagulant → treat at least 3 months → reassess whether provoked or unprovoked and recurrence/bleeding risks.

Swollen leg with chest symptoms

Dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, hypoxia or syncope → assess ABCDE and urgent PE pathway; do not treat as isolated uncomplicated leg disease.

Symptomatic varicose veins

Clinical assessment → vascular service referral and duplex reflux mapping → endothermal ablation if suitable → foam sclerotherapy if not → surgery if neither suitable.

Leg ulcer

Examine wound, pulses and neuropathy → ABPI / arterial assessment → compression only if safe → dressings, exercise, elevation, skin care → treat clinical infection only → venous specialist review.

Urgent vascular red flags

Bleeding varicosity: elevate leg and direct pressure, immediate referral. Severe cyanotic swollen painful limb: suspect phlegmasia and seek emergency vascular care. Cold pulseless foot: assess for arterial ischaemia instead.

Four clinical cases with reasoning

Case 1 — Postoperative swollen calf

A 58-year-old develops unilateral calf swelling and tenderness 5 days after abdominal surgery. No chest symptoms. Reasoning: surgery and immobilisation raise VTE risk; signs alone cannot confirm DVT. Plan: calculate Wells score, follow NICE ultrasound/D-dimer algorithm, consider interim therapeutic anticoagulation if imaging delayed and safe, check bleeding risk.

Case 2 — Sudden breathlessness after leg swelling

A patient with painful swollen leg develops pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia and oxygen desaturation. Reasoning: possible pulmonary embolism. Plan: urgent ABCDE, senior/emergency assessment and PE diagnostic/treatment pathway; assess haemodynamic stability.

Case 3 — Aching varicose veins

A 43-year-old has visible tortuous veins, heaviness after work and ankle eczema. Reasoning: symptomatic venous reflux with skin changes. Plan: refer to vascular service for duplex assessment; discuss endothermal ablation as preferred intervention if suitable, with alternatives and shared decision-making.

Case 4 — Medial ankle ulcer

A 72-year-old has a shallow exudative ulcer near the medial malleolus, brown pigmentation and leg oedema. Reasoning: venous ulcer likely, but exclude arterial disease. Plan: pulses and ABPI, trained compression if safe, wound/skin care, mobility and elevation, venous service referral; antibiotics only for clinical infection.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Suspected deep vein thrombosis

Station: A patient presents with a swollen, painful calf following recent surgery.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and assess stability; ask immediately about breathlessness, chest pain, haemoptysis or fainting.
  2. Clarify onset, unilateral swelling, pain, trauma, fever, prior VTE, malignancy, pregnancy, oestrogen, surgery, immobility and anticoagulants.
  3. Examine both legs for asymmetry, tenderness, warmth, pitting oedema and skin changes; check pulses and vital signs.
  4. Explain that symptoms can suggest a clot but cannot confirm it; calculate two-level Wells score.
  5. Describe urgent ultrasound and/or D-dimer according to score; explain interim anticoagulation when indicated if scan is delayed and safe.
  6. Discuss anticoagulation benefits, bleeding risks, interactions and adherence if DVT is confirmed; seek senior advice for complex cases.
  7. Safety-net: call emergency services for sudden breathlessness, chest pain, collapse or major bleeding; arrange follow-up and document SBAR handover.

Avoid calf squeezing/Homan sign as a diagnostic test. Always consider PE.

Rapid revision — 12 must-know facts

  1. Virchow triad: stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability.
  2. Suspected DVT signs are nonspecific; assess for PE symptoms.
  3. Two-level DVT Wells: ≥2 likely, ≤1 unlikely.
  4. Likely DVT: prompt proximal ultrasound; interim anticoagulation if delayed when appropriate.
  5. Negative proximal ultrasound plus positive D-dimer: repeat ultrasound in 6–8 days.
  6. Confirmed proximal DVT generally needs at least 3 months anticoagulation.
  7. Pregnancy-associated DVT usually uses LMWH, not a DOAC.
  8. Phlegmasia cerulea dolens is limb-threatening venous thrombosis.
  9. Bleeding varicose veins require immediate vascular referral.
  10. For truncal reflux: endothermal ablation → foam sclerotherapy → surgery if earlier options unsuitable.
  11. Venous ulcers classically affect the medial gaiter region with pigmentation and oedema.
  12. Check arterial supply before compression; antibiotics only for clinically infected ulcers.
