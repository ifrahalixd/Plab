Viral Hepatitis (A, B, C, D & E)

PLAB priority: Interpret HBV serology, confirm active HCV with RNA, recognise acute liver failure, and counsel on prevention and curative treatment.

01. Overview and clinical approach

Viral hepatitis is inflammation of the liver caused by hepatotropic viruses A–E. Presentations range from asymptomatic infection to acute hepatitis, acute liver failure, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Assess exposure history, symptoms, pregnancy, immunosuppression and signs of decompensation.

PLAB application: In any jaundiced patient, check liver biochemistry and synthetic function; identify urgent red flags.

02. Transmission patterns

HAV and HEV are usually faecal–oral, linked to contaminated food or water. HBV spreads through blood, sexual contact and perinatal exposure. HCV is predominantly blood-borne; HDV requires concurrent HBV infection.

PLAB application: Ask sensitively about travel, food/water, injection equipment, sexual exposure and birth-country risks.

03. Symptoms and examination

Acute viral hepatitis may cause fatigue, anorexia, nausea, right upper-quadrant discomfort, dark urine, pale stools, pruritus and jaundice. Examination may reveal hepatomegaly, jaundice or stigmata of chronic liver disease.

PLAB application: Assess for encephalopathy, bleeding, ascites and haemodynamic instability.

04. Initial investigations

Request ALT/AST, bilirubin, ALP, albumin, INR/PT, FBC, renal function and glucose; perform appropriate viral serology and nucleic acid tests. Ultrasound helps exclude biliary obstruction where indicated.

PLAB application: INR and mental status assess acute liver failure risk; ALT alone does not reflect liver function.

05. Hepatitis A

HAV is usually self-limiting and does not cause chronic infection. Diagnosis is supported by anti-HAV IgM in the correct clinical context. Management is supportive, with public-health notification and contact vaccination or immunoglobulin according to guidance.

PLAB application: Consider HAV after travel or foodborne exposure and counsel on hygiene.

06. Hepatitis B: virology and phases

HBV is a DNA virus capable of chronic infection. Risk of chronicity is highest after perinatal acquisition. Chronic HBV can lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma even without cirrhosis.

PLAB application: Do not assume normal ALT excludes significant chronic HBV.

07. HBV serology interpretation

HBsAg indicates current HBV infection; anti-HBs indicates immunity; anti-HBc indicates previous or current natural infection (not vaccine-only immunity). IgM anti-HBc suggests recent infection but can occur in flares. HBeAg and HBV DNA inform replication and treatment decisions.

PLAB application: Vaccinated person: anti-HBs positive, anti-HBc negative; resolved infection: HBsAg negative, anti-HBc positive, often anti-HBs positive.

08. Chronic hepatitis B assessment

Confirm persistence of HBsAg for at least six months. Assess HBV DNA, ALT trends, fibrosis, coinfections (HIV/HCV/HDV), pregnancy status and hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance eligibility. Refer for specialist management.

PLAB application: Treatment eligibility depends on viral load, liver inflammation, fibrosis and clinical circumstances.

09. Hepatitis B treatment and prevention

First-line long-term antiviral options often include tenofovir or entecavir, selected by specialists. Vaccination prevents HBV. Screen pregnant patients; use neonatal vaccine and, when indicated, hepatitis B immunoglobulin, plus maternal antiviral treatment for high viral load per protocols.

PLAB application: Post-exposure management is time-sensitive and depends on source and immune status.

10. Hepatitis C diagnosis

Anti-HCV antibody indicates exposure, not necessarily current infection. Confirm active infection using HCV RNA (or validated HCV core antigen where applicable). Chronic infection may remain asymptomatic for years.

PLAB application: A positive antibody test requires a test for active viraemia.

11. Hepatitis C treatment

Direct-acting antiviral combinations can cure most HCV infections. Regimen selection considers cirrhosis, interactions, renal function, previous therapy and specialist protocols. Confirm sustained virological response with RNA testing after treatment. No licensed HCV vaccine exists.

PLAB application: Refer confirmed active HCV for curative treatment and harm-reduction support.

12. Hepatitis D

HDV is a defective RNA virus that requires HBsAg for its lifecycle. Coinfection or superinfection may cause severe disease and accelerate fibrosis. Test at-risk HBsAg-positive patients for anti-HDV and confirm with HDV RNA when appropriate.

PLAB application: HBV vaccination prevents HDV acquisition in people not already infected with HBV.

13. Hepatitis E

HEV usually spreads faecal–orally; zoonotic foodborne infection also occurs in some settings. Most infections resolve, but severe disease may occur in pregnancy and chronic HEV may develop in immunosuppressed patients. Diagnosis uses HEV serology and/or RNA depending on context.

PLAB application: Consider HEV in unexplained hepatitis, especially pregnancy or immunosuppression.

14. Acute liver failure and emergency red flags

Acute liver failure is acute hepatic injury with coagulopathy and encephalopathy in a patient without pre-existing cirrhosis. Confusion, elevated INR, hypoglycaemia, bleeding or rapidly worsening jaundice warrant urgent senior and specialist assessment.

PLAB application: Do not delay transfer or specialist discussion in suspected acute liver failure.

15. Counselling, public health and follow-up

Discuss transmission prevention, alcohol avoidance, medication safety, vaccination of contacts where indicated, safer sex, needle safety and stigma-free testing. Follow UK notification and contact-tracing procedures for relevant infections.

PLAB application: Use clear explanations of serology, confidentiality and treatment expectations in PLAB 2.

16. PLAB differential diagnosis and OSCE approach

Differentials include drug-induced liver injury (including paracetamol), alcohol-associated hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, biliary obstruction and metabolic liver disease. Take a structured jaundice history, assess risk, interpret results and arrange appropriate referral.

PLAB application: Never label hepatitis viral without checking other dangerous causes and synthetic function.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Hepatocellular injury versus liver function

ALT and AST rise with hepatocyte injury, but their height does not directly measure liver function. INR, glucose, mental state and bilirubin trajectory are critical in severe acute hepatitis.

HBV serology in context

HBsAg denotes current infection; anti-HBs denotes immunity; anti-HBc identifies natural exposure. Interpret combinations and use HBV DNA, HBeAg and clinical context for staging.

HBV chronicity and cancer risk

HBV persists as a stable nuclear template, enabling chronic infection and long-term carcinogenic risk. HCC surveillance eligibility is determined by specialist assessment of cirrhosis and other risk factors.

HCV diagnostic cascade

HCV antibody reflects exposure and can persist after clearance. RNA demonstrates ongoing replication. Curative treatment uses oral direct-acting antiviral combinations and is confirmed by post-treatment virological response.

HDV and HEV nuances

HDV cannot infect independently of HBV surface antigen and may worsen liver disease. HEV can be severe in pregnancy and persistent in immunocompromised patients; choose tests according to clinical setting.

Acute liver failure physiology

Massive hepatic dysfunction causes impaired clotting-factor production, metabolic disturbance and encephalopathy. Rapid recognition and liver-centre coordination are essential.
