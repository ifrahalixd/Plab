Acute Breathlessness: Pulmonary Embolism, Pneumothorax, Pulmonary Oedema & Respiratory Failure

Emergency rule: Severe hypoxaemia, exhaustion, altered consciousness, shock or suspected tension pneumothorax requires immediate escalation and treatment.

First five minutes of acute breathlessness

Approach any acutely breathless patient with ABCDE, early senior help, continuous observations, pulse oximetry, ECG and IV access. Assess ability to speak, exhaustion, consciousness, cyanosis and perfusion. Treat immediately reversible threats while investigating.

PLAB application: Do not delay treatment of a life-threatening diagnosis while awaiting imaging.

Airway and breathing examination

Listen for stridor, wheeze, crackles and asymmetry of breath sounds. Measure respiratory rate, work of breathing, SpO₂, temperature and blood pressure; inspect tracheal position, chest expansion and peripheral oedema. Auscultation alone cannot exclude PE or pneumothorax.

PLAB application: A silent chest with exhaustion is an emergency.

Oxygen targets and monitoring

Use controlled oxygen tailored to risk: most acutely ill adults target SpO₂ 94–98%; those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure generally target 88–92% pending blood gases, unless a condition-specific protocol applies. Do not withhold life-saving oxygen from a critically hypoxaemic patient.

PLAB application: Repeat saturation and clinical assessment after every intervention.

Pulmonary embolism presentation

PE may cause sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia, hypoxaemia, haemoptysis or syncope. Ask about previous VTE, recent surgery or immobility, cancer, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen and unilateral leg swelling. Normal oxygen saturation does not exclude PE.

PLAB application: Haemodynamic instability raises concern for high-risk PE.

PE risk stratification

In a stable patient, use the NICE two-level Wells score. If PE is likely, arrange immediate CTPA where appropriate; if imaging cannot be done immediately, consider interim therapeutic anticoagulation per guideline. If unlikely, use D-dimer with age adjustment when applicable; positive tests require imaging.

PLAB application: Do not use D-dimer to rule out PE in a high-probability presentation.

PE investigations and treatment

CT pulmonary angiography is usual confirmatory imaging; V/Q scanning may be appropriate when CTPA is unsuitable. ECG, chest radiograph and blood tests assess alternatives and severity, not definitively exclude PE. Anticoagulation is standard in confirmed PE unless contraindicated; unstable high-risk PE needs urgent specialist reperfusion discussion.

PLAB application: Assess bleeding risk, renal function, pregnancy status and local anticoagulation protocols.

Pneumothorax recognition

Spontaneous pneumothorax can cause sudden unilateral pleuritic pain and dyspnoea with reduced breath sounds. A chest X-ray or bedside ultrasound can confirm in a stable patient. Management depends on symptoms, physiology, underlying lung disease and current pleural guidelines—not size alone.

PLAB application: Avoid assuming every small pneumothorax needs a drain.

Tension pneumothorax

Suspect tension physiology when a patient has severe respiratory compromise and circulatory instability with unilateral reduced air entry. Tracheal deviation is a late and unreliable sign. Call for help and decompress immediately according to local emergency protocol; do not wait for radiography.

PLAB application: Tension pneumothorax is a clinical emergency.

Acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema

Acute pulmonary oedema typically causes severe dyspnoea, orthopnoea, bilateral crackles, hypoxaemia and sometimes pink frothy sputum. Check ECG, troponin when indicated, chest imaging, renal function and bedside ultrasound. Look for ACS, arrhythmia and hypertensive crisis as triggers.

PLAB application: Pulmonary oedema with shock requires careful haemodynamic management.

Treating acute pulmonary oedema

Sit the patient upright, provide appropriate oxygen and consider CPAP/NIV for severe distress when suitable. Give IV loop diuretic when congestion is present; consider nitrates selectively, particularly with hypertension, while monitoring BP. Avoid indiscriminate fluid boluses.

PLAB application: Escalate rapidly if respiratory fatigue or hypotension develops.

Type 1 respiratory failure

Type 1 failure is hypoxaemia without elevated PaCO₂, often from V/Q mismatch or shunt. Causes include pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE and acute lung injury. Blood gas confirms severity; treat the underlying cause and use escalating oxygen or ventilatory support.

PLAB application: A near-normal PaCO₂ does not make profound hypoxaemia safe.

Type 2 respiratory failure

Type 2 failure combines raised PaCO₂ with respiratory acidosis or chronic compensation. Causes include COPD exacerbation, neuromuscular weakness, obesity hypoventilation and sedative toxicity. Use controlled oxygen and repeat arterial gases; NIV may be indicated for persistent acidotic hypercapnia under specialist protocols.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose chronic CO₂ retention from a single gas without context.

Blood gas interpretation

Interpret pH, PaCO₂, bicarbonate, PaO₂ and lactate together, considering supplemental oxygen and clinical context. Acidaemia with elevated PaCO₂ indicates respiratory acidosis. Venous gases can help assess pH and CO₂ trends but do not reliably assess arterial oxygenation.

PLAB application: Clinical deterioration overrides a superficially reassuring blood gas.

NIV, CPAP and intubation

CPAP may help cardiogenic pulmonary oedema; bilevel NIV is used in selected hypercapnic respiratory failure. Check contraindications such as inability to protect airway, vomiting or severe instability. Worsening consciousness, exhaustion or refractory hypoxaemia requires urgent critical-care review for intubation.

PLAB application: Never leave a deteriorating patient on failing NIV without an escalation plan.

Key alternative diagnoses

Consider severe asthma/COPD, pneumonia, anaphylaxis, upper-airway obstruction, metabolic acidosis, anaemia and anxiety only after dangerous causes have been assessed. Use history, ECG, focused examination, imaging and blood tests selectively.

PLAB application: Wheeze does not always mean asthma; cardiac asthma can occur with oedema.

PLAB 2 communication and handover

Explain what you are doing while treating; ask permission where possible without delaying emergency care. Use SBAR: situation, background, assessment including ABCDE and observations, and recommendation for immediate review. For discharge after stabilisation, explain diagnosis, treatment, follow-up and specific return precautions.

PLAB application: A patient who remains unstable is not a discharge candidate.

PLAB 2 — Breathless patient station

Introduce yourself, request help, assess ABCDE aloud, give appropriate oxygen, obtain monitoring and access, identify red flags, initiate time-critical treatment and deliver an SBAR handover. Explain the plan without delaying resuscitation.

Deep Dive — Acute breathlessness

PE: probability before testing

Wells scoring guides investigation in stable adults. A negative D-dimer is useful only in an appropriately low-probability setting. In suspected unstable PE, resuscitate and seek urgent expert imaging and reperfusion decisions.

Tension versus uncomplicated pneumothorax

Uncomplicated pneumothorax may permit imaging and shared decision-making. Tension physiology causes obstructive shock and needs immediate decompression. Do not depend on tracheal deviation.

Pulmonary oedema: haemodynamics

Acute pulmonary oedema may occur with severe hypertension, normal BP or cardiogenic shock. Treatment choices change with perfusion and BP: diuresis, ventilatory support and selected vasodilators are not interchangeable in every patient.

ABG pattern recognition

Type 1 failure: low PaO₂, often low or normal PaCO₂. Type 2: elevated PaCO₂, with pH determining acute respiratory acidosis. Chronic hypercapnia may show raised bicarbonate; acute-on-chronic deterioration may reduce pH.

Ventilation escalation

Track work of breathing, mental state, respiratory rate, gas trends and oxygen requirement. Inability to protect the airway, worsening acidosis or persistent hypoxaemia despite support needs urgent critical care.

Exam pitfalls

Normal SpO₂ does not rule out PE; clear lungs do not rule out serious disease; chest X-ray cannot exclude PE; a patient with severe respiratory distress may deteriorate despite initially preserved BP.
