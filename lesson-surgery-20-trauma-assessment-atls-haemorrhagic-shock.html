Trauma Assessment, ATLS Principles & Haemorrhagic Shock

Surgery · Lesson 20 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Major trauma: structured initial assessment

Time-critical: activate the major trauma team, use a structured <C>ABCDE primary survey, treat life-threatening findings as they are identified, and reassess after every intervention. Follow local major-trauma and massive-haemorrhage protocols.

1 · Preparation, handover and priorities

Pre-alert and assemble trauma team, resuscitation equipment, blood products and imaging. Receive an ATMIST handover: Age, Time, Mechanism, Injuries, Signs and Treatment. Wear PPE, allocate team roles and document vital signs, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), temperature and response to treatment. A normal initial blood pressure does not rule out significant bleeding, especially in younger patients.

2 · <C> Catastrophic external haemorrhage

Immediately control life-threatening external bleeding with direct pressure, haemostatic dressing or a correctly applied tourniquet for severe limb haemorrhage when indicated; record tourniquet time. Suspected pelvic bleeding after high-energy trauma warrants a pelvic binder positioned over the greater trochanters, not the iliac crests, with minimal pelvic manipulation. Consider early massive haemorrhage protocol activation and urgent haemorrhage control.

3 · A: airway with cervical-spine protection

Assess whether the patient can speak, look for obstruction, blood, vomit, facial injury and stridor. Maintain manual in-line stabilisation when indicated; use jaw thrust, suction, airway adjuncts and oxygen as appropriate. Call an experienced airway clinician early for threatened airway, severe head injury or reduced consciousness; definitive airway management must include a failed-airway plan. Avoid unnecessary movement while protecting airway and ventilation.

4 · B: breathing and chest injuries

Assess respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, chest movement, tracheal position, percussion, auscultation and signs of respiratory distress. Treat tension pneumothorax clinically without waiting for imaging when there is haemodynamic instability or severe respiratory compromise; decompress immediately using the locally recommended technique and follow with definitive chest drainage. Recognise open pneumothorax, massive haemothorax, flail chest and pulmonary contusion. Oxygen is titrated to clinical needs; support ventilation when required.

5 · C: circulation and haemorrhagic shock

Assess pulse, perfusion, mental state, blood pressure, capillary refill, skin and obvious bleeding. Suspect concealed haemorrhage in chest, abdomen, pelvis, retroperitoneum and long-bone fractures. Obtain two large-bore IV lines or intraosseous access if needed. Send FBC, coagulation, fibrinogen, blood gas/lactate, electrolytes, group and crossmatch; monitor trends. Activate local massive transfusion protocol early for major bleeding, prioritising blood components and definitive bleeding control rather than large volumes of crystalloid. Avoid hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy.

6 · Tranexamic acid and haemostatic resuscitation

For major trauma with active or suspected active bleeding, give tranexamic acid as soon as possible, within 3 hours of injury according to local protocol (commonly 1 g IV over 10 minutes followed by 1 g over 8 hours). Do not routinely give beyond 3 hours unless specialist indications apply. Use balanced blood component resuscitation per protocol, monitor calcium, temperature and coagulation; consider early interventional radiology or operating theatre for source control. A haemodynamically unstable patient with suspected bleeding needs immediate senior decisions rather than prolonged imaging.

7 · D: disability and neurological injury

Measure GCS (E/V/M), pupil size/reactivity, limb movement and blood glucose. Assess for spinal cord injury and seizures. Prevent hypoxia and hypotension in traumatic brain injury; urgently involve anaesthesia/neurosurgery when indicated. Reassess neurological status repeatedly and document changes.

8 · E: exposure and environmental control

Fully inspect front and back with a controlled log roll only when safe and needed; examine for penetrating wounds, deformity and bruising. Preserve dignity and prevent hypothermia with warm blankets, warm fluids/blood and minimal exposure. Record temperature.

9 · Imaging and secondary survey

Use eFAST as an adjunct for suspected intraperitoneal/pericardial fluid and pneumothorax, but negative eFAST does not exclude internal bleeding. CT is for patients sufficiently stable to transfer and when it will inform management; do not delay operative or interventional haemorrhage control in unstable patients. Once immediate threats are addressed, undertake head-to-toe secondary survey, AMPLE history (Allergies, Medications, Past history/Pregnancy, Last meal, Events), and repeat primary survey if deterioration occurs.

10 · Shock differentiation and special populations

Haemorrhagic shock may cause tachycardia, cool clammy skin, narrow pulse pressure and reduced urine output, but beta-blockers, older age and pregnancy can mask patterns. Neurogenic shock can cause hypotension with relative bradycardia after spinal injury; obstructive shock includes tension pneumothorax and tamponade. Children can maintain blood pressure until late; older adults may deteriorate with apparently modest signs. Reassess response to resuscitation and seek the bleeding source.

11 · Trauma destinations and communication

Use major trauma network pathways, immediate surgical/IR/anaesthetic escalation, and clear team communication. Explain procedures to the patient where possible, involve relatives appropriately, document capacity/consent in emergencies, and use a structured handover at transfer.

High-yield trauma algorithms

Primary survey: <C>ABCDE

Catastrophic bleeding control → Airway with C-spine protection → Breathing and immediate chest threats → Circulation and haemorrhage control → Disability (GCS, pupils, glucose) → Exposure and temperature. Treat threats immediately, then repeat survey.

Suspected major haemorrhage

Call trauma team and activate massive haemorrhage protocol → direct pressure/tourniquet or pelvic binder when appropriate → IV/IO access, bloods and crossmatch → blood-component resuscitation and early TXA within 3 hours → definitive surgery or interventional radiology → monitor temperature, calcium, coagulation and response.

Unstable chest trauma

Severe respiratory compromise with suspected tension pneumothorax → immediate decompression without waiting for X-ray → chest drain and ongoing resuscitation. Consider massive haemothorax and tamponade when shock persists.

Imaging decision

Unstable and ongoing suspected internal haemorrhage → urgent haemorrhage control, bedside adjuncts only if they do not delay. Stable enough for transfer → CT according to injury pattern and major trauma protocol. Negative eFAST cannot rule out injury.

After primary survey

Reassess <C>ABCDE → secondary head-to-toe survey + AMPLE → definitive care, analgesia, tetanus assessment when indicated, documentation and handover.

Four clinical decision cases

Case 1 — Road traffic collision, hypotension

A 27-year-old after high-speed collision has tachycardia, BP 82/50, pelvic pain and cool skin. Priorities: trauma activation, <C>ABCDE, pelvic binder over greater trochanters, blood-component resuscitation and urgent definitive haemorrhage control; avoid repeated pelvic springing.

Case 2 — Tension pneumothorax

A patient with blunt chest trauma becomes hypoxic, hypotensive and has markedly reduced unilateral breath sounds. Priorities: suspect tension pneumothorax, immediate decompression by trained staff without waiting for radiology, then chest drain.

Case 3 — Normal BP but occult bleeding

A 22-year-old after a fall has HR 126, pale cool skin and BP 118/76. Priorities: recognise possible compensated shock, repeat observations, seek concealed bleeding and escalate; do not be reassured by systolic BP alone.

Case 4 — Head injury and deteriorating consciousness

A 63-year-old on anticoagulation after a fall has decreasing GCS and vomiting. Priorities: protect airway and cervical spine as appropriate, avoid hypoxia/hypotension, urgent senior and neurosurgical assessment, imaging when safe, and anticoagulant reversal discussion per protocol.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Major trauma primary survey

Station: A 35-year-old arrives after a motorcycle collision, confused, tachycardic and with a painful pelvis.

  1. Introduce yourself to the team, call for senior trauma/anaesthetic help, apply PPE and request monitoring.
  2. Check for catastrophic bleeding: apply pressure to visible bleeding; consider pelvic binder if mechanism and findings suggest pelvic fracture.
  3. A: assess speech and airway patency; protect C-spine, suction and escalate threatened airway.
  4. B: assess respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, chest expansion and breath sounds; treat life-threatening chest injury immediately.
  5. C: check pulse, BP and perfusion; establish IV/IO access, send crossmatch and bloods, activate massive haemorrhage protocol if indicated and request TXA within 3 hours.
  6. D: assess GCS, pupils, limb movements and glucose.
  7. E: inspect for further injury while keeping the patient warm.
  8. Reassess continuously, discuss bedside imaging versus CT based on stability, and organise definitive haemorrhage control.
  9. Communicate an ATMIST/SBAR handover and document times, interventions and responses.

Examiner priorities: life-saving actions before detailed history, no delay to treat tension pneumothorax, correct pelvic binder placement, early blood products, timely TXA and repeated reassessment.

Rapid revision: 12 essentials

  1. Use <C>ABCDE; treat immediate threats as discovered.
  2. ATMIST is a useful prehospital trauma handover.
  3. Control catastrophic bleeding before the rest of the primary survey.
  4. Pelvic binders sit over the greater trochanters.
  5. Threatened airway requires early expert support and C-spine protection as appropriate.
  6. Unstable suspected tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis: decompress without imaging delay.
  7. Normal blood pressure does not exclude haemorrhagic shock.
  8. Major trauma bleeding requires early massive haemorrhage protocol and source control.
  9. Give TXA promptly within 3 hours when indicated; avoid routine late administration.
  10. Prevent hypothermia, coagulopathy and acidosis; monitor calcium.
  11. Negative eFAST does not exclude internal injury; CT depends on stability.
  12. Repeat <C>ABCDE after interventions and deterioration; secondary survey follows stabilisation.
