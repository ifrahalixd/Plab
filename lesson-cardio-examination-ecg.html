Learning objectives

Peripheral vascular disease and limb-threatening emergencies

Recognise chronic PAD and acute limb ischaemia, interpret ABPI, identify threatened limbs and select safe UK referral and treatment pathways.

Emergency rule: A suddenly painful, cold, numb or weak limb is a vascular emergency. Call the vascular team immediately; do not delay for routine testing. Do not use this educational page as a clinical protocol.

Browser text-to-speech, if supported.

Before you touch the patient

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, gain consent, ask about pain, wash hands, position at 45°, expose chest appropriately and maintain dignity.

Clinical application: Look for immediate instability: severe chest pain, breathlessness, shock or altered consciousness. Escalate rather than completing a routine examination.

General inspection and hands

Observe distress, scars, devices, oxygen, oedema and cachexia. Examine hands for clubbing, splinter haemorrhages, peripheral cyanosis and capillary refill.

Clinical application: Check pulse rate, rhythm and character; compare radial pulses if indicated. Irregularly irregular pulse suggests AF, but confirm with ECG.

Face, neck and JVP

Look for conjunctival pallor, central cyanosis and xanthelasma. Assess jugular venous pressure with the patient at about 45° and adequate lighting.

Clinical application: Raised JVP can indicate right heart failure, fluid overload or tamponade; assess the full clinical context.

Precordial examination

Inspect for scars and visible pulsations. Palpate apex beat position and character; check for heaves and thrills. Auscultate with diaphragm and bell at aortic, pulmonary, tricuspid and mitral areas.

Clinical application: Listen for S1/S2, added sounds and murmurs; describe timing, site, radiation, pitch and response to manoeuvres.

Completing the cardiovascular examination

Auscultate lung bases for crackles, examine sacral and ankle oedema, assess peripheral pulses and consider BP in both arms if clinically indicated.

Clinical application: Summarise findings, offer a differential, request observations, ECG and echocardiography when appropriate.

ECG systematic approach

Confirm patient details, date/time, calibration (usually 25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV), quality and rate. Then assess rhythm, axis, intervals, QRS morphology, ST segments, T waves and compare previous ECGs.

Clinical application: Always interpret with symptoms and observations. An ECG cannot independently rule out acute coronary syndrome.

Heart rate and rhythm

At 25 mm/s each large square is 0.2 s and each small square is 0.04 s. For a regular rhythm, rate ≈300 divided by large squares between R waves. For irregular rhythms count QRS complexes in 10 seconds ×6.

Clinical application: Sinus rhythm has appropriate P waves preceding QRS complexes with consistent PR intervals. Check for AF, flutter, ectopy and heart block.

Cardiac axis and intervals

Normal adult frontal QRS axis is roughly −30° to +90°. PR is typically 120–200 ms, QRS under 120 ms and QTc depends on rate and sex.

Clinical application: Prolonged QTc raises torsades risk. Automated QTc can be inaccurate; check manually when important.

Ischaemia and infarction patterns

Assess contiguous lead ST elevation/depression, dynamic T-wave changes and pathological Q waves. Inferior leads II, III, aVF; lateral I, aVL, V5–V6; anterior V1–V4.

Clinical application: Acute symptoms with concerning ECG require urgent ACS assessment. STEMI equivalents and posterior infarction can be missed by simplistic criteria.

Bradyarrhythmias and AV block

First-degree AV block has PR >200 ms with all P waves conducted. Mobitz I shows progressive PR lengthening before a dropped beat. Mobitz II has intermittent non-conducted P waves without progressive PR lengthening. Complete heart block shows AV dissociation.

Clinical application: Unstable bradycardia needs emergency assessment and current resuscitation algorithms; high-grade block may require pacing.

Tachyarrhythmias

Narrow-complex regular tachycardia suggests SVT or flutter with fixed conduction; irregular narrow-complex rhythm suggests AF or variable flutter. Wide-complex tachycardia should be treated as VT until proven otherwise.

Clinical application: Unstable tachycardia requires immediate resuscitation-team input and synchronised cardioversion when indicated.

PLAB OSCE presentation

Give a concise structured presentation: patient comfort and observations, positive findings, relevant negatives, likely diagnosis, urgent concerns and next tests.

Clinical application: Avoid claiming 'normal' findings you did not assess. Explain limitations and ask for senior help when red flags are present.

PLAB 2: focused OSCE approach

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent, hand hygiene and 45° positioning.
  2. Inspect and examine hands, pulse, face and JVP.
  3. Inspect, palpate and auscultate precordium; listen at lung bases and check oedema.
  4. Summarise positive and relevant negative findings and suggest ECG, observations and echo.
  5. Interpret ECG systematically and escalate red flags.

Advanced OSCE and ECG reasoning

Deep Dive

Why the JVP rises

The jugular venous pulse reflects right atrial pressure. It may be elevated in right heart failure, tamponade, constriction and fluid overload; measurement is technique-dependent.

Heart sounds and valve disease

S1 reflects atrioventricular valve closure and S2 semilunar closure. Murmur timing distinguishes stenosis from regurgitation across different valves.

Electrical conduction

Sinus node → atria → AV node (PR delay) → His-Purkinje system → ventricles (QRS). AV nodal and infranodal disease can produce different block patterns.

ECG lead territories

Inferior: II, III, aVF. Anterior: V1–V4. Lateral: I, aVL, V5–V6. These patterns are approximations; coronary anatomy varies.

QT correction

QT changes with heart rate. QTc estimates vary by formula and may be inaccurate at extreme rates. Review drugs, potassium, magnesium and congenital risk when prolonged.

Safety

Normal initial ECG does not exclude ACS. Wide-complex tachycardia may be VT and needs urgent assessment. ECG findings must always be integrated with clinical context.
