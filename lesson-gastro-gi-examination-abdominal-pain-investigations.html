Gastrointestinal Examination, Abdominal Pain & Clinical Investigations

Emergency: Severe sudden abdominal pain, haemodynamic instability, GI bleeding or peritonism needs urgent assessment and escalation.

Approach to abdominal pain

Assess ABCDE and observations first. Clarify onset, site, character, radiation, associated symptoms and severity.

PLAB application: Sudden severe pain, shock or peritonism requires immediate senior review.

SOCRATES history

Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associated symptoms, Time course, Exacerbating/relieving factors and Severity.

PLAB application: Ask about migration of pain, meals, bowel movements and urinary symptoms.

Associated gastrointestinal symptoms

Ask about vomiting, haematemesis, dysphagia, reflux, diarrhoea, constipation, melaena, rectal bleeding, jaundice and weight loss.

PLAB application: Quantify bleeding and dehydration and establish whether flatus is passing.

Background and risks

Review operations, medications including NSAIDs and anticoagulants, alcohol, smoking, travel, pregnancy possibility and family history.

PLAB application: Previous abdominal surgery raises suspicion of adhesional obstruction.

General examination and consent

Introduce yourself, wash hands, confirm identity, explain the examination, gain consent and offer a chaperone as appropriate.

PLAB application: Assess pain and dignity; expose from xiphisternum to pubis with appropriate draping.

End-of-bed inspection

Look for distress, pallor, jaundice, cachexia, dehydration, drains, stomas and medical devices. Check vital signs.

PLAB application: A patient who looks unwell needs escalation before a lengthy examination.

Hands, face and chest

Inspect hands for clubbing, palmar erythema, leukonychia and asterixis when relevant. Check sclerae and mouth; inspect for spider naevi.

PLAB application: These findings may support chronic liver disease but are not diagnostic alone.

Abdominal inspection

Observe contour, distension, scars, striae, visible masses, hernias and prominent veins.

PLAB application: Inspect before palpation; ask about the site of maximal tenderness.

Auscultation and percussion

Auscultate bowel sounds if indicated; percuss for tenderness, tympany, liver span and shifting dullness when ascites suspected.

PLAB application: Bowel sounds alone cannot exclude obstruction or establish its cause.

Superficial and deep palpation

Start away from pain, assess tenderness, guarding, rigidity, masses and organomegaly. Examine liver and spleen appropriately.

PLAB application: Involuntary rigidity or rebound/percussion tenderness suggests peritoneal irritation.

Special tests and finishing

Use Murphy’s sign, renal angle tenderness, hernial examination or rectal examination only when indicated and consented. Thank patient, cover them and wash hands.

PLAB application: Offer examination of external genitalia/pelvis when clinically indicated, with consent and appropriate clinician.

Pain location patterns

RUQ: biliary/liver; epigastric: peptic/pancreatic/cardiac; RIF: appendicitis; LIF: diverticulitis; diffuse: obstruction, ischaemia or peritonitis.

PLAB application: Location is a clue, not a definitive diagnosis; consider extra-abdominal causes.

Initial bedside investigations

Record observations, NEWS2, glucose, urine dip, pregnancy test when applicable and ECG when cardiac pathology is possible.

PLAB application: Pregnancy testing is important in relevant patients with acute abdominal pain.

Blood and stool investigations

Choose FBC, U&Es, CRP, LFTs, lipase/amylase, coagulation, lactate and group-and-save according to presentation; stool tests if diarrhoea or bleeding.

PLAB application: Normal blood results do not exclude early serious intra-abdominal disease.

Imaging and endoscopy

Ultrasound for suspected gallbladder disease; CT for selected acute abdominal emergencies; endoscopy for indicated upper or lower GI pathology.

PLAB application: Select imaging based on stability, suspected diagnosis, pregnancy and local pathways.

Emergency management and OSCE synthesis

Resuscitate as needed, give analgesia and antiemetics, keep nil by mouth when appropriate, secure IV access and escalate to surgical/medical teams.

PLAB application: Summarise findings, offer differentials, investigations, management and clear safety-netting.

PLAB 2: Abdominal examination OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm patient identity, explain and gain consent; offer a chaperone.
  2. Ask about pain, ensure privacy, position supine and expose appropriately.
  3. Inspect generally, then hands, face and abdomen.
  4. Auscultate if indicated, percuss, palpate gently away from pain, assess organs and relevant special signs.
  5. Offer appropriate additional examination, thank the patient and cover them.
  6. Summarise positive and negative findings, differential diagnoses and next steps.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Visceral versus somatic pain

Visceral pain is often poorly localised; parietal peritoneal irritation causes more focal pain and guarding.

Why pain migrates in appendicitis

Early visceral midgut pain is periumbilical; later parietal inflammation localises to the right iliac fossa.

Bowel sounds limitation

Neither normal nor absent bowel sounds reliably rule in or rule out obstruction.

Lactate interpretation

Elevated lactate may indicate hypoperfusion but a normal lactate does not exclude early bowel ischaemia.

Imaging trade-offs

Ultrasound is useful for biliary disease; CT may be needed for obstruction, perforation or uncertain acute pathology.

PLAB 2 communication

Explain each step, check pain, preserve dignity, ask permission for sensitive examination and safety-net appropriately.
