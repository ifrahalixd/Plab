ABCDE Assessment, Recognising the Deteriorating Patient & Initial Resuscitation

Emergency priorities: Call for help early; treat airway obstruction, hypoxia, haemorrhage, shock and hypoglycaemia as you find them. Reassess continuously.

Why ABCDE matters

ABCDE is a structured approach to recognising and treating immediately reversible life threats. Assess, intervene, reassess, and call for help early. Do not wait for a definitive diagnosis before treating hypoxia, shock or hypoglycaemia.

PLAB application: Treat problems as you find them; repeat ABCDE after every intervention.

First 30 seconds: safety and response

Check scene safety, infection precautions, responsiveness and whether the patient appears critically unwell. Shout for help and activate the local emergency response. Request monitoring, oxygen, suction, airway equipment, IV access and a resuscitation trolley.

PLAB application: A collapsed or unresponsive patient requires immediate assessment of breathing and circulation.

A — airway assessment

Ask the patient to speak. Listen for stridor, snoring, gurgling or silence; look for foreign bodies, vomit, swelling and reduced consciousness. An obstructed airway can cause rapid hypoxic arrest. Avoid blind finger sweeps.

PLAB application: Unable to speak with stridor or reduced consciousness is an airway emergency.

A — immediate airway actions

Open the airway with head tilt–chin lift if appropriate, or jaw thrust when cervical spine injury is suspected. Suction visible secretions and use trained airway adjuncts as indicated. Escalate promptly to experienced airway clinicians; do not delay definitive airway support when needed.

PLAB application: Protect cervical spine when trauma suggests injury, without delaying oxygenation.

B — breathing assessment

Assess respiratory rate, work of breathing, chest symmetry, air entry, oxygen saturation and ability to speak. Check for cyanosis, wheeze, crackles and unilateral absent breath sounds. A normal SpO₂ does not exclude impending respiratory failure.

PLAB application: Look for exhaustion, silent chest and reduced respiratory effort.

B — oxygen and ventilation

Give oxygen for critical illness while monitoring saturation and titrate as the patient stabilises. Most acutely ill adults have a target saturation of 94–98%; those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure often require 88–92% pending blood gas results and local guidance. Support ventilation if inadequate and seek senior help.

PLAB application: Never withhold urgently needed oxygen from a critically hypoxic patient because of COPD.

B — immediately reversible causes

Consider tension pneumothorax in shock with severe respiratory distress and unilateral reduced air entry; treat clinically without waiting for imaging. Consider anaphylaxis, severe asthma, pulmonary oedema, pneumonia and pulmonary embolism according to presentation.

PLAB application: Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis in an unstable patient.

C — circulation assessment

Measure pulse rate and character, blood pressure, capillary refill, peripheral temperature, mental state and urine output. Examine for external bleeding and signs of fluid overload. Hypotension is a late finding in some forms of shock. Attach ECG and obtain IV or IO access when appropriate.

PLAB application: A normal blood pressure does not exclude compensated shock.

C — shock and fluid decisions

Control catastrophic haemorrhage immediately. Identify likely shock type: hypovolaemic, distributive, cardiogenic or obstructive. Use appropriate IV crystalloid boluses when indicated with frequent reassessment; use particular caution in heart failure and suspected cardiogenic shock. Activate major haemorrhage or sepsis pathways when indicated.

PLAB application: Fluids are not a universal treatment for every shocked patient.

D — disability and glucose

Assess AVPU or GCS, pupils, focal neurological signs, seizures and capillary blood glucose. Treat hypoglycaemia promptly using the local protocol. Consider opioid toxicity when respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils coexist; support ventilation and administer naloxone when indicated.

PLAB application: Every patient with altered consciousness needs a bedside glucose check.

E — exposure and temperature

Expose sufficiently to inspect for rashes, trauma, bleeding, infection, pressure injuries or medical devices. Check temperature, then cover the patient and prevent hypothermia. Respect dignity and preserve warmth.

PLAB application: A non-blanching rash with systemic illness may signal meningococcal disease.

Monitoring and investigations

Use continuous pulse oximetry, ECG, repeated observations and an early warning score such as NEWS2 for adults where appropriate. Obtain blood gas including lactate, FBC, U&E, cultures, ECG, imaging and other tests based on clinical suspicion. Tests must not delay urgent treatment.

PLAB application: Trends and clinical appearance matter more than a single observation.

Recognising sepsis and escalation

Suspect sepsis when infection is associated with organ dysfunction or deterioration. Assess perfusion, respiratory function, mental status, urine output and lactate. Escalate rapidly, obtain cultures when feasible without delaying treatment, and follow current NICE and local antimicrobial guidance.

PLAB application: Not every febrile patient has sepsis; an afebrile patient can still have it.

Peri-arrest and cardiac arrest

If unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate resuscitation response and begin CPR and AED/defibrillator use according to current Resuscitation Council UK guidance. Agonal gasps are not normal breathing. In peri-arrest patients, correct reversible causes and prepare for rapid deterioration.

PLAB application: Do not spend prolonged time checking a pulse in an unresponsive, non-breathing adult.

Handover, documentation and teamwork

Use SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation. State vital signs, ABCDE findings, interventions, response, concerns and specific help requested. Allocate roles, use closed-loop communication and document timings and reassessments.

PLAB application: A concise escalation call should make the urgency unmistakable.

PLAB 2 practical approach

Verbalise your immediate safety check, request help, start ABCDE, treat each abnormality, monitor response and summarise your working diagnosis and plan. Explain to patient or family when possible. Avoid pretending to perform unavailable procedures; state what you would do and who you would call.

PLAB application: Safe prioritisation and reassessment are central to emergency OSCE performance.

PLAB 2 — deteriorating patient station

Introduce yourself while requesting immediate assistance. Perform an audible ABCDE assessment, initiate appropriate first-line interventions, recheck observations, identify likely causes and hand over using SBAR. Explain your actions succinctly and maintain patient dignity.

Deep Dive — Emergency clinical reasoning

ABCDE is iterative

When a new problem appears, return to airway and reassess. Repeated observations reveal whether treatment is working. Escalate before the patient arrests.

Oxygen in respiratory failure

The target saturation depends on clinical context. Give oxygen immediately for life-threatening hypoxaemia, then adjust after blood gases and senior review. Hypercapnic-risk patients need controlled oxygen once stabilised.

Shock phenotypes

Hypovolaemic shock requires haemorrhage or fluid-loss control; distributive shock may be septic or anaphylactic; cardiogenic shock may worsen with indiscriminate fluid; obstructive shock requires relief of the obstruction.

Recognising airway failure

A noisy airway may indicate partial obstruction; silence with ineffective breathing can indicate complete obstruction. Call anaesthetics or critical care early. Use basic manoeuvres while definitive support is arranged.

Resuscitation decisions

CPR and defibrillation pathways are for cardiac arrest, whereas ABCDE addresses the deteriorating patient with signs of life. Follow current adult or paediatric life-support algorithms as appropriate.

PLAB 2 marking mindset

Prioritise immediate threats, communicate clearly, avoid unsafe delays, state limitations, and repeatedly assess the effect of each intervention.
