Organ Donation, Death Certification & Medical Ethics

Objectives: Understand donation consent, deceased donor pathways, diagnosis/confirmation of death, medical certification and referral to the coroner, and sensitive communication with families. This is UK-focused PLAB teaching: legal rules vary by nation and death certification procedures have changed over time.

1 · Organ donation: consent and authorisation

Donation is voluntary and requires a lawful basis. England, Wales and Scotland operate deemed-consent/opt-out systems for eligible adults, subject to exceptions and safeguards; Northern Ireland also has a soft opt-out system. Eligibility, residency, age and capacity exclusions differ by nation. Opt-out does not mean every deceased person is automatically a donor. Check the relevant register, applicable law, the patient's known wishes, clinical suitability and specialist donation-team advice. Family discussion remains central to establishing wishes and supporting loved ones.

2 · Donation pathways

Donation after brainstem death (DBD): death is confirmed using established neurological criteria in an appropriately assessed patient. Donation after circulatory death (DCD): death follows permanent cessation of circulatory function, with relevant clinical and legal safeguards. Not all patients are medically suitable; do not promise donation. Refer potential donors promptly to the specialist nurse for organ donation (SNOD) according to local policy.

3 · Diagnosing and confirming death

Diagnosis of death and medical certification are different processes. Death can be confirmed following established circulatory criteria or, in appropriate circumstances, neurological criteria under current professional guidance. Neurological testing requires stringent prerequisites, exclusion of confounders and authorised experienced clinicians; it is not equivalent to a coma, persistent vegetative state or a single absent reflex. A junior doctor should not attempt to apply a memorised test list independently.

4 · Medical certification of cause of death (MCCD)

The MCCD records the medical opinion of the cause of death, not merely that death occurred. Part I describes the causal sequence, with the immediate cause above underlying causes; Part II records other significant contributing conditions. Avoid vague mechanisms such as 'cardiac arrest' alone. In England and Wales, the statutory medical examiner system reviews deaths not investigated by the coroner; certification processes and requirements differ across UK nations.

5 · Coroner and procurator fiscal referral

In England and Wales, deaths potentially requiring coroner investigation include suspected unnatural or violent deaths, unknown cause and certain deaths in custody or state detention; use the current notification regulations and local medical examiner/coroner procedures. In Scotland, reportable deaths are referred to the Procurator Fiscal, not an English coroner. Do not issue a speculative cause of death to avoid referral.

6 · Communication, confidentiality and ethics

Provide clear, compassionate explanations; give relatives time; use an interpreter where needed; respect cultural and religious concerns without assumptions. Confidentiality duties continue after death, subject to lawful disclosure. Separate clinical decisions about withdrawal of treatment from donation discussions; decisions must be based on the patient's clinical interests, not organ retrieval needs. Declare and manage conflicts of interest.

7 · PLAB examination traps

Remember: confirmation of death ≠ MCCD; brainstem death ≠ coma; opt-out ≠ guaranteed donation; 'cardiac arrest' alone is not an adequate causal diagnosis; suspicious deaths need appropriate referral; do not pressure families or promise organ suitability.

Two practical decision pathways

A · Potential deceased organ donor

  1. Identify possible donation according to clinical triggers and local policy.
  2. Continue appropriate patient-centred clinical care and involve senior staff.
  3. Refer promptly to the specialist nurse for organ donation.
  4. Confirm death only through the appropriate clinical pathway and authorised team.
  5. Explore known wishes, donor register status and applicable consent/authorisation law with specialists.
  6. Support the family with compassionate, non-coercive communication and documentation.

B · After a patient dies

  1. Verify/confirm death using the appropriate procedure and record it.
  2. Inform senior clinician and communicate sensitively with family.
  3. Review the clinical history and determine whether referral to coroner/Procurator Fiscal is required.
  4. Follow the relevant nation's medical examiner and MCCD process when appropriate.
  5. Document cause-of-death reasoning, referrals and communication; respect confidentiality.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Family asks about organ donation

A relative asks whether their loved one could donate organs. Best approach: respond sensitively, avoid promises, and involve the specialist donation team to check eligibility, wishes and lawful authorisation.

Case 2 · Coma after severe brain injury

A ventilated patient is unresponsive. A colleague says they are 'brain dead' without formal assessment. Best approach: do not equate coma with death; escalate for the correct specialist assessment under current criteria.

Case 3 · Unexpected death after a fall

An older adult dies after a fall with possible traumatic injury. Best approach: preserve accurate documentation and follow coroner notification/local medical examiner advice rather than certifying a speculative natural cause.

Case 4 · MCCD causal sequence

A patient dies of pneumonia due to advanced motor neurone disease. Best approach: document a medically supportable causal sequence, not 'cardiac arrest' alone, and follow the current certification pathway.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Talking to a bereaved family about donation

Station: A patient's partner asks, 'He always wanted to donate his organs. Can you make sure that happens?'

Consultation structure

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm relationship and ensure privacy.
  2. Offer condolences; ask what the partner understands about the patient's condition and death.
  3. Listen to the patient's expressed wishes and the family's concerns.
  4. Explain that donation may be possible but depends on clinical suitability and lawful authorisation.
  5. Offer referral to the specialist nurse for organ donation; do not promise a specific outcome.
  6. Explain next steps clearly and check understanding.
  7. Respect cultural, spiritual and religious preferences without assumptions.
  8. Offer bereavement support and document the discussion.
