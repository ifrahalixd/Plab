BIOCHEMISTRY

Ionisation, pKa and membrane crossing

Weak acids and bases exist in ionised and unionised forms. The unionised fraction often crosses lipid membranes more readily. Environmental pH and drug pKa influence this balance, but transporters, solubility and formulation also matter. Urinary alkalinisation can increase renal elimination of selected weak acids in specific poisoning situations under toxicology guidance; it is not a general strategy for all overdoses.

RECEPTOR SCIENCE

Affinity, intrinsic efficacy and signalling

Affinity reflects binding tendency; intrinsic efficacy describes receptor activation after binding. Receptors include ligand-gated ion channels, G-protein-coupled receptors, enzyme-linked receptors and intracellular nuclear receptors. Effects may occur in milliseconds (ion channels) or over hours to days (changes in gene transcription).

PHARMACOGENETICS

Why patients respond differently

Genetic variants in metabolic enzymes or drug targets can alter exposure or effect. Examples include CYP2D6 variability affecting codeine activation and HLA-associated severe hypersensitivity with particular medicines in susceptible populations. Clinical testing depends on the drug and guideline; do not assume routine testing for every prescription.

CLINICAL CALCULATIONS

Concentration and units

Always write units before calculating: mg/L × L = mg. A maintenance dose rate is mg/hour when clearance is L/hour and target concentration is mg/L. Check whether a target is total or unbound concentration and whether bioavailability is expressed as a fraction rather than a percentage.

THERAPEUTIC MONITORING

Drug levels are only part of the picture

Sampling time, dose history, steady-state status, renal function, protein binding and clinical effect determine interpretation. Lithium, digoxin, gentamicin and some antiseizure medicines have drug-specific protocols. A random level without the dosing timeline can be difficult to interpret.

OSCE

Explain a renal dose adjustment

“Your kidneys help remove this medicine. Your recent blood test suggests it may stay in your body longer than expected, which could increase side effects. We need to review the dose and arrange monitoring while still treating your condition safely.” Check understanding, concerns, adherence and follow-up.

In 60 seconds: the essentials

  • Pharmacokinetics (PK): absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) determine concentration over time.
  • Pharmacodynamics (PD): receptor binding, efficacy, potency and concentration–response relationships determine effects.
  • Bioavailability (F): fraction of administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged; IV administration has F = 1.
  • Clearance: volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time; reduced clearance generally increases exposure at a given dosing rate.
  • Half-life: depends on both volume of distribution and clearance; approximately 4–5 half-lives to approach steady state with linear kinetics.
  • Clinical rule: review kidney/liver function, interactions, therapeutic index and monitoring before changing a dose.

1. Pharmacokinetics: what the body does to a drug

After a drug is administered, its molecules enter the circulation, move between plasma and tissues, may be chemically transformed, and are eventually eliminated. The concentration at a receptor is influenced by all four processes. PK is particularly important when choosing a route, deciding dose intervals, managing organ impairment and interpreting drug levels.

2. Absorption, routes and first-pass metabolism

Absorption is movement from the administration site into the systemic circulation. Oral absorption depends on dissolution, gastric emptying, intestinal transit, intestinal surface area, transport proteins, pH and food effects. A drug can be absorbed from the gut yet have low systemic bioavailability if intestinal or hepatic first-pass metabolism removes much of it.

Bioavailability (F) is the proportion of an administered dose reaching systemic circulation unchanged. Intravenous drugs bypass absorption barriers and first-pass metabolism, so F is 100%. Oral bioavailability varies; do not assume oral and IV milligram doses are interchangeable. A switch between formulations or routes may require a specific conversion and monitoring plan.

Clinical link: glyceryl trinitrate for rapid angina relief is given sublingually because swallowed GTN undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism. This does not mean all sublingual drugs act instantly.

3. Distribution, protein binding and volume of distribution

Distribution is the reversible movement of drug between blood and tissues. Blood flow, capillary permeability, lipid solubility, ionisation, transporters and plasma protein binding influence where a drug accumulates. Albumin binds many acidic drugs; alpha-1-acid glycoprotein binds some basic drugs. Usually the unbound fraction can cross membranes, bind receptors and be cleared.

Volume of distribution (Vd) is an apparent mathematical volume: amount of drug in the body divided by measured plasma concentration. It is not a literal anatomical compartment. A large Vd suggests extensive tissue distribution relative to plasma concentration; a small Vd suggests a larger proportion remains within plasma or extracellular fluid.

Formula: Vd = amount of drug in body ÷ plasma concentration. For IV administration, an approximate loading dose = target plasma concentration × Vd (with units made consistent). For non-IV routes, divide by F.

Clinical relevance: fluid overload, burns, critical illness, obesity and hypoalbuminaemia can change distribution. Low albumin may increase the unbound fraction of highly protein-bound medicines; interpreting a total measured level without context can mislead. Some drugs require measurement of free concentrations in selected situations.

4. Metabolism: Phase I, Phase II and interactions

Metabolism generally makes drugs more water-soluble, although metabolites may remain active or become toxic. Phase I reactions include oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis; cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes are important for many drugs. Phase II reactions conjugate molecules (for example glucuronidation), often facilitating elimination. These phases are not compulsory sequential steps for every medicine.

Do not memorise that every inhibitor raises every drug concentration: the outcome depends on the pathway, whether the drug is a prodrug, and the role of transporters. Use the current BNF interaction checker and clinical pharmacy support for specific combinations.

5. Clearance, half-life and renal elimination

Clearance (CL) describes the volume of plasma effectively cleared of drug per unit time. Total clearance may include renal and non-renal pathways. The kidneys eliminate many unchanged medicines and metabolites through glomerular filtration, tubular secretion and reabsorption. Renal impairment can therefore increase drug exposure and prolong effects.

For drugs with approximately first-order elimination, the elimination rate is proportional to concentration: a constant fraction is removed per unit time. Half-life is the time for concentration to fall by half during the elimination phase, and is approximately 0.693 × Vd / CL. It is not a fixed property independent of the patient's condition.

Some medicines exhibit capacity-limited or nonlinear elimination. For example, phenytoin can show disproportionate increases in concentration after small dose increases, making careful titration and level interpretation important. Do not apply a simple constant half-life formula indiscriminately to nonlinear kinetics.

Safety: an estimated GFR reported by the laboratory is not interchangeable with every drug-dosing estimate. Some medicines use creatinine clearance calculated by Cockcroft–Gault, and dosing recommendations may depend on weight, age and the specific product. Follow current BNF/SmPC guidance.

6. Loading doses, maintenance doses and steady state

A loading dose is used when the target concentration is needed promptly; it depends mainly on Vd and bioavailability. A maintenance dose rate replaces drug removed over time and depends mainly on clearance and bioavailability. A loading dose does not eliminate the need for careful monitoring.

Worked example: a hypothetical IV medicine has Vd 40 L and a target plasma concentration of 5 mg/L. Its theoretical loading dose is 40 × 5 = 200 mg. This is a mathematical demonstration, not a prescribing recommendation. Actual loading doses depend on formulation, patient factors, desired clinical effect and validated guidance.

Renal impairment: if clearance falls but Vd stays similar, a maintenance dose or dosing interval often needs adjustment while the initial loading dose may be less affected. However, critical illness and fluid shifts can alter Vd, and toxicity risk can change the entire regimen. Never automatically apply a universal percentage reduction.

7. Pharmacodynamics: what a drug does to the body

Pharmacodynamics links drug concentration to its biological and clinical effects. An agonist activates a receptor; a competitive antagonist binds without activating it and competes with an agonist; a partial agonist produces a lower maximum response than a full agonist in a given system, even when occupying receptors. An inverse agonist reduces constitutive receptor activity when that activity is present.

Potency describes how much drug is needed for a specified effect; efficacy describes the maximum effect achievable in the system. A more potent drug is not necessarily more clinically effective or safer. Competitive antagonism commonly shifts an agonist concentration–response curve rightwards without reducing maximum response when sufficient agonist can overcome the block; irreversible or noncompetitive antagonism may reduce maximum response.

TermMeaningClinical example

Therapeutic window is the concentration range associated with useful effect and acceptable toxicity, when a meaningful concentration range is established. Drug response also depends on receptor regulation, tolerance, comorbidity, genetics and interactions. For many drugs, clinical response is more useful than routine blood levels.

8. Applying PK and PD safely at the bedside

Step 1 — Define the therapeutic goal.

What condition is being treated? Is the target symptom relief, prevention of events, a measured laboratory endpoint or a drug concentration?

Step 2 — Assess the patient.

Check allergies, current medicines, kidney and liver function, pregnancy potential where relevant, age, weight, hydration and relevant comorbidities.

Step 3 — Choose drug, route and formulation.

Consider absorption, speed of onset, bioavailability, swallowing ability, interactions and whether the patient is clinically stable.

Step 4 — Plan dose, interval and monitoring.

Consider clearance, accumulation, therapeutic window and whether monitoring involves symptoms, ECG, blood counts, renal function or drug concentrations.

Step 5 — Reassess after changes.

Clinical status, new interacting medicines and changing renal function may require repeat review; document the reason for any dose adjustment.

9. Worked clinical cases

Case A — Gentamicin in acute kidney injury

A 74-year-old receiving gentamicin develops a rising creatinine. Gentamicin is eliminated mainly by the kidneys, and reduced clearance can increase exposure and toxicity risk. Reassess indication, renal function, dosing regimen and timing of serum levels using the local gentamicin protocol; involve pharmacy/microbiology and review for nephrotoxicity and ototoxicity. Do not simply repeat the previous dose.

Case B — Phenytoin and a small dose increase

A patient taking phenytoin develops nystagmus and ataxia after a dose increase. Phenytoin has capacity-limited metabolism; a small increase can lead to a large concentration rise. Assess urgently for toxicity, check a suitably timed level and albumin/renal context, and seek specialist advice rather than applying a linear dose calculation.

Case C — Morphine in advanced renal impairment

An older patient with reduced kidney function becomes unusually drowsy after repeated morphine doses. Active morphine metabolites can accumulate in renal impairment, increasing sedation and respiratory depression risk. Assess ABCDE and respiratory rate, withhold further opioid pending urgent review, treat opioid toxicity according to emergency guidance when indicated, and plan a safer analgesic strategy.

Case D — Starting a strong enzyme inducer

A patient using combined hormonal contraception starts an enzyme-inducing antiseizure medicine. Increased metabolism may reduce contraceptive effectiveness. Do not simply reassure: check current contraception guidance, discuss an effective alternative or additional method and document counselling with specialist/pharmacy support.

10. PLAB 1 and PLAB 2 examination reasoning

PLAB 1 frequently tests the next safest action: recognise toxicity, identify a relevant interaction, or adjust prescribing based on renal function rather than calculating a theoretical dose in isolation. In PLAB 2, explain to the patient why a medicine or dose is being changed, check understanding and adherence, ask about over-the-counter products, and arrange monitoring and safety-netting.

Common traps: (1) potency ≠ efficacy; (2) half-life depends on Vd and CL; (3) IV and oral doses may not be interchangeable; (4) a normal total drug concentration can be misleading when protein binding changes; (5) the same creatinine value does not imply identical renal function in all patients.

    Three practical checkpoints

    Renal impairment: use the medicine-specific renal function measure and current dosing guidance, not a generic reduction.
    Interaction: consider whether inhibition/induction affects an active drug, a prodrug or a transporter.
    Suspected toxicity: assess clinical stability first; stop or withhold further doses when appropriate, seek urgent advice and use drug-specific protocols.
