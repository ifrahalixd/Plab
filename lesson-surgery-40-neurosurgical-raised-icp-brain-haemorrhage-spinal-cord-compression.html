Neurosurgical Emergencies: Raised Intracranial Pressure, Brain Haemorrhage & Spinal Cord Compression

Surgery · Lesson 40 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Neurosurgical emergencies — PLAB high-yield

Time-critical: falling GCS, new focal neurological deficit, unequal pupils, thunderclap headache, seizure with altered consciousness, and suspected malignant spinal cord compression need immediate emergency escalation. Stabilise ABCDE while arranging definitive imaging and specialist care.

1 · Initial assessment and priorities

Use ABCDE, check capillary glucose, oxygenation, blood pressure, temperature and pupils. Record GCS components (E/V/M), limb power, sensation, speech and lateralising signs; reassess frequently. Protect cervical spine when trauma is possible. In reduced consciousness, involve senior airway expertise early. Avoid hypoxia and hypotension, which worsen secondary brain injury. Obtain urgent CT when indicated; do not delay resuscitation.

2 · Raised intracranial pressure (ICP)

Causes include traumatic haematoma, intracranial bleeding, mass lesion, hydrocephalus, cerebral oedema and infection. Symptoms: worsening headache, vomiting, reduced alertness, papilloedema (often absent acutely), diplopia from VI nerve palsy. Cushing response (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing) is a late dangerous sign. Unequal or fixed pupils and deteriorating consciousness may indicate herniation. Immediate senior/neurosurgical review, head elevated about 30° if haemodynamically safe, neutral neck, analgesia, prevent hypoxia/hypotension, and urgent imaging. Hyperosmolar treatment is specialist-led; never perform lumbar puncture when mass effect/herniation risk is suspected.

3 · Traumatic extradural haematoma (EDH)

Often associated with temporal bone fracture and middle meningeal artery injury. May follow head injury with a lucid interval before deterioration, but this is not universal. CT classically shows a biconvex/lens-shaped extra-axial collection limited by sutures. Urgent neurosurgical assessment; large or symptomatic collections may require evacuation.

4 · Subdural haematoma (SDH)

Usually tearing of bridging veins; risk increases with older age, falls, alcohol misuse and anticoagulation. Acute SDH may cause rapid neurological deterioration; chronic SDH may cause weeks of headache, confusion, gait change or focal deficits. CT typically crescent-shaped, may cross sutures but is limited by dural reflections. Correct anticoagulant-associated coagulopathy urgently with specialist input; neurosurgical evacuation depends on severity.

5 · Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH)

Classically sudden thunderclap headache reaching maximal intensity within minutes, with neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, collapse or seizure. Non-contrast CT head urgently. NICE NG228: if CT performed within 6 hours of onset and reported by a radiologist as negative, do not routinely offer lumbar puncture; if CT is negative more than 6 hours after onset, consider LP after at least 12 hours from onset to assess xanthochromia. Follow specialist advice and exact clinical context. Confirmed aneurysmal SAH needs urgent neurosurgical/neurovascular care, aneurysm securing (coiling/clipping), and nimodipine where indicated.

6 · Intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH)

Sudden focal deficits, headache, vomiting or impaired consciousness; causes include hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, vascular lesions and anticoagulation. Urgent CT differentiates haemorrhage from ischaemic stroke. Activate stroke/neurosurgical pathways, manage blood pressure under protocol, reverse anticoagulation when indicated, monitor for hydrocephalus and expansion. Do not give thrombolysis for haemorrhagic stroke.

7 · Head injury and CT decisions

Use NICE NG232 head injury criteria for urgent CT and observation; consider reduced GCS, skull fracture signs, focal deficit, post-traumatic seizure, repeated vomiting, age, loss of consciousness/amnesia and anticoagulants. Do not rely on a normal initial examination if symptoms worsen. Basal skull fracture clues: Battle sign, raccoon eyes, haemotympanum, CSF leak. Avoid nasal instrumentation when skull base injury suspected unless specialist-directed.

8 · Hydrocephalus and shunt failure

CSF outflow obstruction or impaired absorption may cause headache, vomiting, papilloedema and deteriorating consciousness. Ventriculoperitoneal shunt malfunction/infection may present with headache, lethargy, fever, vomiting or altered behaviour. Urgent neuroimaging and neurosurgical review; definitive CSF diversion is specialist-led.

9 · Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC)

Suspect in known or possible cancer with new progressive back pain, nocturnal pain, radicular symptoms, weakness, gait disturbance, sensory change or bladder/bowel dysfunction. This is an oncological emergency. NICE NG234: immediate contact with MSCC coordinator for neurological signs, urgent whole-spine MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 hours for suspected MSCC; give dexamethasone 16 mg orally or equivalent parenterally as soon as possible in adults with neurological signs of MSCC, continue while awaiting surgery/radiotherapy per specialist plan. Immobilisation decisions depend on stability and symptoms; avoid unsafe mobilisation.

10 · Cauda equina syndrome

Red flags: new urinary retention or altered urinary sensation, saddle/perineal sensory loss, bilateral sciatica or leg weakness, bowel dysfunction and sexual dysfunction. Emergency MRI and spinal surgical assessment; do not wait for all classic features or use normal rectal tone to rule it out. Document bladder symptoms and focused neurological examination.

11 · Spinal trauma

Protect spinal alignment; assess motor/sensory function, reflexes and sacral function. High cervical injuries can compromise ventilation. CT is generally first-line for suspected significant bony injury; MRI if cord injury or ligamentous injury suspected. Seek trauma/spinal specialists and prevent hypotension and hypoxia.

12 · Consent, communication and escalation

Explain concerns clearly, avoid false reassurance, involve relatives according to consent/capacity, document serial GCS and neurology, and use SBAR for urgent handover. In a deteriorating patient, life-saving treatment may be required under emergency best-interests principles.

Rapid decision pathways

Deteriorating consciousness / suspected raised ICP

ABCDE + glucose → senior anaesthetic/neurosurgical help → protect airway, avoid hypoxia/hypotension → serial GCS/pupils → urgent CT → specialist ICP control and surgery when indicated. No LP if mass effect/herniation risk.

Head injury with deterioration

Immobilise C-spine if indicated → ABCDE → assess GCS and pupils → CT per NICE NG232 → discuss haemorrhage urgently with neurosurgery → correct coagulopathy with specialist advice.

Thunderclap headache

Suspect SAH → urgent non-contrast CT → if negative, interpret with onset-to-scan interval (≤6 h versus >6 h) and radiology expertise → consider LP ≥12 h after onset when appropriate → neurovascular referral for confirmed SAH.

New neurological deficit

Activate stroke pathway → glucose and urgent brain imaging → haemorrhage versus ischaemia → condition-specific blood pressure, anticoagulant reversal and neurosurgical/stroke management.

Cancer + back pain + neurological change

Suspect MSCC → immediate MSCC coordinator contact → urgent whole-spine MRI within 24 h → dexamethasone 16 mg promptly for neurological signs → urgent surgical/radiotherapy plan.

Saddle anaesthesia / new bladder dysfunction

Suspect cauda equina → urgent spinal referral and emergency MRI → do not delay for complete symptom triad or normal rectal tone.

Four original clinical cases

Case 1 — Deterioration after head trauma

A 22-year-old briefly loses consciousness after a cycling crash, seems well, then becomes drowsy with a dilated pupil. Reasoning: expanding intracranial haematoma, classically EDH, with herniation risk. Action: immediate ABCDE, senior airway/neurosurgical help, urgent CT, serial GCS/pupils; avoid hypoxia/hypotension.

Case 2 — Sudden worst-ever headache

A 46-year-old has a thunderclap headache beginning 9 hours ago and a negative non-contrast CT. Reasoning: SAH remains possible after a negative scan performed more than 6 hours after onset. Action: urgent specialist assessment; consider LP performed at least 12 hours after onset to assess xanthochromia according to NICE pathway.

Case 3 — Confusion after a minor fall

An 81-year-old taking anticoagulants develops progressive confusion and unsteady gait over two weeks after a fall. Reasoning: possible chronic subdural haematoma. Action: urgent CT and assessment; review anticoagulant reversal if bleeding confirmed and consult neurosurgery.

Case 4 — Cancer with leg weakness

A 63-year-old with metastatic lung cancer reports worsening thoracic back pain, leg weakness and new difficulty passing urine. Reasoning: suspected MSCC. Action: emergency MSCC escalation, urgent whole-spine MRI within 24 hours, dexamethasone 16 mg promptly for neurological signs, urgent definitive treatment discussion.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Suspected metastatic spinal cord compression

Station: A patient with known cancer attends with back pain and new leg weakness. You must assess, explain concerns and initiate emergency care.

  1. Introduce and assess stability: consent, pain, ABCDE, observations; escalate immediately if unstable.
  2. Focused history: cancer type/treatment, onset and progression of back pain, night pain, radicular pain, gait, leg weakness, numbness, saddle sensation, urinary retention/incontinence and bowel symptoms.
  3. Focused examination: lower-limb power, reflexes, sensation, gait only if safe, spinal tenderness, bladder assessment; document baseline neurological function.
  4. Explain urgency: “The symptoms may mean pressure on the spinal cord. We need urgent tests and specialist treatment to protect movement and bladder function.”
  5. Immediate actions: call senior and MSCC coordinator, arrange whole-spine MRI within 24 h, give dexamethasone 16 mg promptly for neurological signs per NICE, assess spinal stability before mobilisation, provide analgesia and supportive care.
  6. Definitive plan: spinal surgery and/or urgent radiotherapy depending on imaging, prognosis and specialist assessment.
  7. Communication: empathetic explanation, check understanding, involve preferred support person, document and hand over using SBAR.

Do not delay referral while waiting for imaging or assume the absence of bowel incontinence excludes cord compression.

Rapid revision — 12 essential facts

  1. ABCDE, glucose, GCS and pupils first in any neurosurgical emergency.
  2. Hypoxia and hypotension worsen secondary brain injury.
  3. Cushing response is a late sign of raised ICP.
  4. EDH is classically biconvex on CT; lucid interval is possible but not universal.
  5. SDH is classically crescentic on CT and common in older/anticoagulated patients.
  6. Thunderclap headache requires urgent assessment for SAH.
  7. Negative CT within 6 hours of SAH onset, reported by radiologist, usually does not need routine LP under NICE NG228.
  8. Negative CT more than 6 hours after onset: consider LP at least 12 hours after onset.
  9. ICH is not treated with thrombolysis.
  10. Do not perform LP when mass effect or herniation risk is suspected.
  11. Suspected MSCC with neurological signs: immediate escalation, MRI within 24 h and dexamethasone 16 mg promptly.
  12. Cauda equina red flags require emergency MRI and spinal review.
