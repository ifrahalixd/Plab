Eye Trauma & Chemical Injuries

Emergency: Chemical eye burns need immediate copious irrigation. Suspected open globe needs a rigid shield and urgent ophthalmology without pressure on the eye.

Initial approach

Assess ABCDE for major trauma, mechanism, time, vision in each eye, pain, photophobia, contact lens use and chemical identity.

PLAB application: Do not delay urgent treatment to complete a lengthy examination.

Visual acuity

Record each eye separately before intervention when feasible, including pinhole; document pupils and relative afferent pupillary defect.

PLAB application: For chemical burns, start irrigation immediately rather than waiting for acuity.

External examination

Inspect lids, orbit, conjunctiva and cornea gently; assess eye movements and diplopia if safe.

PLAB application: Do not apply pressure to a suspected ruptured globe.

Chemical eye injury

Alkali injuries may penetrate deeply; acids also cause significant damage. Both require immediate copious irrigation.

PLAB application: Irrigate immediately with saline or water if saline is unavailable.

Chemical irrigation

Remove contact lenses if possible, evert lids and clear retained particles when safe. Continue irrigation and check ocular surface pH until neutral and stable.

PLAB application: Analgesia and topical anaesthetic may facilitate irrigation under supervision.

Chemical injury referral

All significant chemical ocular injuries need urgent ophthalmology assessment after immediate irrigation.

PLAB application: Never postpone irrigation while arranging transfer.

Corneal abrasion

Causes pain, tearing, photophobia and foreign body sensation; fluorescein highlights an epithelial defect.

PLAB application: Assess contact lens use and exclude penetrating injury.

Corneal abrasion treatment

Offer analgesia and appropriate topical antibiotic prophylaxis according to local policy; advise no contact lenses until healed.

PLAB application: Do not send patients home with topical anaesthetic drops.

Corneal foreign body

Small superficial particles may cause localised pain and fluorescein uptake. Examine under lids for retained debris.

PLAB application: High-velocity metal injury can conceal intraocular foreign body.

Penetrating injury and open globe

Suspect with high-velocity trauma, irregular pupil, visible wound, shallow chamber or markedly reduced vision.

PLAB application: Place a rigid eye shield, avoid pressure or manipulation, keep nil by mouth and urgently refer.

Intraocular foreign body

Metal-on-metal or power-tool injuries are high risk even with a tiny entry wound.

PLAB application: Urgent ophthalmology; CT orbit is commonly used, and MRI is avoided if metallic foreign body possible.

Hyphaema

Blood in the anterior chamber after blunt trauma can raise intraocular pressure and threaten vision.

PLAB application: Protect the eye and obtain urgent ophthalmology review.

Orbital blowout fracture

Diplopia, restricted upward gaze, infraorbital numbness and enophthalmos can follow blunt trauma.

PLAB application: Urgently assess entrapment, especially in children; avoid nose blowing.

Retrobulbar haemorrhage

After trauma, severe pain, proptosis, tense orbit, reduced vision and RAPD suggest orbital compartment syndrome.

PLAB application: Immediate emergency escalation; decompression may be sight-saving.

Traumatic retinal detachment

Flashes, new floaters or a curtain/shadow over vision after trauma suggest retinal tear or detachment.

PLAB application: Urgent same-day ophthalmology assessment.

PLAB 2 OSCE

Explain each step, gain consent, ask about mechanism and tetanus status where relevant, assess acuity and red flags, and communicate a clear urgent plan.

PLAB application: Never delay emergency irrigation or referral for a full OSCE sequence.

PLAB 2 — Eye trauma consultation

Introduce yourself, obtain consent, assess mechanism and time, and check vision in each eye when feasible. Look for chemical exposure, penetrating trauma, high-velocity particles, hyphaema, orbital compartment syndrome and retinal symptoms. Start emergency care immediately and use SBAR for urgent referral.

Deep Dive

Why alkali is dangerous

Alkalis saponify cell membranes and may penetrate deeply, causing damage beyond the surface.

Open globe precautions

Pressure can worsen extrusion of intraocular contents; avoid tonometry, patching tightly or probing wounds.

Corneal fluorescein

Dye highlights epithelial defects but does not replace assessment for deeper injury.

Orbital compartment syndrome

Rising orbital pressure reduces retinal and optic nerve perfusion; time-critical decompression may be necessary.

Post-traumatic visual loss

Think retinal detachment, optic neuropathy, hyphaema, lens dislocation and intraocular foreign body.

PLAB prioritisation

Emergency interventions and escalation take precedence over a complete routine eye examination.
