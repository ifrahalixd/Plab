Meningitis, Encephalitis & CNS Infections

PLAB priority: Suspected bacterial meningitis or HSV encephalitis is time-critical. Do not delay appropriate treatment for imaging or lumbar puncture.

01. Why CNS infection is an emergency

Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges; encephalitis is inflammation of brain parenchyma. Bacterial meningitis, meningococcal sepsis and HSV encephalitis can deteriorate rapidly. Fever, headache, neck stiffness and altered mental status may not all be present.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency treatment for a complete classical triad.

02. Typical clinical presentations

Meningitis may cause fever, severe headache, photophobia, neck stiffness, vomiting, confusion or seizures. Meningococcal disease can cause a non-blanching rash, shock and limb pain. Encephalitis often features altered behaviour, memory problems, focal deficits, reduced consciousness or seizures.

PLAB application: A normal-looking rash-free patient can still have meningococcal disease.

03. Red flags and immediate triage

Assess ABCDE, vital signs, GCS, capillary glucose and sepsis physiology. Red flags include shock, rapidly deteriorating consciousness, new focal signs, recurrent seizures, purpuric rash and signs of raised intracranial pressure. Obtain urgent senior and critical-care support where indicated.

PLAB application: Treat suspected bacterial meningitis or encephalitis as time-critical.

04. Focused history and examination

Ask onset, headache, fever, rash, sick contacts, vaccination, travel, immune suppression, ear/sinus infections, recent neurosurgery and antibiotics. Examine skin, mental state, neck, cranial nerves, limbs, fundi when appropriate and for possible infection sources.

PLAB application: Kernig and Brudzinski signs have limited sensitivity; absence does not exclude meningitis.

05. Immediate management of suspected bacterial meningitis

Call emergency/senior teams, establish IV access, take blood cultures and blood tests promptly, and give empiric IV antibiotics according to age, allergy, pregnancy, risk factors and local guidance. NICE NG240 recommends antibiotics within 1 hour of hospital arrival when bacterial meningitis is suspected; investigations must not cause delay.

PLAB application: Do not wait for CT or lumbar puncture before antibiotics if that would delay treatment.

06. Empiric antibiotics and dexamethasone

In adults with suspected community-acquired bacterial meningitis, IV ceftriaxone is a common first-line choice under UK protocols; adjust for allergy and resistant pathogens. Add amoxicillin when Listeria risk is present (for example older age or immunocompromise) per local guidance. Give IV dexamethasone with or before the first antibiotic dose when indicated, without delaying antibiotics.

PLAB application: Use current hospital protocols; avoid memorising one regimen for every patient.

07. Lumbar puncture: when and when not

CSF analysis helps identify infection: opening pressure when appropriate, cell count/differential, protein, paired CSF/blood glucose, Gram stain/culture and pathogen PCR. Perform LP promptly when safe. Stabilise shock, airway compromise, uncontrolled seizures or other contraindications first; neuroimaging before LP is reserved for specific concerning findings, not routine.

PLAB application: CT is not a routine prerequisite to LP and a normal CT does not guarantee LP safety.

08. CSF patterns

Typical bacterial meningitis: neutrophilic pleocytosis, high protein and low CSF:serum glucose ratio. Viral meningitis often shows lymphocytic pleocytosis, modest protein elevation and normal glucose. TB/fungal meningitis may show lymphocytes, high protein and low glucose. Early or partially treated disease can be atypical.

PLAB application: Interpret CSF alongside timing, clinical features and microbiology.

09. Viral meningitis

Enteroviruses are frequent causes of viral meningitis. Patients are often less systemically unwell than with bacterial infection, but appearance alone cannot safely distinguish causes. Give supportive care and investigate for dangerous alternatives; targeted antiviral therapy depends on pathogen.

PLAB application: Do not label an undifferentiated severe headache as viral meningitis without assessment.

10. HSV encephalitis

HSV encephalitis may present with fever, altered personality, confusion, focal temporal-lobe symptoms, aphasia or seizures. Arrange urgent IV aciclovir when suspected, plus MRI brain, EEG where indicated and CSF HSV PCR once LP is safe. An early negative PCR may require reassessment/repeat testing if suspicion remains high.

PLAB application: Start aciclovir promptly; do not wait for confirmatory PCR.

11. Meningococcal disease and public health

Neisseria meningitidis can cause meningitis, septicaemia or both. Rapid deterioration, purpura and shock are major warning signs. Follow infection-prevention procedures and notify public health teams promptly. Close contacts may need antibiotic chemoprophylaxis, determined by public health guidance.

PLAB application: A non-blanching rash is an emergency, but its absence does not rule out disease.

12. TB meningitis and subacute CNS infection

TB meningitis may evolve over days to weeks with headache, fever, cranial nerve palsies and confusion. CSF commonly has high protein, low glucose and lymphocytes. Seek infectious-disease expertise for TB testing, multi-drug treatment and corticosteroids where appropriate. Consider fungal infection in immunocompromised patients.

PLAB application: A subacute course does not make meningitis benign.

13. Brain abscess and focal infection

Brain abscess may cause headache, fever, focal deficits, seizures or raised intracranial pressure. MRI or contrast-enhanced CT is important; LP may be dangerous and is generally avoided when abscess/mass effect is suspected. Urgent neurosurgical and infectious-disease input is needed.

PLAB application: Focal neurological signs plus fever should prompt consideration of an intracranial collection.

14. Complications and monitoring

Monitor consciousness, seizures, haemodynamics, electrolytes, fluid balance, renal function and signs of raised intracranial pressure. Complications include hearing loss, cognitive impairment, focal deficits, hydrocephalus and epilepsy. Arrange audiology and rehabilitation follow-up when appropriate.

PLAB application: Consider hearing assessment after bacterial meningitis.

15. Important differential diagnoses

Consider subarachnoid haemorrhage, migraine, intracranial mass, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, toxic-metabolic encephalopathy, autoimmune encephalitis and sepsis-related delirium. A thunderclap headache needs emergency evaluation for haemorrhage even without fever.

PLAB application: Do not anchor on infection when examination suggests an alternative emergency.

16. PLAB 2: explanation and safety-netting

Explain that infection around or within the brain can be serious and that rapid antibiotics or antivirals and tests may be necessary. Obtain consent for LP when feasible, discuss headache/back discomfort and rare complications, and explain why treatment may start before results. In the UK, call 999 for suspected meningitis with deterioration, rash, seizures or altered consciousness.

PLAB application: Demonstrate empathy while emphasising urgent hospital treatment and clear escalation.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why the classical triad is unreliable

The triad is a useful memory aid but not sufficiently sensitive to exclude bacterial meningitis. Older adults and immunocompromised people may present atypically.

How inflammation changes CSF

Bacterial inflammation recruits neutrophils and disrupts the blood–brain barrier, increasing protein and often reducing CSF glucose. Patterns overlap and can change after antibiotics.

LP versus imaging decisions

LP can establish a diagnosis rapidly, but clinical instability, signs suggesting mass effect and other contraindications must be addressed first. Routine CT before every LP delays diagnosis and therapy.

Why HSV targets temporal structures

HSV encephalitis often involves temporal and frontal networks, explaining behavioural changes, aphasia, memory disturbance and focal seizures. MRI may be more sensitive than early CT.

Meningococcal shock physiology

Invasive meningococcal infection can trigger systemic inflammation, endothelial injury, capillary leak and disseminated coagulation, causing purpura and rapidly progressive shock.

Aftercare and prevention

Bacterial meningitis may leave hearing, cognitive or neurological impairments. Vaccination, public-health contact management and structured follow-up reduce preventable harm.
