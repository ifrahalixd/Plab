Intestinal Obstruction, Volvulus & Strangulated Hernias

Surgery · Lesson 03 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Intestinal obstruction: diagnose, resuscitate, recognise ischaemia

Immediate danger: continuous severe pain, peritonism, shock, fever, tachycardia or organ dysfunction may indicate strangulation or bowel ischaemia. Resuscitate and contact the senior surgical team immediately; do not delay source control for routine investigations.

1 · Mechanical obstruction versus ileus

FeatureMechanical obstructionParalytic ileus
MechanismPhysical blockage: adhesions, hernia, tumour, volvulusReduced gut motility after surgery, sepsis, electrolyte disturbance or drugs
PainOften colicky initially; continuous pain is concerningUsually diffuse discomfort and distension
Bowel soundsMay be high-pitched early; not diagnosticOften reduced; not diagnostic
ImagingCT may show transition pointDiffuse bowel dilatation without a mechanical transition point

2 · Clinical presentation

Classical features: colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, distension and failure to pass flatus or stool. Proximal small bowel obstruction tends to produce early vomiting; distal obstruction often causes greater distension. Early stool passage does not rule out obstruction.

Ask about previous abdominal operations (adhesions), hernias, cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, constipation, medication and previous episodes. Examine hydration, observations, abdomen, scars and all hernial orifices; check for peritonitis. Consider rectal examination only when clinically indicated, with consent.

3 · Initial investigations and management

  1. ABCDE and repeat observations; obtain IV access, FBC, U&Es, CRP, glucose, venous blood gas/lactate, and group and save if surgery possible.
  2. Nil by mouth, appropriate IV crystalloid and electrolyte correction, analgesia and antiemetic; consider nasogastric decompression particularly with significant vomiting or distension.
  3. Urgent surgical assessment and contrast-enhanced CT abdomen/pelvis in many adults when safe, to identify site, cause, closed loop and possible ischaemia. Adapt imaging for pregnancy and renal risks.
  4. Use antibiotics if perforation, sepsis or bowel compromise is suspected according to local policy; antibiotics are not automatically needed in every uncomplicated obstruction.
  5. Adhesional small bowel obstruction without peritonitis or ischaemia may be managed initially non-operatively under surgical supervision; deterioration or failed management requires escalation.

4 · Volvulus

Sigmoid volvulus: often marked distension, constipation and pain, particularly in frail or institutionalised adults. If stable and no suspected ischaemia/perforation, specialist endoscopic detorsion/decompression is often first-line; recurrence is common and definitive surgery may be considered. Peritonitis, perforation or ischaemia requires emergency surgery.

Caecal volvulus: a surgical emergency usually managed operatively; endoscopic decompression is generally not recommended because of poor success and risk of delay.

5 · Hernias and strangulation

Incarcerated/irreducible hernia cannot be returned to the abdomen. Obstructed hernia causes bowel obstruction. Strangulated hernia has compromised blood supply and needs immediate surgical assessment. A tender irreducible lump with persistent pain, skin changes, vomiting, fever or peritonitis is a red flag. Do not attempt forceful reduction when strangulation is suspected.

Exam trap: Normal lactate or absent fever does not exclude early bowel ischaemia. A strangulated hernia can present without all four classical obstruction symptoms.

Decision pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Suspected obstruction

Vomiting + distension + colicky pain/obstipation → ABCDE, NBM, IV access/fluids, analgesia, bloods and surgical referral → CT when appropriate → assess for transition point, closed loop, ischaemia or perforation.

Stable without ischaemia: specialist-led non-operative management may be appropriate for selected adhesive SBO. Unstable, peritonitic or suspected strangulation: resuscitate and prepare for urgent surgery.

Pathway B — Volvulus

Sigmoid volvulus + no perforation/ischaemia → urgent specialist endoscopic decompression where appropriate → plan recurrence prevention. Sigmoid volvulus with peritonitis/ischaemia, or caecal volvulus → emergency surgical management.

Slide 1 — Small versus large bowel obstruction

Adhesions and hernias commonly cause SBO; colorectal cancer and volvulus are important causes of LBO. CT usually defines the cause and complications better than plain radiography.

Slide 2 — Ischaemia warning signs

Continuous worsening pain, tenderness/guarding, shock, tachycardia, fever, metabolic acidosis or concerning CT findings. Do not rely on any one sign or blood test.

Slide 3 — NG tube and fluids

Replace fluid losses and monitor urine output, electrolytes and clinical response. Nasogastric decompression is useful when vomiting or distension is significant and should not delay urgent surgery.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Adhesional SBO

A 61-year-old with previous laparotomy has colicky pain, bilious vomiting, distension and no flatus. Stable observations; CT shows a transition point without ischaemic signs. Plan: NBM, IV fluids/electrolyte correction, NG decompression if indicated, serial examination and senior surgical review; consider supervised conservative management.

Case 2 — Strangulated femoral hernia

A 78-year-old woman has a painful irreducible groin lump, vomiting, HR 122 and local erythema. Plan: treat as suspected strangulation, ABCDE, IV access, analgesia, bloods/crossmatch and immediate surgical escalation. Avoid forceful reduction.

Case 3 — Sigmoid volvulus

An 82-year-old with chronic constipation has marked distension and obstipation but no peritonism. Imaging suggests sigmoid volvulus. Plan: resuscitate, urgent surgical/endoscopic review; endoscopic detorsion may be suitable if no ischaemia or perforation; plan to address recurrence.

Case 4 — Caecal volvulus

A 46-year-old has acute pain, vomiting and a markedly dilated caecum on CT. Plan: urgent operative management rather than routine endoscopic decompression.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute bowel obstruction station

Brief: You are an FY2 in ED. A 66-year-old with prior abdominal surgery presents with vomiting, abdominal distension and no bowel motions. Take a focused history and explain your management.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, assess comfort and obtain consent.
  2. Assess ABCDE red flags, observations, pain and dehydration.
  3. Explore onset, colicky versus continuous pain, vomiting (including bilious/faeculent), distension, stool and flatus.
  4. Ask about surgery, hernias, cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, medications, comorbidities and allergies.
  5. Explain abdominal and hernia examinations with dignity and consent.
  6. Discuss blood tests, IV fluids, nil by mouth, analgesia, antiemetic and possible NG tube.
  7. Explain CT imaging and urgent surgical review; acknowledge uncertainty.
  8. Recognise worsening pain, fever, shock or rigidity and escalate immediately; use SBAR handover.

Communication: “Your symptoms suggest the bowel may be blocked. We need to give you fluids, relieve your symptoms and ask the surgical team to assess you urgently. Some blockages settle without an operation, but we must check for signs that surgery is needed.”

Rapid revision & UK references

  1. Four classical obstruction features: pain, vomiting, distension, obstipation.
  2. Prior laparotomy → adhesions are a common cause of small bowel obstruction.
  3. Persistent pain or peritonism → consider strangulation/ischaemia.
  4. Sigmoid volvulus without ischaemia/perforation → endoscopic decompression may be appropriate; caecal volvulus → usually surgery.
  5. Painful irreducible hernia with systemic features → urgent surgical review; never force reduction if strangulation suspected.
