Final surgery revision — prioritise, diagnose, act

PLAB decision rule: identify the immediate threat, start ABCDE/resuscitation, call the appropriate senior team early, and arrange definitive treatment without delaying it for unnecessary tests. This is a synthesis of earlier surgery topics, not a substitute for local emergency protocols.

1 · The surgical first five minutes

Assess airway and breathing, oxygenation, circulation and haemorrhage, neurological state, temperature and exposure. Record observations, pain score and urine output. Obtain IV access, appropriate blood tests, crossmatch if bleeding, ECG where indicated, analgesia, fluids or blood products tailored to physiology, and keep nil by mouth if urgent surgery is possible. Repeat assessment after every intervention. Give time-critical antibiotics for suspected sepsis and source control when indicated.

2 · Acute abdomen: pattern recognition

Peritonitis: rigid abdomen, guarding, systemic illness → urgent senior surgical assessment and resuscitation. Obstruction: colicky pain, vomiting, distension, obstipation; CT often clarifies cause in adults, but unstable patients need immediate escalation. Appendicitis: migratory periumbilical-to-right-iliac-fossa pain; pregnancy test where relevant, assess differential. Cholecystitis: RUQ pain, fever, Murphy sign; ultrasound. Cholangitis: fever, jaundice and RUQ pain may indicate infected biliary obstruction requiring antibiotics and urgent drainage. Pancreatitis: epigastric pain radiating to back, raised lipase; supportive care and severity assessment.

3 · Trauma and haemorrhage

Use a structured major-trauma approach, control external bleeding, protect cervical spine when indicated, assess for internal bleeding, activate major haemorrhage protocol when needed, and involve trauma/vascular/orthopaedic teams early. Unstable suspected ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm is a vascular emergency. Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis in a deteriorating patient and must not wait for imaging.

4 · Vascular and venous emergencies

Acute limb ischaemia: six Ps (pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia); urgent vascular input and anticoagulation when appropriate. DVT: use Wells score, D-dimer and ultrasound according to NICE pathway. PE: assess haemodynamic stability and follow urgent VTE pathway. Compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion and on passive stretch; pulses may remain present; urgent surgical decompression.

5 · Urology and genital emergencies

Infected obstructed kidney: sepsis + obstructing stone → antibiotics and urgent drainage. Acute urinary retention: bladder scan where helpful, catheterise unless urethral injury suspected; monitor renal function. Testicular torsion: sudden unilateral scrotal pain → immediate urological exploration pathway; do not delay for ultrasound when suspicion is high. Fournier gangrene: resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics and urgent debridement.

6 · Neurosurgical, eye and ENT red flags

Intracranial bleed: altered consciousness or focal signs after head injury → urgent CT under NICE criteria and neurosurgical escalation. Cauda equina: new bladder/bowel dysfunction, saddle sensory changes or bilateral neurological symptoms → emergency MRI pathway. MSCC: cancer with progressive back pain and neurological deficits → immediate escalation and urgent MRI. Acute angle closure: painful red eye, halos, nausea → emergency ophthalmology. Chemical eye injury: immediate copious irrigation. Airway compromise: stridor, drooling, deep neck infection → urgent airway-skilled help; avoid provoking airway obstruction.

7 · Paediatric and burn emergencies

Bilious vomiting in neonate: bowel obstruction/malrotation until proven otherwise; urgent paediatric surgery. Intussusception: episodic pain, pallor, vomiting; ultrasound and specialist reduction where appropriate. Pyloric stenosis: non-bilious projectile vomiting, hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis; correct dehydration/electrolytes before surgery. Burns: stop burning, cool thermal burn with cool running water for 20 minutes when feasible, avoid hypothermia, assess airway and depth/area, refer appropriately.

8 · Perioperative safety

Document consent and capacity, allergies, anticoagulants, comorbidities, VTE and infection risk. Use WHO surgical safety checklist, appropriate prophylaxis and postoperative monitoring. Postoperative tachycardia, hypotension, oliguria, fever or new hypoxia warrant prompt evaluation for bleeding, sepsis, VTE, myocardial injury or fluid disturbance. Apply NICE IV-fluid 5 Rs: resuscitation, routine maintenance, replacement, redistribution and reassessment.

9 · Oncology and communication

Suspected cancer needs symptom- and age-specific referral pathways, tissue diagnosis where appropriate, staging and MDT discussion. In PLAB 2, communicate uncertainty, avoid premature reassurance, explore patient concerns, use teach-back, obtain informed consent and safety-net with clear return precautions.

High-yield emergency algorithms

Unstable surgical patient

ABCDE → call senior/critical care help → IV access, monitoring, targeted bloods/crossmatch → haemorrhage/sepsis management → bedside reassessment → definitive source control or operation.

Acute abdomen

Pregnancy test if applicable → assess peritonitis/sepsis/obstruction → analgesia, fluids and NBM as appropriate → focused imaging when stable → urgent surgical plan. Never delay escalation for imaging in shock.

Time-critical limb and organ threats

Acute limb ischaemia → immediate vascular review. Torsion → urgent exploration. Septic obstructed kidney → antibiotics + urgent drainage. Compartment syndrome → urgent fasciotomy assessment. Chemical eye injury → irrigate immediately.

Neurological red flags

New focal deficit or reduced GCS after trauma → urgent neuroimaging pathway. Cauda equina → emergency MRI/spinal review. Cancer + new leg weakness → MSCC pathway and urgent MRI.

Postoperative deterioration

Check observations and ABCDE → consider haemorrhage, infection/sepsis, PE, cardiac causes, urinary obstruction and electrolyte disturbance → investigate and treat in parallel → escalate early.

Four integrated clinical cases

Case 1 — Postoperative collapse

A 68-year-old is tachycardic, hypotensive and confused six hours after major abdominal surgery, with increasing drain output. Reasoning: postoperative haemorrhage until proven otherwise. Actions: ABCDE, emergency senior/anaesthetic review, oxygen if indicated, two large-bore IV lines, FBC/coagulation/crossmatch, activate major haemorrhage pathway as appropriate, and urgent source control. Do not send an unstable patient for routine CT without a resuscitation plan.

Case 2 — Septic stone

A 52-year-old has flank pain, rigors, BP 86/52 and CT evidence of an obstructing ureteric stone. Reasoning: infected obstruction with septic shock. Actions: sepsis resuscitation, cultures without delaying IV antibiotics, immediate urology/interventional radiology discussion for nephrostomy or ureteric stent, monitor renal function and urine output. Antibiotics alone are insufficient source control.

Case 3 — Sudden cold foot

A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation develops sudden severe leg pain, pallor, numbness and weak movement. Reasoning: threatened limb from acute arterial occlusion. Actions: immediate vascular consultation, assess neurovascular status and viability, urgent anticoagulation if not contraindicated under local protocol, analgesia and definitive revascularisation planning.

Case 4 — Bilious newborn vomiting

A two-day-old develops green vomiting and abdominal distension. Reasoning: possible malrotation with volvulus; bowel viability is at risk. Actions: urgent paediatric surgical assessment, NBM, NG decompression, IV fluids/glucose and monitoring, with imaging directed by the specialist team. Do not attribute bilious emesis to simple reflux.

PLAB 2 OSCE — emergency postoperative deterioration

Station: You are the FY2 called to see a patient six hours after abdominal surgery who is dizzy, tachycardic and hypotensive. Explain your assessment and initial management to the examiner and communicate with the patient.

  1. Immediate safety: hand hygiene, confirm identity, call for urgent help, ABCDE and observations; assess airway, breathing, perfusion, consciousness and temperature.
  2. Focused history: bleeding/drain output, pain, dyspnoea, chest pain, fever, medications including anticoagulants, fluid balance and urine output.
  3. Focused examination: surgical wound, drains, abdomen, peripheral perfusion, chest, calves and catheter output.
  4. Investigations: bedside glucose, ECG, FBC, U&Es, clotting, blood gas/lactate and crossmatch; further imaging only when stable and clinically justified.
  5. Immediate treatment: oxygen if hypoxic, IV access, cautious haemodynamic resuscitation, haemorrhage pathway if indicated, senior surgical and anaesthetic review, prepare for urgent intervention.
  6. Communication: “Your blood pressure is low, and we need to find the cause quickly. I am bringing the senior team now while we support your circulation.”
  7. Close: handover using SBAR, document findings, repeat observations and response to treatment.

Examiner priorities: recognition of shock, escalation, resuscitation, likely postoperative bleeding, safe reassessment and clear communication.

Final 12-point surgery checklist

  1. ABCDE and early escalation precede detailed diagnosis in unstable patients.
  2. Peritonitis and shock require urgent surgical review and source control.
  3. Sepsis with an obstructed urinary system requires antibiotics and drainage.
  4. Suspected testicular torsion must not wait for routine imaging.
  5. Acute limb ischaemia: six Ps and immediate vascular assessment.
  6. Compartment syndrome may have palpable distal pulses.
  7. Sudden severe headache or reduced GCS may indicate intracranial haemorrhage.
  8. Cauda equina and metastatic cord compression are time-critical spinal emergencies.
  9. Bilious vomiting in a newborn is a surgical red flag.
  10. Postoperative hypotension demands assessment for haemorrhage and sepsis.
  11. Neutropenic sepsis requires urgent empiric antibiotics.
  12. Explain uncertainty, consent, safety-netting and escalation in every PLAB 2 station.
