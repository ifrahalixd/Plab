Neonatal Jaundice, Sepsis & Respiratory Distress

Emergency red flags: Jaundice in the first 24 hours, pale stools, apnoea, central cyanosis, seizures, poor perfusion or a baby who is difficult to wake require urgent neonatal assessment.

Neonatal triage and ABCDE

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure and temperature. Look for apnoea, cyanosis, grunting, severe recession, poor perfusion, lethargy, seizures and poor feeding. Escalate early to neonatal senior staff.

PLAB application: Unwell neonates can deteriorate quickly; stabilise before prolonged investigation.

Physiological versus pathological jaundice

Jaundice reflects bilirubin accumulation. Physiological jaundice typically appears after the first 24 hours; jaundice within 24 hours is pathological until assessed. Visual inspection cannot quantify bilirubin reliably.

PLAB application: Any jaundice in the first 24 hours requires urgent serum bilirubin measurement and medical review.

Unconjugated and conjugated bilirubin

Unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia may arise from increased haemolysis, reduced conjugation or increased enterohepatic circulation. Conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia suggests cholestasis or hepatobiliary disease and needs urgent evaluation.

PLAB application: Pale stools or dark urine suggest conjugated jaundice, including biliary atresia.

Jaundice risk factors

Prematurity, early jaundice, haemolytic disease, G6PD deficiency, significant bruising and feeding difficulties increase risk. Ask about family history, blood groups, feeding, weight loss and stool/urine colour.

PLAB application: Identify babies needing closer monitoring rather than relying on skin colour.

Measuring bilirubin

Use a validated transcutaneous bilirubinometer when appropriate for gestational age and age in hours. Serum bilirubin is required in babies under 24 hours and in other situations specified by NICE, including readings above device thresholds.

PLAB application: Interpret against age-in-hours and gestational-age-specific NICE treatment graphs.

Phototherapy and exchange transfusion

Phototherapy converts bilirubin into more readily eliminated forms. Start or intensify treatment using NICE thresholds. Exchange transfusion may be required for severe hyperbilirubinaemia or signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy, with specialist management.

PLAB application: Never use a single bilirubin number without the age-in-hours treatment chart.

Acute bilirubin encephalopathy

Severe unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia may cause poor feeding, lethargy, abnormal tone, high-pitched cry, arching or seizures; permanent injury is termed kernicterus.

PLAB application: Neurological signs with jaundice are an emergency.

Prolonged neonatal jaundice

Jaundice persisting beyond 14 days in term infants or 21 days in preterm infants needs assessment, including conjugated bilirubin and stool/urine colour. Breast-milk jaundice is a diagnosis made after considering other causes.

PLAB application: Pale chalky stools and dark urine require urgent assessment for biliary atresia.

Early- and late-onset neonatal infection

Early-onset infection is usually within 72 hours of birth; late-onset follows this. Risk factors include maternal infection, prolonged membrane rupture, prematurity and previous neonatal infection. Group B streptococcus and E. coli are important pathogens.

PLAB application: Normal temperature does not exclude neonatal sepsis.

Sepsis signs and initial investigations

Features include poor feeding, altered responsiveness, apnoea, respiratory distress, temperature instability, abnormal perfusion and seizures. Obtain blood culture before antibiotics if feasible without delay; consider CRP, glucose, blood gas and lumbar puncture when safe and indicated.

PLAB application: Do not delay antibiotics in a clinically unwell neonate.

Antibiotic management and reassessment

Follow NICE NG195 and local neonatal antimicrobial protocols. Early-onset suspected sepsis often receives IV benzylpenicillin plus gentamicin, adjusted for local policy, cultures, renal function and therapeutic monitoring. Reassess need for treatment using clinical course and results.

PLAB application: Drug doses and gentamicin intervals are neonatal-age and weight dependent: use local charts.

Neonatal meningitis

Suspect meningitis with sepsis, seizures, bulging fontanelle, irritability or altered consciousness. A normal fontanelle does not rule it out. Lumbar puncture is considered when clinically safe and should not delay urgent treatment.

PLAB application: Seizures or reduced responsiveness require urgent senior neonatal review.

Respiratory distress: recognition

Tachypnoea, nasal flaring, grunting, recession, hypoxia and apnoea indicate respiratory compromise. Monitor oxygen saturation, respiratory effort, temperature and perfusion; check glucose and consider chest X-ray and blood gas.

PLAB application: Central cyanosis, apnoea or exhaustion require immediate resuscitation.

TTN versus respiratory distress syndrome

Transient tachypnoea of the newborn results from delayed lung fluid clearance, often after caesarean delivery, and usually improves over 1–3 days. Respiratory distress syndrome arises from surfactant deficiency, particularly in preterm babies, and may need CPAP or surfactant.

PLAB application: Preterm baby with early grunting and increasing oxygen need suggests RDS.

Other respiratory emergencies

Consider meconium aspiration, pneumonia, pneumothorax, persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn and critical congenital heart disease. Sudden deterioration with asymmetric breath sounds suggests pneumothorax; differential pre/postductal saturations can support PPHN evaluation.

PLAB application: Do not assume all neonatal cyanosis is due to lung disease.

Family communication and discharge safety net

Explain the likely diagnosis and uncertainty, investigations, feeding support, monitoring and follow-up in plain language. Advise urgent assessment for poor feeding, difficulty breathing, fever or low temperature, worsening jaundice, pale stools, dark urine, seizures or unusual sleepiness.

PLAB application: Use teach-back and clear emergency return advice.

PLAB 2 — Parent consultation

Ask about onset of jaundice, feeding, wet nappies, stool colour, breathing, fever, birth history and risk factors. Explain uncertainty, obtain consent for tests, involve neonatal seniors early and safety-net clearly.

Deep Dive

Understanding NICE bilirubin charts

Interpret bilirubin by gestational age and exact age in hours; thresholds for phototherapy and exchange transfusion differ. Serum bilirubin is required for early jaundice and other situations defined in NICE CG98.

Haemolysis and bilirubin

ABO/Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency and bruising may raise unconjugated bilirubin. Consider blood group, DAT, blood count and film when indicated.

Conjugated jaundice and biliary atresia

Pale stools, dark urine and prolonged jaundice should prompt fractionated bilirubin and urgent paediatric review. Timely recognition of biliary atresia is important.

Sepsis and antibiotic stewardship

Early treatment saves lives, but review antibiotics against cultures, CRP trends and clinical status. Follow NICE NG195 and local neonatal microbiology protocols.

TTN versus RDS

TTN reflects delayed fluid clearance; RDS reflects surfactant deficiency. Both require monitoring and supportive respiratory care as indicated.

Neonatal respiratory emergencies

Consider pneumothorax, pneumonia, PPHN and congenital heart disease when hypoxia is severe, persistent or atypical.
