Kidney Stones: Renal Colic & Urolithiasis

PLAB focus: Recognise renal colic, choose imaging and analgesia, and urgently escalate infected obstruction.

01. Overview and epidemiology

Urolithiasis is formation of stones in kidney or urinary tract. Calcium oxalate stones are most common. Recurrence is frequent and prevention matters.

PLAB application: Consider stones in acute unilateral loin pain.

02. Renal colic presentation

Sudden severe colicky flank pain radiating to groin, restlessness, nausea, vomiting and possible haematuria. Pain may migrate as a ureteric stone moves.

PLAB application: Unlike peritonitis, patients with renal colic often cannot keep still.

03. Immediate red flags

Fever or rigors with obstruction, sepsis, anuria, solitary kidney, bilateral obstruction, AKI, uncontrolled pain or vomiting, and pregnancy require urgent specialist consideration.

PLAB application: Infected obstructed kidney needs emergency drainage.

04. History and examination

Ask onset, radiation, urinary symptoms, fever, prior stones, hydration, diet, drugs, renal history and pregnancy possibility. Check vitals, abdomen, costovertebral angle and hydration.

PLAB application: Do not overlook alternative life-threatening abdominal diagnoses.

05. Differential diagnoses

Pyelonephritis, appendicitis, abdominal aortic aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian torsion, biliary disease and musculoskeletal pain can mimic stones.

PLAB application: Consider AAA particularly in older patients with sudden back or abdominal pain.

06. Urinalysis and laboratory tests

Dipstick may show blood but absence of haematuria does not exclude a stone. Check renal function, FBC and inflammatory markers when indicated; urine culture if infection suspected; assess calcium and stone analysis for prevention.

PLAB application: A normal urine dipstick does not rule out renal colic.

07. Imaging in non-pregnant adults

NICE NG118 recommends urgent low-dose non-contrast CT within 24 hours for adults with suspected renal colic; avoid CT in pregnancy.

PLAB application: CT KUB identifies size, location and obstruction.

08. Imaging in pregnancy and children

Ultrasound is preferred first-line for pregnant patients and children/young people; discuss uncertain findings with specialists.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary ionising radiation.

09. Acute analgesia

NICE recommends an NSAID first-line by any route if appropriate, IV paracetamol if NSAIDs contraindicated or insufficient, and opioids if both are unsuitable or inadequate. Do not offer antispasmodics for renal colic.

PLAB application: Check renal function, pregnancy, bleeding risk and NSAID contraindications.

10. Hydration and supportive care

Treat dehydration and vomiting, but forced IV fluids do not hasten stone passage. Give antiemetics and reassess pain, renal function and ability to tolerate oral intake.

PLAB application: Do not prescribe excessive fluids to force passage during acute obstruction.

11. Spontaneous passage and follow-up

Smaller distal ureteric stones are more likely to pass. Arrange follow-up, safety-netting and confirmation of resolution where appropriate; assess obstruction and renal function.

PLAB application: Persistent pain or obstruction needs reassessment.

12. Medical expulsive therapy

NICE advises considering alpha blockers for distal ureteric stones under 10 mm, recognising local prescribing protocols and patient-specific risks.

PLAB application: Tamsulosin may be considered, not prescribed indiscriminately.

13. Procedures and urgent decompression

Ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy decompresses an infected obstructed system. Definitive stone treatment may use ureteroscopy, shockwave lithotripsy or percutaneous nephrolithotomy depending on stone factors.

PLAB application: Drain infection first; definitive stone removal usually follows stabilisation.

14. Stone composition and risk factors

Calcium oxalate/phosphate, uric acid, struvite and cystine stones differ in causes. Risks include low fluid intake, recurrent UTIs, hyperparathyroidism, gout and inherited cystinuria.

PLAB application: Struvite stones are associated with urease-producing infections.

15. Prevention of recurrence

NICE recommends adequate water intake, dietary salt reduction and normal dietary calcium rather than calcium restriction; individualise measures based on stone analysis and metabolic assessment.

PLAB application: Aim for sustained prevention rather than short-term restriction.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and safety-netting

Explain suspected stone, imaging, analgesia, hydration, follow-up and recurrence prevention. Advise emergency assessment for fever, rigors, anuria, worsening pain, persistent vomiting or systemic illness.

PLAB application: Use clear shared decisions and safety-net instructions.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Stone formation

Urinary supersaturation allows crystals to nucleate, grow and aggregate. Low urine volume increases risk.

Pain mechanism

Ureteric obstruction raises upstream pressure and causes smooth-muscle spasm and inflammation, producing severe colicky pain.

Stone composition

Calcium oxalate is most common; uric acid stones favour acidic urine; struvite is infection-related; cystine stones arise in cystinuria.

Imaging rationale

Non-contrast CT accurately detects most stones and alternative diagnoses; ultrasound avoids radiation but may miss ureteric stones.

Why infected obstruction is dangerous

An obstructed infected collecting system cannot drain; antibiotics alone may be inadequate without urgent source control.

PLAB 2 OSCE approach

Assess pain, urinary symptoms, infection, pregnancy and kidney risk; explain analgesia, imaging, follow-up and prevention empathetically.
