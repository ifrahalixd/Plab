Communication Ethics, Breaking Bad News & Shared Decision-Making

PLAB core: Communication is a clinical and ethical duty. Listen, establish what the patient knows and wants to know, explain information in accessible language, check understanding, and agree a plan that respects their values and choices.

1 · Person-centred consultation

  • Introduce yourself, confirm identity, privacy and preferred form of address.
  • Ask open questions and explore ideas, concerns, expectations, goals and priorities.
  • Use short, clear explanations; avoid unexplained jargon and assumptions.
  • Listen without interruption; acknowledge emotion and allow silence.
  • Check understanding with teach-back rather than asking only “Do you understand?”

2 · Sharing serious news: SPIKES

  1. Setting: private, quiet place, adequate time; ask about supporters.
  2. Perception: assess current understanding.
  3. Invitation: ask how much detail the patient wants now.
  4. Knowledge: warning shot, small chunks of accurate information, avoid false reassurance.
  5. Emotions: pause, listen, name and validate feelings.
  6. Strategy and summary: discuss options, support, follow-up and safety-netting.

3 · Shared decision-making

Explain the condition, reasonable treatment options (including no treatment), likely benefits and harms, uncertainties and risks important to this particular patient. Invite questions and give time and support where practicable. The patient may decline a recommended intervention if they have capacity.

4 · Language, disability and accessible care

Arrange an appropriate professional interpreter when language barriers affect consent or safety; do not rely on children to interpret sensitive clinical discussions. Provide reasonable communication adjustments, including accessible formats, hearing support and extra time.

5 · Confidentiality and family involvement

Ask whether the patient wants relatives involved and what information can be shared. A family request to conceal a diagnosis does not automatically override a patient's right to information. Respect a patient's preference not to receive detailed information while ensuring decisions remain informed and voluntary.

6 · Handling conflict and complaints

Stay calm, acknowledge concerns, assess immediate safety, avoid defensiveness, explain what is known, apologise where appropriate, seek senior help when needed, document and arrange follow-up.

Exam trap: “Breaking bad news” is not delivering a memorised speech. Adapt the pace, amount of information and next steps to the individual patient.

Practical communication pathways

A · A difficult diagnosis

  1. Check setting, identity, privacy and support preferences.
  2. Ask what the patient understands and how much they wish to know.
  3. Give a warning shot: “I’m afraid the results are more serious than we hoped.”
  4. Explain the finding plainly, in small steps, and pause.
  5. Recognise emotions; allow silence and questions.
  6. Discuss next steps, uncertainty, written information, follow-up and support.
  7. Document what was discussed and the patient's preferences.

B · Shared decision-making

  1. Confirm the clinical decision and assess capacity when there is a reason for concern.
  2. Explain all reasonable options, including no treatment.
  3. Discuss benefits, harms and material risks relevant to the patient.
  4. Explore priorities, beliefs, circumstances and concerns.
  5. Check understanding; offer time and communication support.
  6. Agree and record the decision; revisit it if circumstances change.

C · Communication barrier

Identify language, sensory, literacy or cognitive barriers → provide suitable adjustments/interpreter → speak to the patient directly → check understanding and document support used.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Cancer diagnosis

A biopsy confirms cancer. The patient asks, “Is it serious?” Best response: establish what they understand and want to know, give a clear warning shot and accurate information, respond empathetically, then discuss support and next steps.

Case 2 · Family asks you to conceal the truth

A patient's adult son asks you not to tell his mother about her diagnosis. Best response: explore his concerns, assess the patient's information preferences directly and respect her autonomy and confidentiality. Do not promise secrecy from the patient.

Case 3 · Limited English proficiency

A patient needs consent for a non-emergency procedure; their teenage child offers to translate. Best response: arrange an appropriate professional interpreter, provide translated/accessible information where possible and check understanding.

Case 4 · Patient declines treatment

An adult with capacity declines recommended surgery because of personal priorities. Best response: explore concerns, explain material risks and alternatives, check understanding, respect the refusal and document the discussion.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Breaking serious news

Station: You are an FY2 doctor. A 58-year-old patient attends for a scan result showing a likely lung malignancy. Explain the finding and plan. The diagnosis requires further specialist confirmation.

Suggested consultation

  1. Introduce yourself; confirm identity, privacy, comfort and whether the patient wants anyone present.
  2. Ask: “What have you been told about the scan so far?”
  3. Ask permission: “Would you like me to explain what it shows?”
  4. Warning shot: “I’m sorry, the scan has shown something concerning.”
  5. Explain accurately: “There is a growth in the lung that could be cancer. We need further tests to know for certain.”
  6. Pause. Acknowledge distress: “I can see this is frightening.”
  7. Invite questions; avoid unsupported prognosis or reassurance.
  8. Explain referral, investigations, who will contact them, support services and safety-netting.
  9. Ask the patient to explain their understanding; summarise and document.

Examiner checklist

✓ Empathy ✓ Patient preferences ✓ Clear language ✓ Honest uncertainty ✓ Avoids jargon ✓ Appropriate next steps ✓ Understanding ✓ Support ✓ Documentation

Do not state that cancer is confirmed when the scan is only suspicious.
