Equality, Diversity & Discrimination

Learning objectives: Provide equitable, respectful care; recognise direct and indirect discrimination; make reasonable adjustments; communicate through interpreters; respond to discriminatory behaviour; distinguish equality from identical treatment.

1. GMC professional standards

GMC Good medical practice (2024) requires doctors to treat patients fairly and with respect, avoid unfair discrimination, respect dignity, and work in partnership with patients. Doctors must not allow personal beliefs to affect patient care and must challenge discriminatory behaviour and support an inclusive workplace. The GMC MLA content map includes equality, diversity and inclusion among professional capabilities.

2. Equality Act 2010 — England, Scotland and Wales

Protected characteristics include age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation. Not every characteristic operates identically in every part of the Act or context. Northern Ireland has a different set of equality statutes; do not automatically apply the Equality Act 2010 there.

3. Recognising discrimination

ConceptClinical example
Direct discriminationRefusing to register a patient because of their ethnicity
Indirect discriminationA blanket policy that disadvantages a protected group without adequate justification
HarassmentUnwanted conduct related to a protected characteristic that violates dignity or creates a hostile environment
VictimisationPenalising a person for making or supporting an equality complaint
Failure to make reasonable adjustmentsNot providing a feasible communication adjustment for a disabled patient

4. Reasonable adjustments

Disability-related adjustments may include accessible appointment systems, longer consultations, quiet waiting areas, accessible formats, mobility access, or communication support. Ask the patient what helps; avoid assumptions. The legal duty is to make reasonable adjustments, not necessarily every requested adjustment.

5. Language, culture and belief

For significant clinical decisions, use a competent professional interpreter when needed; check understanding and confidentiality. Do not routinely rely on children or untrained relatives. Respect religious and cultural preferences while explaining clinically appropriate options. If a patient refuses treatment for religious reasons, assess decision-specific capacity and ensure informed, voluntary refusal; do not override a capacitous adult solely because you disagree.

6. Fair access and clinical prioritisation

Equal care does not always mean identical care. Adjust support to achieve meaningful access, and prioritise according to clinical need and relevant evidence rather than stereotypes. Ask about a patient's preferences for name, pronouns, privacy and support without making assumptions.

PLAB trap: “Treat everyone exactly the same” is not always equitable; reasonable adjustments and individualised communication can be essential.

Practical decision pathway: FAIR CARE

  1. Find the barrier: Ask what is preventing safe, respectful access or understanding.
  2. Assess urgent needs: Prioritise immediate clinical risk and safety.
  3. Identify adjustments: Offer accessible communication, suitable facilities, extra time or support as reasonable.
  4. Respect choice: Elicit preferences, beliefs and goals; avoid stereotypes.
  5. Communicate clearly: Use a qualified interpreter when needed; check understanding with teach-back.
  6. Act on discrimination: Challenge or escalate discriminatory conduct proportionately, following local policy.
  7. Record and review: Document relevant adjustments and the agreed plan without unnecessary sensitive details.

When a colleague makes a discriminatory remark

Protect the patient; challenge the remark safely and professionally; seek senior support or report through appropriate channels if needed. Do not ignore repeated conduct or publicly disclose confidential information.

When patient preferences conflict with clinical practice

Explore the reason, offer practical alternatives, explain limitations honestly, assess consent/capacity when relevant and escalate if patient safety or service provision is affected.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Deaf patient and consent

A Deaf patient who uses British Sign Language is booked for an elective procedure. Their relative offers to interpret. Best approach: arrange an appropriate qualified BSL interpreter, provide accessible information, allow questions and confirm valid consent.

Case 2 · Learning disability and appointments

A patient with a learning disability repeatedly misses rushed appointments. Best approach: ask about barriers, offer reasonable adjustments such as an easy-read explanation, longer appointment and agreed support.

Case 3 · Racist remark on the ward

A colleague makes a derogatory comment about a patient's ethnicity. Best approach: challenge professionally, protect the patient and escalate if necessary; document/report appropriately.

Case 4 · Religious refusal

An adult with capacity declines a recommended blood transfusion for religious reasons. Best approach: explore understanding and voluntariness, discuss alternatives and consequences, respect an informed refusal and document the discussion.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Accessible consultation

Station: A patient with hearing impairment has not understood their medication changes and feels ignored. Address the concern and agree a safe plan.

Suggested opening

“I'm sorry our explanation hasn't been accessible. I'd like to understand how you prefer us to communicate and make sure you have the information you need to decide about your treatment.”

Station structure

  1. Introduce yourself; check identity, privacy and preferred communication method.
  2. Listen without defensiveness; acknowledge the patient's experience.
  3. Ask which adjustments or interpreter support would help.
  4. Explain medication changes in accessible language, including benefits and risks.
  5. Use teach-back and invite questions; do not mistake language barriers for lack of capacity.
  6. Agree written/accessible instructions, follow-up and documentation.

Examiner checklist

Avoid shouting, speaking only to a companion, or assuming that the patient cannot decide for themselves.

High-yield examination traps

  • Do not confuse a language barrier with impaired mental capacity.
  • Do not automatically use a child to interpret sensitive medical discussions.
  • Do not assume equal treatment requires identical communication methods.
  • Do not allow personal beliefs to deny clinically appropriate care.
  • Do not ignore discriminatory behaviour that compromises dignity or safety.
