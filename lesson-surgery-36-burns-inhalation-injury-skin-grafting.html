Burns, Inhalation Injury & Skin Grafting

Surgery · Lesson 36 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Burns: recognise major injury and protect the airway

Emergency priorities: ABCDE, stop the burning process, high-flow oxygen if smoke inhalation or carbon monoxide exposure is suspected, early senior/anaesthetic/burns-team involvement for airway risk, and prompt transfer advice. Do not delay airway assessment for imaging or blood tests.

1 · Immediate first aid

Ensure scene safety; remove patient from heat, remove hot or contaminated clothing and jewellery unless adherent. Cool thermal burns with cool running water for 20 minutes as soon as possible, ideally within 3 hours; avoid ice and prevent hypothermia by keeping the rest of the patient warm. Cover with clean non-adherent dressing or cling film laid lengthways, not wrapped circumferentially. Do not apply butter, toothpaste or creams to fresh burns. Chemical burns require appropriate PPE, removal of contaminated clothing, brushing off dry powders before irrigation where appropriate, and copious water irrigation; electrical burns require power isolation before touching patient.

2 · Assess burn depth

Superficial epidermal: red, dry, painful, blanches, no blisters; not counted in TBSA. Superficial partial thickness: pink, moist, blistering, very painful, brisk capillary refill. Deep partial thickness: pale/mottled, reduced sensation and sluggish blanching; often needs specialist review/grafting. Full thickness: dry/leathery, white/brown/black, insensate centre, absent blanching; usually requires excision and grafting. Depth can evolve over 48–72 hours.

3 · Estimate size

Calculate total body surface area (TBSA) for partial- and full-thickness burns only. Adult rule of nines: head/neck 9%, each arm 9%, each leg 18%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, perineum 1%. Patient's palm including fingers ≈1% TBSA for small scattered burns. Use age-adjusted Lund–Browder chart for children.

4 · Inhalation injury and carbon monoxide

Suspect after enclosed-space fire, facial/neck burns, soot in mouth/sputum, hoarseness, stridor, cough or respiratory distress. Early anaesthetic assessment is essential because oedema may progress. Give 100% oxygen for suspected carbon monoxide exposure; standard pulse oximetry may look normal because it cannot reliably distinguish oxyhaemoglobin from carboxyhaemoglobin. Obtain blood gas with co-oximetry for carboxyhaemoglobin where available. Consider cyanide toxicity in severe smoke inhalation with profound lactic acidosis/shock; urgent toxicology/critical-care advice and antidote per protocol.

5 · Resuscitation and monitoring

ABCDE, IV access, analgesia, temperature, tetanus status, associated trauma and baseline bloods. Significant burns may need IV balanced crystalloid guided by burn service and response; Parkland is a starting estimate, not a rigid target: 4 mL × kg × %TBSA in first 24 h, half in first 8 h from time of injury and remainder over next 16 h. Local protocols may use different starting formulas. Titrate to perfusion, haemodynamics and urine output (often adult target ~0.5 mL/kg/h); avoid over-resuscitation and fluid creep. Do not use formula for minor burns or superficial erythema.

6 · When to refer to a burns service

Seek specialist advice for suspected inhalation injury; any full-thickness burn; deep dermal burn; significant TBSA; burns involving face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum or major joints; circumferential burns; chemical or electrical burns; children and vulnerable patients; or concerning comorbidity/trauma. Use current local UK burns network criteria, as thresholds differ by region and age.

7 · Circumferential burns and compartment risk

Deep circumferential limb burns can impair distal perfusion: monitor pain, colour, capillary refill, sensation, movement, pulses and Doppler as appropriate. Circumferential chest burns can restrict ventilation. Urgent burns surgery assessment for escharotomy if vascular or ventilatory compromise; do not delay for absent pulses, a late sign.

8 · Wound care, infection and healing

Assess and document size/depth, cleanse and dress using local burn guidance, provide adequate analgesia and review. Do not routinely prescribe prophylactic systemic antibiotics for uncomplicated burns; treat diagnosed infection. Partial-thickness burns may heal spontaneously; deep dermal/full-thickness injuries often need surgical excision and skin grafting. Early nutrition, physiotherapy, splinting and psychological support matter in major burns.

9 · Skin grafts and reconstructive care

Split-thickness skin graft (STSG) contains epidermis plus part of dermis, covers large areas, donor site re-epithelialises; can be meshed, but may contract and differ in texture. Full-thickness skin graft (FTSG) includes all dermis, usually better colour/texture and less secondary contraction, but donor site needs closure and graft demands a well-vascularised bed. Grafts require vascular ingrowth; infection, haematoma, shear and poor perfusion cause failure. Flaps bring their own blood supply and are needed when critical structures are exposed or graft bed unsuitable.

10 · Special populations and safety

Children have different body proportions, higher hypothermia risk and different fluid requirements. Consider non-accidental injury if burn pattern/history is inconsistent (e.g., sharply demarcated immersion injuries), while remaining non-judgemental and following safeguarding policy. Electrical injury can cause deep tissue injury and arrhythmia despite small skin wounds; assess ECG and systemic injury. Chemical burns require poison/chemical-specific guidance.

Burns emergency decision pathways

First 20 minutes

Scene safety → stop burning → cool thermal burn with cool running water for 20 minutes → remove jewellery/non-adherent clothing → prevent systemic hypothermia → cover and assess ABCDE.

Enclosed-space fire + hoarseness/soot

100% oxygen → immediate anaesthetic/critical-care review for evolving airway oedema → co-oximetry for CO → burns service discussion. Normal SpO₂ does not exclude CO poisoning.

Major burn fluid strategy

Estimate partial/full-thickness TBSA → start local-protocol crystalloid resuscitation when indicated → calculate from injury time → reassess perfusion and urine output frequently → adjust rate; avoid fluid overload.

Circumferential deep burn

Serial distal neurovascular checks or chest expansion assessment → immediate burns surgeon if compromise → escharotomy when indicated.

Graft selection

Large defect with healthy vascular bed → often STSG. Small cosmetically/functionally important area → consider FTSG. Exposed bone/tendon without suitable bed → consider flap reconstruction.

Child with suspicious burn

Stabilise and treat pain → document injury and history objectively → consult senior/safeguarding team → protect child; do not delay emergency burn care.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — House fire and hoarse voice

A 39-year-old rescued from an enclosed-space fire has facial burns, soot around the mouth and increasing hoarseness. SpO₂ reads 99%. Interpretation: evolving inhalation injury and possible carbon monoxide exposure despite apparently normal saturation. Action: 100% oxygen, immediate anaesthetic airway assessment, co-oximetry and burns/critical-care referral.

Case 2 — Hot-water scald

A 24-year-old spills boiling water on the forearm 10 minutes ago; skin is blistered, pink and painful. Action: cool running water for 20 minutes, remove jewellery, avoid ice, cover with non-adherent dressing, give analgesia, estimate partial-thickness area and arrange review/referral based on depth, location and size.

Case 3 — Tight circumferential burn

A patient has a deep circumferential forearm burn; fingers are cool with delayed capillary refill and increasing pain. Concern: compromised limb perfusion from constricting eschar. Action: urgent burns surgical assessment and possible escharotomy, with repeated neurovascular examination.

Case 4 — Major burn and resuscitation

A 70-kg adult has 20% TBSA partial/full-thickness burns after a fire 2 hours ago. Parkland starting estimate: 4 × 70 × 20 = 5,600 mL/24 h; 2,800 mL in the first 8 h after injury (6 h remain), and 2,800 mL over the next 16 h. Important: consult local burns protocol and titrate fluids to clinical response and urine output; do not blindly follow the calculated volume.

PLAB 2 OSCE — counselling after a scald burn

Station: A 30-year-old has a painful hot-water burn with blisters on the hand and asks what happens next.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, establish time/mechanism and first aid already given; check immediate safety and ABCDE red flags.
  2. Ask about pain, numbness, circumferential injury, function, tetanus status, other injuries, comorbidities and safeguarding concerns where relevant.
  3. Explain cooling with cool running water for 20 minutes (if still within an appropriate timeframe), removing rings, keeping the rest of the body warm and avoiding ice or home remedies.
  4. Assess size and depth, examine hand movement and circulation, provide analgesia and a clean non-adherent dressing.
  5. Explain that hand burns merit burns specialist advice because scarring can impair movement; discuss possible follow-up, dressings, therapy and grafting if deep.
  6. Advise against bursting blisters at home; follow local wound-care guidance.
  7. Safety-net urgently for worsening pain, numbness, pale/cold fingers, fever, spreading redness, foul discharge or reduced movement.
  8. Check understanding, offer written information and arrange follow-up/referral.

Exam pitfall: do not calculate Parkland fluids for a minor superficial scald; assess clinically and refer based on site/depth.

12 high-yield revision facts

  1. Cool thermal burns under cool running water for 20 minutes; avoid ice and hypothermia.
  2. Remove rings and non-adherent clothing early; cover with a clean non-adherent dressing.
  3. Superficial erythema is not included in TBSA for fluid calculations.
  4. Adult rule of nines: each arm 9%, each leg 18%, head/neck 9%, anterior and posterior trunk 18% each, perineum 1%.
  5. Use Lund–Browder charts for paediatric burn TBSA.
  6. Facial burns, soot, hoarseness or stridor after enclosed-space fire raise airway concern.
  7. Pulse oximetry can be misleadingly normal in carbon monoxide poisoning.
  8. Parkland 4 mL × kg × %TBSA is a starting estimate; time starts at injury, not hospital arrival.
  9. Adjust major-burn fluids to haemodynamics, perfusion and urine output; avoid fluid creep.
  10. Deep circumferential burns can require urgent escharotomy for perfusion/ventilation compromise.
  11. STSG covers larger wounds; FTSG contracts less but needs a vascular bed and donor closure.
  12. Routine systemic prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated for uncomplicated burns.
