Learning objectives

Acute aortic syndromes and aneurysms

Recognise dissection and ruptured aneurysm; use Stanford classification; interpret urgent imaging; understand AAA screening, surveillance and referral.

Emergency rule: Sudden severe chest, back or abdominal pain with collapse, neurological deficits or shock needs immediate emergency assessment. Do not use this educational page as a clinical protocol.

Aortic dissection: the emergency

A tear in the aortic intima allows blood to enter a false lumen. Sudden severe chest, back or abdominal pain is typical, but pain may be absent. Syncope, stroke, limb ischaemia, unequal pulses, a new aortic regurgitation murmur or shock are important clues.

Assessment and management: Use ABCDE, IV access, continuous monitoring, urgent senior/vascular or cardiothoracic input. CT angiography is usually the definitive test in a stable patient. Do not delay emergency escalation for a normal chest X-ray.

Stanford classification

Type A involves the ascending aorta, regardless of tear origin; type B does not involve the ascending aorta. This distinction guides management.

Assessment and management: Type A generally needs emergency cardiothoracic surgery. Uncomplicated type B is commonly managed with specialist blood-pressure and heart-rate control; complicated type B may need urgent endovascular or surgical repair.

Initial management of suspected dissection

Pain and sympathetic activation can increase aortic wall stress. Control heart rate and blood pressure under specialist supervision while arranging definitive imaging and treatment.

Assessment and management: IV beta blockade is often used first where appropriate, with a vasodilator added if needed after rate control. Targets depend on perfusion and specialist guidance. Avoid anticoagulation or thrombolysis until dissection has been considered when clinically suspected.

Thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA)

A thoracic aortic aneurysm is abnormal dilation of the thoracic aorta. Many are asymptomatic; large aneurysms may cause chest or back pain, hoarseness or compressive symptoms. Causes include degeneration, bicuspid aortic valve and inherited connective-tissue disease.

Assessment and management: Confirm size and anatomy with appropriate imaging, assess valve and family history, manage cardiovascular risk and arrange specialist surveillance. Repair decisions depend on diameter, growth, symptoms, site and genetic syndrome—not one universal cut-off.

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

An AAA is a permanent dilation of the abdominal aorta, conventionally 3.0 cm or more in diameter. Risk factors include age, male sex, smoking and family history. Most are asymptomatic.

Assessment and management: Ultrasound is the standard screening and surveillance test. UK NHS screening invites men in their 65th year; people with symptoms or risk factors may need assessment outside screening.

Ruptured AAA

A ruptured AAA may present with sudden abdominal, back or flank pain, collapse, hypotension or a pulsatile abdominal mass. The classic triad is frequently incomplete.

Assessment and management: Call emergency vascular services immediately. Resuscitate with a major-haemorrhage approach and avoid delaying surgery for nonessential tests. Stable enough patients may undergo urgent CT angiography; unstable patients require immediate senior decisions.

AAA size and referral

Surveillance and intervention depend on aneurysm size, symptoms and growth. A symptomatic aneurysm is urgent regardless of size.

Assessment and management: NICE NG156 recommends considering repair for AAA ≥5.5 cm, symptomatic AAA, or AAA ≥4.0 cm with growth >1 cm in one year; refer according to local vascular pathways. The UK screening programme uses size-based ultrasound surveillance.

Aortic rupture vs dissection

Dissection is a tear creating a false lumen; aneurysm is vessel dilation; rupture is full-thickness bleeding. They can coexist but are not synonyms.

Assessment and management: Use the presentation and imaging to distinguish them. Both acute dissection and ruptured aneurysm can cause catastrophic haemorrhage or malperfusion.

Inherited aortopathy

Marfan syndrome, Loeys–Dietz syndrome and vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome can increase aortic complications, often at younger ages.

Assessment and management: Ask about family history, syndromic features and prior aortic events. Refer for specialist genetic evaluation, family screening and tailored surveillance.

Examination and OSCE

Compare blood pressure and pulses when feasible; examine for aortic regurgitation, abdominal pulsatile mass, limb perfusion and neurological deficits. A normal examination does not exclude dissection.

Assessment and management: Explain urgent investigations clearly, avoid false reassurance and escalate immediately if there is sudden severe pain, collapse, neurological deficit or haemodynamic instability.

PLAB 2: focused OSCE approach

  1. Assess ABCDE and call for emergency help if unstable.
  2. Ask about abrupt onset pain, syncope, neurological or limb symptoms and vascular risk.
  3. Check vital signs, peripheral perfusion, pulses, neurological status and cardiac murmurs without delaying escalation.
  4. Explain why urgent CT angiography or bedside assessment and specialist transfer may be needed.
  5. Discuss red flags, follow-up and family assessment when inherited aortopathy is suspected.

Mechanisms and higher-order reasoning

Deep Dive

False lumen and malperfusion

Dissection creates two channels separated by an intimal flap. Branch arteries may be compromised by dynamic flap obstruction or extension of the dissection, causing stroke, renal injury, bowel ischaemia or limb ischaemia.

Why type A is more dangerous

Ascending aortic involvement can lead to cardiac tamponade, acute aortic regurgitation or coronary malperfusion, requiring immediate surgical evaluation.

Anti-impulse therapy

Reducing heart rate and blood pressure lowers the rate of pressure rise (dP/dt) and wall stress. Specialist protocols generally prioritise IV beta blockade before vasodilation when appropriate.

Aneurysm growth and rupture

Rupture risk increases with aneurysm size, growth and symptoms. Repair decisions balance rupture risk against procedural risk, anatomy, comorbidity and patient preferences.

Genetic risk

Heritable connective-tissue disorders may prompt earlier intervention and family screening than sporadic aneurysms. Refer for specialist genetic and aortic service input.

Distinguish the terms

Aneurysm: dilation. Dissection: wall-layer separation with false lumen. Rupture: bleeding through the vessel wall. These can coexist.
