Full Neurological Examination & Clinical Localisation

PLAB priority: A systematic neurological examination identifies lesion location. In emergencies, focused assessment and escalation take priority over completing the entire examination.

01. Purpose and structure

A complete neurological examination evaluates higher functions, cranial nerves, motor and sensory systems, coordination, gait and reflexes. Start with the presenting complaint and assess urgent problems before a lengthy examination.

PLAB application: Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent, wash hands, ask about pain, position safely and expose appropriately.

02. General inspection and vital signs

Observe posture, facial symmetry, speech, spontaneous movements, tremor, muscle wasting, assistive devices and walking. Check observations and conscious level where clinically indicated.

PLAB application: Fever with confusion, acute focal weakness or reduced consciousness changes the priority to emergency assessment.

03. Mental status and cognition

Assess alertness, orientation, attention, memory, language, executive function and visuospatial function as indicated. Use a validated cognitive tool when appropriate, interpreting results in context of education, language and sensory impairment.

PLAB application: Delirium causes acute fluctuating inattention; dementia is generally chronic and progressive.

04. Speech and cortical function

Distinguish dysarthria (articulation problem) from aphasia (language impairment). Test fluency, comprehension, naming and repetition; look for neglect, apraxia and visual field defects.

PLAB application: Dominant hemisphere cortical disease can cause aphasia; nondominant parietal disease can cause neglect.

05. Cranial nerves I–II

Smell is not routinely tested unless indicated. Test visual acuity, fields by confrontation, pupils and direct/consensual light responses; perform fundoscopy when appropriate.

PLAB application: Relative afferent pupillary defect suggests asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal disease.

06. Cranial nerves III, IV and VI

Inspect eyelids and pupil size; test eye movements in an H pattern, ask about diplopia, observe nystagmus. CN III palsy may cause ptosis, a down-and-out eye and pupillary involvement.

PLAB application: Acute painful third-nerve palsy, especially with a dilated pupil, requires urgent assessment for aneurysm.

07. Cranial nerves V–VIII

Test facial sensation in V1–V3 and muscles of mastication; assess facial movements (raise eyebrows, close eyes, smile). Test hearing and, when appropriate, Weber/Rinne; assess vestibular symptoms.

PLAB application: Forehead-sparing facial weakness can suggest a central lesion, but patterns are not perfectly reliable.

08. Cranial nerves IX–XII

Listen for dysphonia, assess palatal movement and cough when indicated, test shoulder shrug/head turn and tongue movement. Avoid gag reflex testing routinely unless specifically indicated.

PLAB application: Bulbar weakness with choking, aspiration or respiratory symptoms is urgent.

09. Upper-limb motor examination

Inspect for wasting, fasciculations, abnormal posture; assess tone, power across shoulder/elbow/wrist/finger movements using MRC 0–5, reflexes (biceps, supinator, triceps) and coordination.

PLAB application: Pronator drift may reveal subtle pyramidal weakness; compare sides.

10. Lower-limb motor examination

Assess bulk, tone, hip/knee/ankle/toe strength, knee and ankle jerks and plantar responses. Test heel–shin coordination and observe for foot drop or spasticity.

PLAB application: Extensor plantar response suggests corticospinal tract dysfunction in an adult.

11. Upper vs lower motor neurone

UMN signs include increased tone, brisk reflexes, clonus and extensor plantars; LMN signs include reduced tone/reflexes, wasting and fasciculations. Acute UMN lesions can initially be flaccid.

PLAB application: ALS may combine UMN and LMN signs; a root lesion produces segmental LMN findings.

12. Sensory examination

Test light touch, pinprick and vibration; consider joint position and temperature. Compare distal with proximal and right with left, map dermatomes and look for a sensory level. Check Romberg safely.

PLAB application: Glove-and-stocking loss suggests polyneuropathy; a sensory level suggests spinal cord pathology.

13. Cerebellar and gait examination

Assess finger–nose, heel–shin, rapid alternating movements, speech, nystagmus, stance, tandem gait and walking if safe. Romberg tests proprioceptive/vestibular dependence rather than cerebellar dysfunction specifically.

PLAB application: Wide-based gait and dysmetria suggest cerebellar disease; shuffling gait suggests parkinsonism.

14. Clinical localisation

Use the pattern: cortical signs (aphasia, neglect, seizures), brainstem crossed signs, spinal sensory level and sphincter symptoms, peripheral nerve distribution, root dermatomes or symmetric distal neuropathy.

PLAB application: Localise first, then build differential diagnoses and choose targeted investigations.

15. Emergency neurological red flags

Sudden focal deficits suggest stroke; fever with altered mental state suggests CNS infection; rapidly ascending weakness can indicate GBS; new bladder dysfunction with saddle numbness suggests cauda equina syndrome.

PLAB application: Escalate, use ABCDE and focused examination; never delay time-critical care to complete a full routine examination.

16. PLAB 2 OSCE presentation

Explain findings clearly: alert and oriented; cranial nerves intact; tone, power and reflexes by side; sensation; coordination and gait; then localisation and next steps. Thank patient, ensure comfort and offer to complete missing components.

PLAB application: State what you would assess additionally, including fundoscopy, cognition or gait when not performed, and summarise key positives and negatives.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Neuroanatomical localisation

Cortical signs include aphasia and neglect; brainstem lesions can cause crossed findings; spinal cord lesions may produce a sensory level and bilateral long tract abnormalities. Peripheral nerve lesions follow nerve territories, roots follow myotomes and dermatomes.

Corticospinal pathway

Motor fibres descend from cortex through internal capsule and brainstem, mostly crossing at the medullary pyramids. A unilateral supratentorial lesion typically causes contralateral weakness.

Sensory pathways

Dorsal column fibres carry vibration and position and cross in the medulla; spinothalamic pain/temperature fibres cross near spinal entry. Patterns can help identify lesion level.

Reflex physiology

A reflex arc includes sensory afferent, spinal synapse and motor efferent. LMN injury reduces the reflex; chronic UMN injury may disinhibit spinal reflexes. Reflex findings must be interpreted with the full examination.

Ataxia distinctions

Cerebellar ataxia persists with eyes open and closed; sensory ataxia worsens without visual input. Vestibular disease may cause vertigo and imbalance. Assess gait only when safe.

PLAB 2 communication

Ask permission before touching, give simple instructions, check comfort, maintain dignity and narrate the examination. Explain important findings without alarming the patient, and promptly escalate red flags.
