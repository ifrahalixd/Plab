Liver Disease, Liver Masses & Surgical Complications

Surgery · Lesson 13 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Liver disease, focal lesions and emergencies

Recognise immediately: haematemesis with cirrhosis, confusion with acute liver failure, fever with right-upper-quadrant pain, shock after trauma, or a rapidly deteriorating jaundiced patient. Start ABCDE, call senior help, and manage sepsis/bleeding promptly.

1 · Surgical anatomy and liver function

The liver receives portal venous and hepatic arterial blood; hepatic veins drain to the IVC. Assess synthetic function with INR and albumin (albumin is also influenced by other illnesses), bilirubin/excretion and hepatocellular injury with ALT/AST. ALP/GGT may indicate cholestasis. A normal ALT does not exclude advanced cirrhosis. Portal hypertension leads to varices, splenomegaly and ascites.

2 · Approach to jaundice and abnormal LFTs

History: onset, dark urine/pale stools, itching, pain, fever, alcohol, metabolic risk, drugs including paracetamol, viral hepatitis risk, travel, weight loss and prior gallstones. Examine for fever, encephalopathy, ascites, bruising, chronic liver disease and focal tenderness. Request FBC, LFTs, INR, U&E, glucose, CRP, hepatitis tests where indicated, and abdominal ultrasound for biliary obstruction or focal lesions. CT/MRI/MRCP depend on findings. Fever + jaundice + RUQ pain suggests cholangitis: emergency antibiotics and drainage assessment.

3 · Cirrhosis and portal hypertension

Causes include alcohol-related liver disease, MASLD/MASH, chronic viral hepatitis and autoimmune or cholestatic disease. Complications: variceal haemorrhage, ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Use Child–Pugh or MELD where appropriate for specialist prognostication; do not delay emergency treatment to calculate scores.

4 · Liver abscess and hydatid disease

Pyogenic liver abscess presents with fever, RUQ pain and raised inflammatory markers; ultrasound/CT identifies a collection. Obtain blood cultures, start IV antibiotics guided by local protocol and arrange image-guided drainage for suitable abscesses; address biliary source. Amoebic abscess is an important differential with relevant travel/exposure: treat with appropriate anti-amoebic agents and luminal therapy; drainage is selective. Hydatid cyst (echinococcosis) requires specialist imaging and treatment planning; avoid unplanned aspiration because of spillage/anaphylaxis risk.

5 · Benign and malignant liver masses

Simple cyst: often incidental, asymptomatic and no treatment. Haemangioma: common benign vascular lesion, usually observation once imaging is characteristic; avoid unnecessary biopsy. Focal nodular hyperplasia: generally benign with characteristic imaging and often no intervention. Hepatic adenoma: associated with oestrogen exposure and metabolic factors; risk of haemorrhage and malignant transformation in selected patients, needing hepatobiliary specialist review. HCC: often occurs in cirrhosis or chronic HBV; may be found through surveillance ultrasound ± AFP according to programme. Multiphasic contrast CT/MRI helps diagnosis and staging. Metastases: common, particularly colorectal; management may include resection/ablation or systemic therapy in selected cases. A liver lesion is not automatically cancer; imaging and context determine next steps.

6 · Liver trauma and postoperative complications

In blunt trauma, follow major trauma ABCDE with haemorrhage control. Haemodynamically unstable patients need immediate trauma/surgical assessment and may need urgent operative intervention; stable patients typically undergo contrast CT and may be managed non-operatively with monitoring or angioembolisation. After liver resection, monitor for haemorrhage, bile leak, infection and post-hepatectomy liver failure; escalate new tachycardia, fever, abdominal pain, jaundice or rising INR.

7 · Acute liver failure

Acute liver failure is acute severe hepatic injury with coagulopathy and encephalopathy in someone without pre-existing cirrhosis. Paracetamol overdose is a major UK cause. Check glucose, INR, blood gas/lactate, drug history and paracetamol level; give N-acetylcysteine promptly when indicated and refer urgently to a specialist liver/transplant centre. Hypoglycaemia, cerebral oedema and multiorgan failure are key dangers.

Clinical algorithms and revision slides

Pathway A — Jaundice

ABCDE if unwell → history and examination → FBC, LFTs, INR, U&E, glucose → ultrasound for obstruction/mass → if febrile/septic, antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage assessment → otherwise targeted hepatology/hepatobiliary investigation.

Pathway B — Cirrhotic GI bleed

Resuscitate, crossmatch and escalate → suspected variceal bleed: vasoactive drug + IV antibiotics → urgent endoscopy/banding → rescue TIPS if refractory, specialist-led.

Pathway C — Focal liver lesion

Review risk factors, previous cancer and imaging → characterise with appropriate contrast MRI/CT → benign characteristic lesion often observe; suspicious mass → hepatobiliary MDT → stage and choose treatment.

Pathway D — Liver trauma

ABCDE + bleeding assessment → unstable: urgent operative/trauma team → stable: contrast CT, monitored non-operative management ± angioembolisation.

High-yield contrasts

HCC: cirrhosis/HBV risk. Metastases: often multiple lesions in patient with known cancer. Abscess: fever, inflammatory response and focal lesion. Haemangioma: characteristic benign vascular imaging; avoid routine biopsy. Acute liver failure: encephalopathy + coagulopathy, urgent transplant-centre discussion.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Haematemesis with cirrhosis

A 57-year-old with known cirrhosis vomits fresh blood, is tachycardic and hypotensive. Answer: possible variceal haemorrhage. ABCDE, IV access, bloods/crossmatch, resuscitate, IV antibiotics, vasoactive therapy and urgent endoscopy with senior involvement.

Case 2 — Fever and liver collection

A 64-year-old has fever, RUQ pain and a 6-cm hepatic fluid collection on ultrasound. Answer: suspected pyogenic liver abscess. Cultures, IV antibiotics, urgent radiology/hepatobiliary discussion for drainage and source identification.

Case 3 — Mass in a cirrhotic liver

A 62-year-old with cirrhosis has a new liver nodule on surveillance ultrasound. Answer: investigate possible HCC with multiphasic contrast CT or MRI and specialist MDT; do not assume a diagnosis from ultrasound alone.

Case 4 — Trauma with hypotension

A 29-year-old after a road collision has RUQ tenderness, BP 78/48 and tachycardia. Answer: possible major hepatic haemorrhage. Activate major trauma/haemorrhage response, ABCDE and urgent operative team assessment; do not delay resuscitation for routine imaging.

PLAB 2 — New jaundice and liver mass

Station: A 60-year-old with chronic hepatitis B has a new lesion seen on ultrasound and is anxious about cancer. Explain the findings, take focused history and plan investigations.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and ask what the patient understands.
  2. Explore symptoms: pain, weight loss, anorexia, jaundice, itching, fevers, bleeding, confusion and abdominal swelling.
  3. Ask hepatitis history/treatment, alcohol, metabolic disease, family history, previous cancer, medications and travel.
  4. Screen red flags requiring emergency assessment: haematemesis, collapse, confusion, fever with jaundice.
  5. Explain that a liver lesion can be benign or malignant and ultrasound alone may not determine the cause.
  6. Discuss blood tests including liver function and clotting; arrange specialist multiphasic CT/MRI.
  7. Explain hepatology/hepatobiliary multidisciplinary review and possible next steps without promising a diagnosis.
  8. Use clear language, check understanding, acknowledge anxiety and invite questions.
  9. Provide safety-net advice and arrange follow-up of results.

Patient-friendly phrase: “A scan has shown an area in your liver that needs a closer look. Many liver findings are not cancer, but because of your liver history we should investigate promptly with a more detailed scan.”

Rapid revision and UK references

  1. INR helps assess liver synthetic dysfunction; transaminases alone do not measure liver function.
  2. Cirrhosis complications: varices, ascites, SBP, encephalopathy, HCC and kidney dysfunction.
  3. Suspected variceal bleeding: resuscitate, vasoactive therapy, IV antibiotics and urgent endoscopy.
  4. New/worsening ascites in admitted cirrhotic patients: consider diagnostic paracentesis promptly.
  5. Fever + RUQ pain + focal collection: think liver abscess; antibiotics and drainage assessment.
  6. Haemangiomas are usually observed after characteristic imaging; biopsy can be hazardous.
  7. Hepatic adenomas can bleed and need specialist risk assessment.
  8. HCC risk rises with cirrhosis and chronic HBV; multiphasic imaging is key.
  9. Unstable liver trauma: urgent haemorrhage control; stable patients may have CT and non-operative care.
  10. Acute liver failure with encephalopathy/coagulopathy needs immediate specialist liver-centre referral.
