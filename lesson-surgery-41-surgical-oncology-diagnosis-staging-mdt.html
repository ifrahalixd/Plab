Surgical oncology — diagnosis to survivorship

PLAB principle: recognise suspected cancer, use site-specific NICE NG12 referral criteria, obtain tissue diagnosis safely, stage accurately and discuss definitive care through a multidisciplinary team (MDT). Emergencies take priority over routine pathways.

1 · Cancer warning features and referral

Persistent unexplained lumps, progressive dysphagia, unexplained weight loss, rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, haematuria, suspicious skin lesions and persistent changes in bowel habit require targeted history, examination and investigation. Ask about smoking, alcohol, family history, previous cancer, occupational exposures, performance status and patient priorities. NICE NG12 provides site-, age- and symptom-specific urgent referral and testing criteria: do not assume every symptom has the same threshold. Safety-net a negative initial test if symptoms persist or worsen.

2 · Diagnostic testing and biopsy

Use site-specific imaging and endoscopy (e.g. colonoscopy, upper GI endoscopy, breast triple assessment, CT). Histology establishes tumour type and grade, and may identify biomarkers. Choose biopsy route with the specialist team to avoid compromising future surgery (especially suspected sarcoma). Explain biopsy benefits, bleeding/infection risks, and when results will be discussed. A normal tumour marker does not exclude cancer; markers are not universal screening tests.

3 · TNM staging and grade

T = primary tumour extent, N = regional nodes, M = distant metastases. Stage describes anatomical spread and informs prognosis and treatment; grade describes microscopic differentiation/aggressiveness. Clinical stage (cTNM) is based on pretreatment evaluation; pathological stage (pTNM) incorporates resection histology. Not every malignancy uses the same TNM framework. Imaging is selected by tumour type and risk; PET-CT is not automatically needed for every patient.

4 · MDT and treatment intent

Surgeons, oncologists, radiologists, pathologists, specialist nurses, palliative care and allied professionals agree an evidence-based plan. Curative treatment aims for durable disease eradication; palliative treatment prioritises symptoms, function and quality of life and can include surgery, radiotherapy or systemic treatment. Patient consent and preferences remain central; MDT advice is not a substitute for shared decision-making.

5 · Surgical margins and lymph nodes

R0 means no microscopic residual tumour at margins; R1 microscopic residual; R2 macroscopic residual disease. Margin goals vary by cancer and anatomy. Sentinel lymph-node biopsy may be appropriate in selected breast and melanoma pathways; full nodal dissection is not always necessary. Consider morbidity, lymphoedema and function.

6 · Neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy

Neoadjuvant treatment is given before definitive surgery (e.g. selected rectal/breast cancers) to downstage disease or improve resectability. Adjuvant treatment follows surgery to reduce recurrence risk. Systemic options include cytotoxic chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy according to histology, stage, biomarkers and fitness. Radiotherapy may be curative, adjunctive or palliative.

7 · Common tumour pathways

Colorectal: colonoscopy and biopsy, CT staging; rectal cancer may need pelvic MRI; surgery ± systemic/radiotherapy by MDT. Breast: clinical examination, imaging and core biopsy (triple assessment); surgery ± radiotherapy and systemic treatment based on receptor status. Upper GI: urgent evaluation of progressive dysphagia; endoscopy with biopsy and staging. Skin: melanoma requires appropriate excision and specialist staging; suspected sarcoma should be referred before unplanned excision.

8 · Surgical oncology emergencies

Malignant bowel obstruction: vomiting, distension, pain and obstipation; ABCDE, IV access, fluids/electrolytes, imaging and urgent surgical review; consider decompression and goals of care. Neutropenic sepsis: fever or illness after systemic anti-cancer therapy; urgent assessment and empiric antibiotics per local protocol, without waiting for neutrophil results if clinically suspected. MSCC: new neurological deficit/back pain in cancer needs immediate pathway escalation, MRI within 24 h and dexamethasone when indicated. Hypercalcaemia: dehydration, constipation, confusion; check corrected calcium, assess volume and treat with specialist advice.

9 · Perioperative optimisation

Assess frailty, comorbidity, nutrition, anaemia, thrombosis risk, functional capacity and prehabilitation needs. Discuss stoma possibility, fertility, body image, recovery, postoperative complications and realistic outcomes. Avoid unnecessary delays for emergencies; coordinate complex decisions with anaesthetics and oncology.

10 · Consent, communication and survivorship

Give information in understandable language, ask what the patient already knows and wants to know, check capacity and preferences, discuss alternatives including no treatment, and explain material risks and uncertainty. Support smoking cessation, rehabilitation, symptom control, psychosocial needs, follow-up and safety-netting for recurrence.

Clinical frameworks

Suspected cancer → definitive plan

Symptoms/signs → site-specific NICE NG12 testing/referral → imaging/endoscopy and biopsy → histology/biomarkers → stage (TNM where applicable) and fitness → MDT → shared decision and treatment → surveillance/support.

Curative versus palliative intent

Curative: complete resection and/or radical oncological treatment where feasible. Palliative: symptom relief, quality of life and patient goals; may include procedures and active anticancer therapy. Intent can change with disease course.

Stage versus grade

Stage = extent of spread (T/N/M); grade = histological appearance. R0/R1/R2 describe residual disease after surgery, not TNM stage.

Neoadjuvant versus adjuvant

Neoadjuvant → before surgery, may downstage. Adjuvant → after surgery, reduces recurrence risk. Neither is appropriate automatically for every cancer.

Cancer emergency triage

Unstable / suspected neutropenic sepsis / obstructed bowel / MSCC → ABCDE and immediate specialist escalation; do not route through ordinary outpatient referral alone.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Iron deficiency and altered bowel habit

A 67-year-old has unexplained iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss and altered bowel habit. Reasoning: possible colorectal cancer. Plan: assess stability and bleeding, arrange guideline-appropriate urgent diagnostic pathway, explain colonoscopy/biopsy, then stage and discuss at colorectal MDT if cancer confirmed. Do not use a normal tumour marker to reassure.

Case 2 — New breast lump

A 49-year-old reports a persistent unilateral breast lump. Reasoning: requires prompt breast assessment, not clinical palpation alone. Plan: urgent referral as indicated; triple assessment with examination, imaging and needle biopsy; discuss receptor results and surgical/systemic options with MDT and patient.

Case 3 — Vomiting and distension in known cancer

A 72-year-old with metastatic colorectal cancer develops colicky pain, abdominal distension, vomiting and no flatus. Reasoning: malignant bowel obstruction. Plan: ABCDE, resuscitate, monitor urine output, correct electrolytes, urgent imaging and surgical/palliative MDT discussion. Options depend on site, fitness, prognosis and preferences.

Case 4 — Fever after chemotherapy

A 58-year-old feels shivery with a temperature of 38.4°C eight days after chemotherapy. Reasoning: possible neutropenic sepsis, even before a neutrophil count is available. Plan: emergency sepsis assessment, cultures and blood tests without delaying empiric IV antibiotics under local policy; urgent oncology/acute care input.

PLAB 2 OSCE — explaining a suspected cancer pathway

Station: A patient has been referred urgently following unexplained rectal bleeding and asks, “Does this mean I have cancer?”

  1. Open: introduce yourself, confirm identity, ask what they understand and what worries them most.
  2. Acknowledge uncertainty: “These symptoms can have several causes. Cancer is one possibility we need to rule out; the referral does not mean you have a diagnosis.”
  3. Explain tests: relevant examination, blood tests and colon assessment; explain biopsy if an abnormal area is found.
  4. Explain next steps: results, possible staging scans and specialist MDT discussion if malignancy is confirmed.
  5. Shared decisions: discuss benefits, risks, alternatives and the patient's wishes; offer support person and written information.
  6. Safety-net: urgent assessment for severe bleeding, fainting, obstruction symptoms or rapid deterioration; provide clear contact and follow-up plan.
  7. Close: check understanding, invite questions and document the discussion.

Avoid giving a definitive diagnosis or promising a particular treatment before investigations and specialist review.

12 exam essentials

  1. NICE NG12 suspected-cancer referral criteria depend on symptom, age and cancer site.
  2. Histology is usually required to establish cancer type; obtain biopsy by an appropriate specialist route.
  3. TNM = tumour, regional nodes, metastases.
  4. Grade is not the same as stage.
  5. Clinical staging differs from pathological staging.
  6. R0 = microscopically clear margins; R1 = microscopic residual; R2 = macroscopic residual.
  7. Neoadjuvant treatment precedes surgery; adjuvant follows surgery.
  8. MDT planning and patient shared decision-making are both essential.
  9. Breast triple assessment combines clinical assessment, imaging and tissue sampling.
  10. Suspected malignant bowel obstruction needs urgent assessment and individualised management.
  11. Suspected neutropenic sepsis requires emergency assessment and prompt antibiotics.
  12. MSCC neurological signs require immediate escalation and urgent MRI.
