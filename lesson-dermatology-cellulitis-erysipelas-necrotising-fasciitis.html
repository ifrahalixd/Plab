Cellulitis, Erysipelas & Necrotising Fasciitis

PLAB priority: Recognise necrotising fasciitis and sepsis immediately; differentiate cellulitis from common mimics.

01. Overview and priorities

Cellulitis is an acute bacterial infection of dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Erysipelas is more superficial, often sharply demarcated and raised. Necrotising fasciitis is a rapidly progressive deep soft-tissue infection requiring immediate surgical source control.

PLAB application: Always identify sepsis and necrotising infection before deciding on routine oral antibiotics.

02. Causative organisms

Non-purulent cellulitis commonly involves beta-haemolytic streptococci; Staphylococcus aureus is important with purulence, penetrating trauma or abscess. Erysipelas is commonly streptococcal. Necrotising infection may be polymicrobial or due to group A streptococcus.

PLAB application: Do not assume every red leg is bacterial or MRSA.

03. Risk factors

Skin breaks, ulcers, eczema, tinea pedis, lymphoedema, venous insufficiency, obesity, diabetes and previous cellulitis increase risk. Ask about animal bites, water exposure, injection use, surgery and immunosuppression.

PLAB application: Treat portals of entry such as interdigital fungal infection.

04. Cellulitis presentation

Unilateral warmth, tenderness, swelling and spreading erythema are typical. Fever and malaise may occur. On darker skin, redness may be less conspicuous; compare warmth, swelling, pain and skin texture.

PLAB application: Bilateral red legs without systemic illness often suggest a mimic rather than bilateral cellulitis.

05. Erysipelas

Bright or dusky red, raised, sharply demarcated painful plaque with abrupt fever is classic. Face and lower limbs may be affected. Blistering can occur.

PLAB application: Erysipelas is treated using the cellulitis antibiotic pathway when uncomplicated.

06. Differential diagnosis

Consider venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, deep vein thrombosis, superficial thrombophlebitis, gout, contact dermatitis, insect reactions and lymphoedema. An abscess is fluctuant and may require drainage.

PLAB application: A unilateral swollen leg needs assessment for DVT where clinically indicated.

07. Necrotising fasciitis warning signs

Pain out of proportion, rapid progression, severe tenderness beyond visible margins, systemic toxicity, hypotension, confusion, skin discoloration, bullae, anaesthesia or crepitus are alarming. Early skin findings may be subtle.

PLAB application: Do not wait for crepitus, imaging or blood tests before urgent surgical review.

08. ABCDE and sepsis

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure; obtain observations and calculate an appropriate UK early-warning score. In suspected sepsis, escalate promptly, take cultures when feasible without delaying treatment, and initiate resuscitation and antibiotics per local sepsis pathway.

PLAB application: Shock, altered mental state or rapidly worsening infection needs emergency hospital care.

09. Investigations

Uncomplicated cellulitis is usually a clinical diagnosis and routine blood cultures or swabs of intact skin are not useful. In severe disease, consider FBC, renal function, CRP, lactate and blood cultures. Swab open wounds when indicated.

PLAB application: Normal early inflammatory markers do not rule out necrotising fasciitis.

10. Antibiotics for uncomplicated cellulitis

Follow NICE NG141: oral flucloxacillin is a usual first choice for adults with uncomplicated cellulitis/erysipelas when appropriate; check allergy, pregnancy, renal function, local resistance and site-specific guidance. Typical course is 5–7 days with review.

PLAB application: Periorbital, facial near nose/eyes, bite-associated or water-associated infection needs tailored management.

11. Penicillin allergy and special sites

NICE alternatives depend on allergy status and patient factors; clarithromycin or doxycycline may be options for some adults. Infection near eyes or nose requires a different regimen and specialist consideration.

PLAB application: Do not give a single antibiotic rule for all anatomical sites.

12. When to use IV antibiotics

Consider hospital assessment for severe infection, inability to take oral medication, systemic illness, immunocompromise, rapid progression or failure to improve. Review IV therapy at 48 hours and switch to oral when suitable.

PLAB application: Document severity, allergies and review plan.

13. Necrotising fasciitis management

Immediate emergency surgical assessment and debridement are essential; give broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering likely organisms according to local protocol, resuscitate and involve critical care and microbiology. Repeat surgery may be necessary.

PLAB application: Surgery is lifesaving; antibiotics alone are insufficient.

14. Monitoring and safety-netting

Mark the edge of erythema where useful, elevate affected limb, manage pain and hydration, and address underlying oedema. Skin changes can appear worse in first 48 hours despite treatment; reassess if rapidly spreading, severe pain, fever, systemic deterioration or no improvement after 2–3 days.

PLAB application: Explain precisely when and where to seek urgent review.

15. Recurrent cellulitis and prevention

Manage lymphoedema, venous disease, skin integrity and tinea pedis; consider compression when appropriate after acute illness. Repeated episodes warrant review of diagnosis and risk factors; antibiotic prophylaxis is specialist-led for selected cases.

PLAB application: Prevention is more than repeated antibiotics.

16. PLAB 2 communication and documentation

Explain suspected infection, why antibiotics or admission are needed, possible mimics and red flags. Ask about allergies and previous episodes. Document observations, examination, extent, treatment, review interval and shared decisions.

PLAB application: A calm safety-netting explanation is often the decisive OSCE skill.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why skin breaks matter

The epidermis provides physical and immune protection. Fissures from tinea pedis or eczema allow bacteria to enter, explaining why treating skin conditions can prevent recurrent cellulitis.

Superficial versus deep infection

Erysipelas predominantly affects upper dermis and superficial lymphatics, producing a defined raised edge. Cellulitis extends into deeper dermis and subcutis; boundaries are less clear.

Why necrotising infection can look mild

Infection spreads along fascia before the overlying skin shows dramatic changes. Microvascular thrombosis and tissue ischaemia drive severe pain and later anaesthesia; urgent surgery must not depend on a characteristic rash.

Why laboratory scores cannot rule it out

Inflammatory markers and scoring tools can be insensitive early. Diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion and surgical evaluation, with imaging only if it does not delay intervention.

Stewardship and treatment review

Uncomplicated cellulitis usually needs a relatively short course. Overly broad antibiotics increase adverse effects and resistance; severe or unusual infection needs broader empiric coverage, narrowed when results allow.

PLAB differential: DVT versus cellulitis

Both may cause unilateral swelling and pain. Cellulitis more often has skin warmth and inflammatory changes; DVT requires clinical probability assessment and testing per relevant guidance. Both can coexist.
