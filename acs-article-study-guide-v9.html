ACS · Clinical deep dive

Build the essential science behind clinical decisions: anatomy, physiology, pathology, pharmacology and relevant molecular mechanisms.

CORE SCIENCES

Foundations that explain the clinical picture

Physiology: coronary perfusion occurs mainly during diastole; oxygen supply depends on coronary flow and arterial oxygen content, while demand rises with heart rate, wall stress and contractility. This explains why tachycardia or hypotension can worsen ischaemia.

Biochemistry: oxygen deprivation reduces oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production. Anaerobic glycolysis and acidosis develop, impairing ion pumps and contractility; prolonged injury damages membranes and releases cardiac troponin.

Pathology: atherosclerosis involves endothelial dysfunction, lipid deposition, inflammation and fibrous-cap formation. Plaque rupture or erosion can trigger platelet activation and fibrin-rich thrombosis.

Pharmacology: aspirin inhibits platelet COX-1 and thromboxane A₂ synthesis; P2Y12 inhibitors block ADP-mediated platelet activation; anticoagulants reduce clot propagation. PCI restores coronary flow mechanically.

Genetics: inherited lipid disorders such as familial hypercholesterolaemia can accelerate premature coronary disease. Consider family history of early cardiovascular events and markedly elevated LDL cholesterol.

For future topics, the Deep Dive will cover genetics, embryology, biochemistry, microbiology, immunology or other core sciences only when they help explain the condition.

FOUNDATIONS

1 · Coronary anatomy and territories

Left anterior descending (LAD): anterior wall and interventricular septum; anterior infarcts often involve V1–V4. Left circumflex (LCx): lateral wall; changes may appear in I, aVL, V5–V6. Right coronary artery (RCA): commonly supplies inferior wall and AV node; inferior changes often in II, III, aVF. Dominance varies, so these are associations, not absolute rules.

Why it matters: inferior infarction with hypotension and clear lungs may suggest right ventricular involvement; consider right-sided ECG leads and avoid preload-reducing drugs such as nitrates until assessed.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

2 · From plaque to infarction

Rupture or erosion of a coronary atherosclerotic plaque exposes thrombogenic material. Platelets adhere and aggregate, and coagulation generates fibrin. An occlusive or near-occlusive thrombus reduces oxygen delivery. Myocytes first become ischaemic; prolonged severe ischaemia causes irreversible necrosis and troponin release.

Clinical link: antiplatelet agents reduce platelet-driven thrombosis; anticoagulants inhibit clot propagation; PCI restores flow mechanically. Not every troponin elevation is due to plaque rupture.

BEDSIDE ASSESSMENT

3 · Focused history and examination

  • History: onset and duration, pressure/tightness, radiation, associated dyspnoea/sweating/nausea, exertional or rest symptoms, previous CAD/PCI, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, renal disease, medication and bleeding history.
  • Examination: ABCDE, vital signs, perfusion, heart sounds, signs of heart failure, pulse asymmetry, and examination for alternative emergencies.
  • Immediate tests: serial 12-lead ECGs, high-sensitivity troponin per local validated pathway, FBC, renal function/electrolytes, glucose; other tests as indicated. Do not delay reperfusion for blood results in clear STEMI.

TROPONIN

5 · Injury versus infarction

A troponin above the assay-specific 99th percentile indicates myocardial injury. A rise/fall indicates an acute process. Myocardial infarction requires acute injury plus evidence of ischaemia. Alternative causes include myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, tachyarrhythmia, sepsis and renal-related chronic elevation.

Why repeat? An early sample may precede the detectable rise. Follow your hospital's assay-specific 0/1-hour, 0/2-hour or other validated pathway rather than assuming one universal interval.

DIFFERENTIALS

7 · Do not miss these alternatives

Aortic dissection: sudden severe tearing pain, pulse/BP differences or neurological signs. Pulmonary embolism: pleuritic pain, tachycardia, hypoxaemia, risk factors. Tension pneumothorax: severe respiratory distress, unilateral reduced air entry, shock. Pericarditis: positional pleuritic pain and diffuse ECG changes. Other: myocarditis, pneumonia, reflux and musculoskeletal pain.

Reasoning: premature antithrombotic treatment in an undetected dissection may be dangerous; escalate uncertain high-risk presentations.

COMPLICATIONS

8 · Early and late complications

  • Electrical: VT/VF, bradyarrhythmia, AV block; monitor and use resuscitation pathways if unstable.
  • Pump failure: pulmonary oedema and cardiogenic shock; urgent senior/cardiology/critical-care review.
  • Mechanical: papillary muscle rupture causing acute severe mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal rupture, free-wall rupture/tamponade.
  • Inflammatory/thrombotic: pericarditis, LV thrombus and embolism.

Clue: sudden new murmur or shock days after MI requires emergency echocardiography and escalation.

PLAB 2

9 · Explain ACS to a patient

“Your symptoms and heart tests suggest that the blood supply to part of your heart may be reduced. We need to monitor your heart, repeat some tests and involve the heart team urgently. If an artery is blocked, we may need a procedure to reopen it. I'll explain each step and check your questions.”

Assess understanding, acknowledge concerns, explain risks and benefits without false certainty, and use a clear safety-net and handover.

Worked clinical cases

Case A · ST elevation

A 56-year-old has 45 minutes of crushing chest pain, diaphoresis and ST elevation in II, III and aVF. BP 88/60, lungs clear.

Reveal reasoning and actions

Suspect inferior STEMI with possible right ventricular involvement. ABCDE, cardiac monitoring, urgent reperfusion-team activation, aspirin if eligible, assess right-sided leads, avoid reflex GTN in hypotension. Do not await troponin before reperfusion decision.

Case B · Troponin rise

A 72-year-old with diabetes has breathlessness and intermittent chest pressure. ECG shows ST depression. Serial hs-troponin rises.

Reveal reasoning and actions

Acute myocardial injury with ischaemic evidence suggests NSTEMI. Monitor, give aspirin if appropriate, evaluate bleeding risk and anticoagulation, use GRACE risk assessment, and obtain urgent cardiology input for invasive timing.

Case C · Normal ECG

A 61-year-old has ongoing chest tightness and sweating. Initial ECG and first early troponin are normal.

Reveal reasoning and actions

Do not rule out ACS. Continue observation, repeat ECG, follow the validated serial hs-troponin pathway, assess other emergencies and escalate if symptoms or observations deteriorate.

Case D · Deterioration

Three days after MI, a patient becomes acutely breathless, hypotensive and develops a new loud systolic murmur.

Reveal reasoning and actions

Suspect a mechanical complication such as acute mitral regurgitation or ventricular septal rupture. Emergency ABCDE, senior/cardiology/critical-care escalation and urgent echocardiography.

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a time-critical cause of chest pain. This guide starts with the clinical decisions you need to make, then explains the anatomy and physiology behind them.

In 60 seconds: the essentials

1. What is ACS?

ACS describes acute reduction in blood flow to heart muscle, most often due to rupture or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque and formation of a clot inside a coronary artery. Myocardial ischaemia occurs when oxygen supply cannot meet demand. Prolonged severe ischaemia may cause irreversible myocardial injury (infarction).

ConditionWhat is happening?Typical findings
STEMIAcute coronary occlusion or equivalent high-risk pattern causing transmural injury in a coronary territoryCompatible symptoms and persistent ST elevation or recognised occlusion pattern; urgent reperfusion
NSTEMIAcute myocardial infarction without persistent diagnostic ST elevationRise/fall of troponin with evidence of ischaemia; ECG may show ST depression, T-wave changes or be normal
Unstable anginaAcute ischaemia without detectable myocardial necrosisNew, worsening or rest angina; no diagnostic troponin rise/fall indicating infarction
Clinical reasoning: An occluded coronary artery can occasionally present without classic ST elevation. If symptoms and ECG suggest ongoing coronary occlusion, seek immediate senior/cardiology input rather than relying on a simple STEMI/NSTEMI label.

2. Understand the mechanism

LDL cholesterol and inflammatory cells accumulate within an artery wall, creating an atherosclerotic plaque. When its surface ruptures or erodes, platelets adhere and the coagulation cascade produces fibrin. The resulting thrombus reduces coronary perfusion. Ischaemic muscle becomes electrically unstable, explaining why dangerous arrhythmias can occur early.

Coronary anatomy varies; ECG territories suggest rather than prove the culprit vessel.

3. How patients present

Typical ACS pain is pressure, tightness or heaviness in the chest, often lasting more than 15 minutes. It may radiate to the arms, jaw, neck, back or epigastrium. Sweating, nausea, vomiting, breathlessness, palpitations or syncope increase concern.

Older adults, people with diabetes and some women may present with breathlessness, fatigue, nausea or other symptoms without prominent central chest pain. Assess the entire presentation rather than using a stereotyped description.

Focused history

Ask about onset and duration, location and radiation, character, associated symptoms, exertion or rest, previous episodes, cardiovascular risk factors, prior MI/PCI, medications (including anticoagulants and phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors), allergies and bleeding history.

Do not miss dangerous alternatives: aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, oesophageal rupture and pericarditis. Sudden tearing pain with pulse or neurological differences particularly raises concern for dissection; antithrombotic treatment may be dangerous if that diagnosis is likely.

4. The first minutes: what to do

1 · ABCDE and call for help.

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Record observations, establish IV access, monitor rhythm and treat immediately life-threatening instability.

↓
2 · Obtain a 12-lead ECG promptly.

Do not delay emergency transfer to obtain an ECG. Repeat the ECG if symptoms persist or evolve; consider posterior/right-sided leads when indicated.

↓
3 · Blood tests and targeted investigations.

Use a validated high-sensitivity troponin pathway, FBC, U&Es/renal function, glucose and other tests guided by presentation. Consider echocardiography when complications or alternative diagnoses are suspected.

↓
4 · Begin appropriate treatment and escalate.

Give a single aspirin 300 mg loading dose unless clearly allergic, consider appropriate analgesia and nitrates after checking haemodynamics and contraindications. Oxygen is for hypoxaemia, not routine use in normoxia. Choose the reperfusion or NSTE-ACS pathway with the emergency/cardiology team.

5. ECG interpretation without memorising blindly

Read the ECG systematically: rate and rhythm, intervals, axis, QRS, ST segments, T waves and comparison with previous tracings. In suspected ACS, search for regional changes in anatomically related leads and reciprocal changes.

PatternWhat it suggestsWhat to do
Persistent regional ST elevationSTEMI or other acute coronary occlusion patternImmediate senior review and reperfusion activation
ST depression / dynamic T inversionPossible subendocardial ischaemia / NSTE-ACSSerial ECGs, troponins and risk assessment
ST depression V1–V3 with tall R wavesPossible posterior infarctionConsider V7–V9 and urgent specialist review
Normal ECGDoes not rule out ACSContinue clinical assessment and appropriate serial testing
Why do we look for reciprocal changes?

In some infarctions, leads facing away from the injured territory show ST depression as a mirror-image change. This can strengthen suspicion but is not required for diagnosis.

Does a new left bundle branch block automatically mean STEMI?

No. LBBB complicates ECG interpretation, but the label alone does not establish acute coronary occlusion. Use the clinical picture, comparison ECGs and urgent expert interpretation, including validated ECG criteria where appropriate.

6. Troponin: injury is not the same as infarction

Cardiac troponin is released when myocardial cells are injured. A value above the assay-specific 99th percentile indicates myocardial injury. A rise and/or fall suggests an acute process, but diagnosing MI also requires evidence of myocardial ischaemia.

Troponin can rise in sepsis, renal dysfunction, pulmonary embolism, heart failure, tachyarrhythmia and other conditions. Very early blood sampling may be nondiagnostic; follow the local validated high-sensitivity troponin protocol.

PLAB distinction: Type 1 MI is generally due to acute atherothrombosis. Type 2 MI results from an oxygen supply–demand mismatch with ischaemia (for example, severe anaemia or tachyarrhythmia). Not every raised troponin is an MI.

7. NICE-based management pathways

The key question after initial stabilisation is whether the patient needs immediate coronary reperfusion or a risk-stratified NSTE-ACS strategy. The interactive NICE Pathways tab in this lesson contains additional decision trees.

STEMI: restore coronary blood flow

Confirmed STEMI with symptoms within 12 hours

Activate the regional reperfusion service immediately. NICE recommends coronary angiography with primary PCI if it can be delivered within 120 minutes of the time fibrinolysis could have been given.

↓
If timely primary PCI is not achievable

Consider fibrinolysis if the patient is eligible, alongside the appropriate antithrombotic regimen and specialist pathway. Assess bleeding risks and absolute contraindications.

↓
After fibrinolysis

Repeat ECG at 60–90 minutes. Persistent ST elevation suggesting failed reperfusion calls for immediate angiography and rescue PCI, not another dose of fibrinolysis.

Presentation after 12 hours is assessed differently: ongoing ischaemia or cardiogenic shock may still justify urgent angiography/PCI. Decisions are specialist-led.

NSTEMI / unstable angina: stratify risk

Use clinical stability, ECG, troponin, bleeding risk and a validated score such as GRACE. Clinically unstable patients need immediate angiography. For stable patients with intermediate or higher risk, NICE advises considering coronary angiography (with PCI if indicated) within 72 hours of first admission. Lower-risk patients may initially receive conservative management with further investigation if ischaemia recurs or is demonstrated.

Antiplatelet and anticoagulant selection depends on diagnosis, bleeding risk, renal function and the planned invasive strategy. NICE recommends fondaparinux for eligible NSTE-ACS patients not undergoing immediate angiography; the interventional team selects the appropriate periprocedural regimen.

Prescribing safety: GTN can worsen hypotension and is contraindicated with recent phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor use; use caution in suspected right ventricular infarction. Antithrombotic drugs can cause serious bleeding. Check the current BNF, local ACS protocol, allergies, kidney function and specialist instructions before prescribing.

Oxygen, analgesia and other supportive care

Oxygen is not routine when saturation is normal. NICE CG95 recommends supplemental oxygen when SpO₂ is below 94% (target 94–98%) in patients not at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, or a target of 88–92% in those at risk pending blood gases. Provide appropriate analgesia and reassess frequently.

9. After the acute event

Secondary prevention usually includes antiplatelet treatment according to the intervention and bleeding-risk plan, intensive lipid lowering, and consideration of an ACE inhibitor and beta blocker when indicated. Review left ventricular function, blood pressure, renal function, potassium, smoking, diabetes and rehabilitation needs.

Offer cardiac rehabilitation, lifestyle support and a clear discharge plan. The exact drug combination and duration are individualised; check NICE NG185, current BNF and the discharge cardiology plan.

10. PLAB and OSCE: how to think

Case 1 · Crushing chest pain and ST elevation — what is first?

ABCDE, immediate ECG interpretation, aspirin unless clearly allergic, urgent emergency/cardiology escalation and activation of the reperfusion pathway. Do not wait for a troponin result to decide whether a clear STEMI needs reperfusion.

Case 2 · Chest pain, normal first ECG and normal first troponin — discharge?

No automatic discharge. Consider timing from symptom onset, risk factors, differential diagnoses, repeat ECG and the local validated serial high-sensitivity troponin algorithm.

Case 3 · Elevated troponin during severe sepsis — NSTEMI?

Not necessarily. Establish whether the patient has myocardial injury, evidence of ischaemia and an acute rise/fall. Consider type 2 MI or non-ischaemic injury. Management targets the cause and involves specialist assessment when appropriate.

    Clinical study notes

    Definition and mechanism

    ACS is acute myocardial ischaemia, usually following atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion with thrombus formation. It includes STEMI, NSTEMI and unstable angina.

    Presentation

    Suspect ACS with prolonged chest pressure or discomfort, radiation to arms, back or jaw, diaphoresis, nausea, breathlessness, or haemodynamic instability. Some patients present without predominant chest pain. Do not use response to GTN to diagnose ACS.

    Immediate assessment

    • ABCDE, observations, IV access, cardiac monitoring and urgent clinical escalation.
    • 12-lead ECG promptly; repeat if symptoms persist or diagnosis is uncertain. A normal ECG does not exclude ACS.
    • High-sensitivity troponin I or T on arrival, with repeat testing according to validated local assay pathways; interpret in context.
    • Consider dangerous alternatives: aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax and other causes.

    Key diagnostic distinction

    STEMI: compatible presentation with persistent regional ST elevation or a STEMI equivalent requiring emergency reperfusion assessment. NSTEMI: myocardial infarction without diagnostic persistent ST elevation, supported by a rise/fall in troponin and evidence of ischaemia. Unstable angina: acute myocardial ischaemia without biomarker evidence of myocardial necrosis.

    Management principles (UK)

    • Suspected ACS needs emergency hospital assessment; obtain ECG without delaying transfer.
    • NICE CG95 recommends a single 300 mg aspirin loading dose as soon as possible unless clear aspirin allergy; check bleeding and other contraindications in practice.
    • Do not routinely administer oxygen: target 94–98% when SpO₂ is below 94% in patients not at risk of hypercapnic failure; target 88–92% for those at risk pending blood gas results.
    • Offer appropriate analgesia; GTN may relieve symptoms but is not diagnostic. Check blood pressure and contraindications before nitrates.
    • STEMI: activate urgent reperfusion pathway. NICE NG185 prefers primary PCI when eligible and timely; fibrinolysis may be considered according to the pathway when PCI cannot be delivered in time.
    • NSTEMI/unstable angina: antithrombotic treatment and GRACE-based risk assessment guide invasive strategy. NICE recommends fondaparinux in eligible patients not undergoing immediate angiography, subject to bleeding risk and contraindications.
    • P2Y12 agent selection, anticoagulation, statin, ACE inhibitor, beta-blocker and longer-term therapy depend on pathway and individual factors. Check current BNF for drug-specific doses, renal adjustment, contraindications and interactions.

    Coronary anatomy and ECG localisation

    The right coronary artery often supplies the inferior wall and may supply the right ventricle and AV node. The LAD supplies much of the anterior wall and septum; the circumflex supplies lateral myocardium. Coronary dominance varies. Inferior ST elevation is usually seen in II, III and aVF; anterior changes in V1–V4; lateral changes in I, aVL, V5–V6. Reciprocal ST depression can strengthen suspicion. A posterior infarct may present with ST depression in V1–V3; obtain posterior leads when clinically indicated. These patterns require interpretation in context and may be affected by conduction abnormalities.

    Focused history and examination

    • History: onset, provoking factors, duration, quality, radiation, severity, associated diaphoresis, nausea, syncope, dyspnoea, previous episodes and exertional symptoms.
    • Risk: previous coronary disease, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, family history and relevant medicines including anticoagulants and PDE5 inhibitors.
    • Examination: ABCDE, perfusion, heart rate and rhythm, blood pressure, oxygenation, heart failure signs, new murmurs and unequal pulses where dissection is a concern.

    Troponin: interpretation rather than memorisation

    High-sensitivity cardiac troponin detects myocardial injury. Acute injury is suggested by a rise/fall with at least one value above the assay-specific upper reference limit. Myocardial infarction additionally requires clinical evidence of acute ischaemia. Other causes of raised troponin include myocarditis, tachyarrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, renal disease and sepsis. Sampling intervals and rule-out thresholds are assay-specific; follow the locally validated pathway.

    Detailed STEMI decision-making

    Reperfusion is time-critical. NICE NG185 compares primary PCI with fibrinolysis using the 120-minute threshold measured from when fibrinolysis could have been given. For eligible patients within 12 hours, primary PCI is preferred when timely. If fibrinolysis is used, repeat the ECG at 60–90 minutes and arrange rescue PCI for failed reperfusion. Ongoing ischaemia or shock requires urgent specialist consideration even after 12 hours. Review bleeding contraindications before fibrinolysis.

    Detailed NSTEMI / unstable angina decision-making

    Evaluate clinical instability and use a formal risk tool such as GRACE to inform angiography timing. NICE recommends considering immediate angiography for clinical instability, and angiography within 72 hours for intermediate or higher risk when clinically appropriate. Fondaparinux is recommended for eligible patients not undergoing immediate angiography unless contraindicated or bleeding risk is high. Treatment choices depend on whether an invasive strategy is planned.

    Secondary prevention and discharge

    After MI, assess LV function and offer appropriate antiplatelet treatment, statin, ACE inhibitor and beta-blocker therapy, adjusted for individual risks and contraindications. Address smoking, exercise, diet, rehabilitation, adherence, driving/work questions and follow-up. Selected patients with LV systolic dysfunction and heart failure may need additional specialist-directed medication.

    Clinical scenarios to reason through

    Scenario A — STEMI: A patient has ongoing chest pain and new inferior ST elevation. Your first priority is emergency reperfusion assessment, not waiting for a troponin result.
    Scenario B — Normal initial ECG: A patient has convincing ischaemic symptoms but no initial ST change. Continue assessment with serial ECGs and assay-specific troponin testing; a normal tracing does not exclude ACS.
    Scenario C — Elevated troponin in sepsis: Troponin elevation indicates injury, but does not automatically diagnose type 1 MI. Look for ischaemic evidence and alternative mechanisms.
    PLAB exam traps: Normal ECG ≠ exclusion; normal first troponin may be too early; elevated troponin alone ≠ type 1 MI; no routine oxygen in normoxia; do not delay reperfusion for troponin in clear STEMI.

    Complications

    Arrhythmias, acute heart failure, cardiogenic shock, mechanical complications, pericarditis and recurrent ischaemia.

    2 · STEMI: reperfusion decision

    Confirmed STEMI → activate emergency reperfusion pathwayCheck time from symptom onset, ongoing ischaemia, cardiogenic shock, PCI availability and fibrinolysis contraindications.

    Presentation within 12 hours of symptom onset?

    3 · NSTEMI / unstable angina

    No persistent diagnostic ST elevationUse symptoms, serial ECGs, high-sensitivity troponin and clinical context to distinguish NSTEMI, unstable angina and non-ACS injury.
    ↓
    Assess risk and stabilityUse an established risk tool such as GRACE (predicted 6-month mortality) alongside bleeding risk, renal function, comorbidities and patient preferences.
    ↓
    Clinically unstableConsider immediate coronary angiography (for example ongoing or recurrent pain, haemodynamic or electrical instability) with urgent cardiology review.
    StableConsider angiography within 72 hours when intermediate or higher risk (NICE example: predicted 6-month mortality above 3%). Lower-risk patients may be managed conservatively with individualised assessment.
    ↓
    Antithrombotic decisionsAspirin; NICE recommends fondaparinux for eligible NSTEMI/UA patients not undergoing immediate coronary angiography, unless high bleeding risk or contraindications. P2Y12 selection and timing depend on coronary strategy and clinical circumstances.

    Exam pitfall: Raised troponin means myocardial injury; it does not by itself prove plaque-rupture type 1 MI. NSTEMI does not routinely receive fibrinolysis.

    4 · Secondary prevention after MI

    Before discharge and at follow-upReview symptoms, LV function, complications, risk factors, medication safety and a clear cardiac rehabilitation plan.
    ↓
    Core medication groupsAntiplatelet therapy (duration and combination depend on intervention and bleeding risk), high-intensity statin, ACE inhibitor and beta-blocker when appropriate. Consider an aldosterone antagonist in selected patients with LV systolic dysfunction and heart failure, following specialist guidance.
    ↓
    Long-term recoverySmoking cessation, physical activity and dietary support, cardiac rehabilitation, lipid and BP management, adherence review, return-to-work advice and safety-netting.

    Review contraindications, renal function, potassium, blood pressure, pregnancy potential and interactions before prescribing. Verify exact doses and durations against current NICE and BNF guidance.
