Colorectal Cancer & Bowel Screening

Emergency: Suspected bowel obstruction, perforation or major bleeding requires urgent hospital assessment.

Overview

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is malignancy arising in the colon or rectum, usually adenocarcinoma. It may develop from precursor polyps over years.

PLAB application: Recognise alarm symptoms and arrange investigation rather than treating presumed haemorrhoids alone.

Risk factors

Age, family history, hereditary syndromes (Lynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis), inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, smoking and alcohol increase risk.

PLAB application: Ask about first-degree relatives and age at diagnosis; refer high-risk families for specialist assessment.

Presenting symptoms

Persistent change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, abdominal pain, unexplained weight loss and fatigue. Right-sided lesions may cause occult blood loss; left-sided lesions may obstruct.

PLAB application: Symptoms can be subtle; normal examination does not exclude cancer.

Red flags and obstruction

Severe colicky pain, distension, vomiting and inability to pass stool or flatus suggest bowel obstruction. Peritonitis or shock suggests perforation or sepsis.

PLAB application: Emergency admission, ABCDE resuscitation and urgent surgical review.

Focused history

Ask onset, duration, stool consistency, bleeding colour, weight change, anaemia symptoms, abdominal pain, family history, IBD, medicines and screening participation.

PLAB application: Check patient ideas and concerns and assess urgency.

Examination

Record observations and assess pallor, cachexia, abdominal masses, distension, tenderness and lymphadenopathy. Perform digital rectal examination with consent and chaperone when indicated.

PLAB application: A normal rectal examination does not rule out proximal cancer.

Initial investigations

FBC and ferritin identify iron-deficiency anaemia; renal function and liver tests may inform assessment. FIT is used in symptomatic primary-care pathways according to current NICE criteria.

PLAB application: Do not use normal blood tests or a low FIT result to dismiss persistent concerning symptoms.

Symptomatic FIT and referral

NICE NG12 recommends quantitative faecal immunochemical testing (FIT) to guide referral for adults with specified colorectal symptoms or iron-deficiency anaemia. FIT ≥10 micrograms Hb/g faeces generally triggers suspected cancer pathway referral.

PLAB application: Follow NG12 eligibility and safety-netting; consider referral despite FIT below threshold if strong clinical concern persists.

Diagnostic colonoscopy

Colonoscopy allows direct visualisation and biopsy. CT colonography is an alternative when colonoscopy is unsuitable or incomplete; imaging choice depends on clinical context.

PLAB application: Histology confirms malignancy; a positive FIT is not a cancer diagnosis.

Staging

After diagnosis, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis assesses spread; pelvic MRI is key for local staging of rectal cancer. TNM classification describes tumour depth, nodes and metastases.

PLAB application: Staging and performance status inform multidisciplinary treatment.

Colon cancer treatment

Resectable colon cancer is usually treated surgically, with adjuvant chemotherapy for selected higher-risk stages. Metastatic disease requires individualised systemic and sometimes local treatment.

PLAB application: Explain that treatment depends on stage, fitness and patient preferences.

Rectal cancer treatment

Rectal cancer care may include neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, surgery and systemic therapy depending on MRI stage and multidisciplinary review.

PLAB application: Discuss possible stoma and effects on bowel, urinary and sexual function sensitively.

Screening in England

The NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme uses home FIT invitations for eligible people aged 50–74 in England, with invitations every two years. Rollout and eligibility may vary across UK nations.

PLAB application: Screening is for people without symptoms; symptomatic patients need diagnostic assessment regardless of screening status.

Positive screening FIT

A positive screening FIT means blood was detected and further assessment, usually colonoscopy, is offered. Most positive results are not cancer.

PLAB application: Explain benefits and risks of colonoscopy, including bleeding and perforation.

Hereditary CRC and surveillance

Lynch syndrome and FAP substantially increase lifetime risk and require specialist genetics, colonoscopic surveillance and sometimes preventive interventions. IBD-associated surveillance is risk-based.

PLAB application: Family history can change the pathway even when the patient is young.

PLAB 2 communication and safety-netting

Use clear non-alarmist language, explore concerns, explain FIT versus colonoscopy, shared decisions and the urgent referral process.

PLAB application: Advise urgent help for obstruction, heavy bleeding, collapse or severe pain; follow up persistent symptoms even after reassuring tests.

PLAB 2 OSCE: suspected colorectal cancer

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and explore concerns.
  2. Ask about rectal bleeding, bowel habit, weight loss, anaemia, abdominal pain and family history.
  3. Explain examination, FIT and possible urgent referral without implying a cancer diagnosis.
  4. Describe colonoscopy, biopsy and potential findings in accessible language.
  5. Safety-net obstruction, heavy bleeding, severe pain and ongoing symptoms despite reassuring tests.

Deep Dive

Adenoma–carcinoma sequence

Many sporadic cancers arise as mutations accumulate in precursor adenomas; not every polyp becomes malignant.

Why right-sided cancers cause anaemia

Proximal tumours may bleed slowly without visible blood, causing iron deficiency.

FIT is not a rule-out test

Sensitivity is imperfect. Persistent bleeding, anaemia, weight loss or strong clinical concern require safety-netting and sometimes referral despite low FIT.

Colon versus rectal treatment

Rectal cancers require precise pelvic MRI staging because the mesorectal anatomy and local recurrence risks influence neoadjuvant treatment.

Hereditary risk

Lynch involves DNA mismatch-repair pathways; FAP involves APC-associated polyposis. Specialist genetics can inform family screening.

Screening versus diagnostic work-up

Screening invites asymptomatic eligible people; a symptomatic person needs assessment regardless of age or previous screening results.
