Deep Dive · Molecular reproductive pharmacology

GnRH pulse frequency and feedback

GnRH acts on pituitary Gq-coupled receptors; pulsatile signalling sustains gonadotropin synthesis. Continuous agonist exposure first causes a flare then receptor desensitisation, whereas competitive antagonists suppress promptly. Estrogen and progesterone feedback varies across the cycle, with sustained estradiol causing the midcycle LH surge.

Steroid receptor pharmacodynamics

Estrogens and progestogens bind intracellular nuclear receptors, altering transcription of genes controlling endometrial development, cervical mucus and hypothalamic feedback. Progestogen preparations vary in androgenic and mineralocorticoid properties; clinical choice depends on the complete product rather than class name alone.

Pregnancy drug distribution

Maternal plasma volume and total body water increase, albumin falls and renal filtration rises; these changes may alter volume of distribution and clearance. Placental transfer can occur by diffusion and transporters. The timing and dose of exposure, fetal developmental stage and maternal disease are central to risk assessment.

Oxytocin receptor signalling

Oxytocin activates myometrial Gq-coupled receptors, phospholipase C, IP3 and intracellular calcium release, increasing uterine contractions. Excess stimulation can cause uterine tachysystole and fetal compromise. Continuous infusion must follow monitored obstetric protocols.

Magnesium and neuromuscular physiology

Magnesium modifies neuronal excitability and neuromuscular transmission; its anticonvulsant effect in eclampsia is distinct from BP control. Because it is renally cleared, oliguria raises toxicity risk. Reflexes, ventilation and urine output are bedside safety markers.

Tranexamic acid and clot stability

Tranexamic acid is a lysine analogue that inhibits plasminogen activation and fibrin breakdown. In postpartum haemorrhage it is an adjunct to urgent obstetric care and is most beneficial when administered promptly within the recommended window.

Fertility drug mechanisms

Letrozole lowers estrogen synthesis by inhibiting aromatase, reducing negative feedback and increasing endogenous FSH. Clomifene blocks estrogen feedback at hypothalamic receptors. Exogenous FSH stimulates follicles directly; hCG mimics the LH surge. Excessive follicular stimulation can trigger OHSS, characterised by vascular permeability and third-spacing.

Clinical synthesis

For each reproductive medicine, identify its receptor or enzyme, clinical indication, pregnancy and lactation implications, contraindications, interaction risks, required monitoring and the patient’s reproductive goals.

1. Clinical framework for reproductive prescribing

Begin with the patient’s goals: preventing pregnancy, planning pregnancy, managing infertility or treating a pregnancy complication. Ask about pregnancy possibility, last menstrual period, breastfeeding, migraine aura, smoking, thrombosis, liver disease, interacting medicines, sexual health and safeguarding. Establish urgency, informed choice and follow-up. Drug safety changes with gestational age and indication.

2. Hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal physiology

Pulsatile hypothalamic GnRH stimulates pituitary FSH and LH. FSH supports follicular growth and granulosa aromatase activity; LH drives theca androgen synthesis and triggers ovulation. Estradiol proliferates endometrium, progesterone makes it secretory. Continuous GnRH agonist exposure eventually downregulates pituitary receptors, unlike physiological pulses. These mechanisms explain contraceptive and fertility medicines.

3. Combined hormonal contraception

Combined pill, patch and vaginal ring suppress ovulation through estrogen–progestogen feedback; progestogen also thickens cervical mucus. Screen for UKMEC contraindications: migraine with aura, current or previous VTE and major thrombotic risk, certain hypertension and smoking at age 35 or older can make combined methods unsuitable. Explain missed-dose instructions are product-specific, and assess interactions with enzyme-inducing medicines.

4. Progestogen-only contraception

Progestogen-only pills primarily thicken cervical mucus; some preparations consistently suppress ovulation. The implant and injectable methods inhibit ovulation; levonorgestrel intrauterine systems act mainly locally. Compare bleeding changes, reversibility, duration, drug interactions and patient preferences. Enzyme-inducing antiepileptics can reduce effectiveness of some systemic hormonal methods, whereas intrauterine methods are generally unaffected.

5. Long-acting reversible contraception

Copper IUDs provide hormone-free highly effective contraception and can be used for emergency contraception if eligibility and timing criteria are met. Levonorgestrel intrauterine systems reduce endometrial proliferation and may treat heavy menstrual bleeding. Discuss insertion, expulsion, perforation, infection risk around insertion and ectopic pregnancy assessment if pregnancy occurs. Device duration and indications depend on product and current guidance.

6. Emergency contraception

Options include copper IUD, ulipristal acetate and levonorgestrel, with effectiveness and eligibility dependent on time since intercourse, cycle timing, body weight and interacting medicines. Copper IUD is the most effective option when suitable. Ulipristal can interact with subsequent progestogen initiation; use current FSRH guidance for timing of starting hormonal contraception and backup precautions. Exclude existing pregnancy where appropriate and advise testing if the period is late.

7. Contraceptive drug interactions

Enzyme inducers such as carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin and some antiretrovirals can lower exposure to hormonal contraceptives. Lamotrigine levels can fall with estrogen-containing contraception, risking seizure breakthrough; changes in estrogen exposure can also alter lamotrigine concentrations. Most routine broad-spectrum antibiotics do not impair pill efficacy, but vomiting and diarrhoea may affect absorption. Use current FSRH and BNF interaction guidance.

8. Preconception medication review

Before pregnancy, optimise chronic illness, review teratogenic medicines and ensure appropriate folic acid supplementation. Medicines with major fetal risk include valproate, methotrexate, isotretinoin, ACE inhibitors and ARBs; stopping or switching must be planned with the relevant specialist, especially for epilepsy and psychiatric illness. Do not abruptly stop essential treatment without balancing maternal disease risk. Discuss vaccines, smoking, alcohol and timing of review.

9. Pregnancy pharmacokinetics and fetal exposure

Pregnancy changes gastric emptying, plasma volume, albumin concentration, renal filtration and some hepatic enzyme activity, so drug concentrations may change. Placental passage depends on molecular size, lipophilicity, protein binding and transporters; the placenta is not an absolute barrier. Teratogenic risk is often highest during organogenesis, but fetal growth and neonatal adaptation can be affected later. Use UKTIS/BUMPS and BNF for medicine-specific decisions.

10. Analgesics, antimicrobials and chronic disease medicines

Paracetamol is commonly considered when analgesia is necessary, using the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate duration. NSAIDs have gestation-dependent fetal and maternal risks and should not be used casually in pregnancy. Penicillins and cephalosporins are often used when indicated, but antimicrobial choice depends on infection and susceptibility. Manage diabetes, asthma, epilepsy and hypertension with condition-specific pregnancy guidance rather than withholding all treatment.

11. Hypertension and pre-eclampsia medicines

Labetalol, nifedipine and methyldopa are commonly used in pregnancy according to clinical context and NICE guidance; ACE inhibitors and ARBs should be avoided in pregnancy. Pre-eclampsia requires maternal and fetal assessment, blood pressure control, laboratory surveillance and obstetric planning. Magnesium sulfate is used for prevention/treatment of eclamptic seizures in appropriate settings; it is not primarily an antihypertensive. Monitor reflexes, respiratory rate and urine output during therapy.

12. Magnesium sulfate and eclampsia

Eclampsia is seizure activity associated with pre-eclampsia and requires immediate ABCDE, maternal positioning, urgent obstetric/anaesthetic assistance and seizure management. Magnesium sulfate reduces recurrent seizures; toxicity causes loss of deep tendon reflexes, respiratory depression and cardiac effects, especially with renal impairment. Stop infusion and follow emergency protocol if toxicity is suspected; calcium gluconate is the antidote under specialist supervision.

13. Labour induction and uterotonics

Oxytocin stimulates myometrial contractions and is used for induction/augmentation and postpartum haemorrhage management. Excessive uterine activity can compromise fetal oxygenation, so monitor contractions and fetal status. Prostaglandin preparations such as dinoprostone or misoprostol have indication- and route-specific uses and restrictions. Choice depends on cervix, uterine scar, gestation and local obstetric protocols.

14. Postpartum haemorrhage medicines

Treat significant bleeding as a time-critical obstetric emergency: call for help, assess ABCDE, resuscitate, identify the 4 Ts (tone, tissue, trauma, thrombin) and use uterotonics and mechanical/surgical measures as indicated. Oxytocin is a key uterotonic; tranexamic acid should be given promptly within the evidence-based treatment window according to protocol. Ergometrine is avoided in hypertension/pre-eclampsia. Misoprostol may be used in specified settings. Never delay source control.

Emergency prescribing distinction

Oxytocin contracts the uterus; tranexamic acid reduces fibrinolysis. Neither replaces resuscitation, examination and definitive treatment of the bleeding source. Ergometrine is contraindicated in important hypertensive settings.

15. Preterm labour and antenatal corticosteroids

Nifedipine may be used for short-term tocolysis in selected preterm labour to permit antenatal corticosteroids and transfer; it is not suitable in every scenario. Antenatal corticosteroids such as betamethasone or dexamethasone accelerate fetal lung maturation when preterm birth is likely within the guideline-defined gestational window. Magnesium sulfate may be used for fetal neuroprotection in selected early preterm births. Follow NICE NG25 and obstetric assessment.

16. RhD prophylaxis and anti-D immunoglobulin

Anti-D immunoglobulin prevents sensitisation in eligible unsensitised RhD-negative pregnant patients after relevant sensitising events and as routine antenatal prophylaxis according to local policy. Check maternal blood group, antibody status, event timing and fetal/newborn RhD status when applicable. It is prophylaxis, not treatment for established maternal alloimmunisation. Doses and timing follow obstetric/transfusion protocols.

17. Infertility and ovulation induction

Evaluate both partners, ovulation, tubal factors and semen analysis before choosing treatment. Letrozole, an aromatase inhibitor, is used for ovulation induction in selected patients with PCOS under specialist guidance; clomifene is a selective estrogen receptor modulator. Gonadotropins stimulate follicular development but increase risks of multiple pregnancy and ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS). Ultrasound monitoring and specialist oversight are essential.

18. GnRH analogues and assisted reproduction

Pulsatile GnRH supports gonadotropin release; continuous GnRH agonists suppress the axis after initial flare. GnRH antagonists suppress LH surge promptly in assisted reproduction. hCG can trigger final oocyte maturation but may contribute to OHSS. IVF protocols use individualized stimulation, trigger and luteal support. Explain that medicines are only one component of infertility treatment and may carry thrombosis and multiple-pregnancy risks.

19. Breastfeeding and postpartum prescribing

Assess infant age, prematurity, medicine dose, milk transfer, oral bioavailability and potential sedation or toxicity. Some drugs are compatible with breastfeeding; others need alternatives or monitoring. UK Drugs in Lactation Advisory Service (SPS) and BNF provide medicine-specific advice. Avoid assuming a medicine is safe merely because it is safe in pregnancy. Discuss postpartum contraception and return of fertility.

20. PLAB 2 counselling and safe handover

For contraception: explore goals, explain typical-use versus perfect-use effectiveness, contraindications, STI protection, bleeding effects, missed-dose and emergency contraception advice. For pregnancy prescribing: explain maternal benefit versus fetal risk, alternatives, monitoring and who to contact urgently. Use teach-back, document shared decisions, and provide red flags such as severe headache/visual symptoms, heavy bleeding, chest pain or breathlessness.

Four worked PLAB clinical cases

Case A — Migraine aura and contraception

A 28-year-old asks for the combined pill and reports visual aura before headaches. Reasoning: migraine with aura makes estrogen-containing combined contraception unsuitable; discuss progestogen-only or intrauterine options, preferences, STI prevention and emergency contraception.

Case B — Epilepsy and planning pregnancy

A patient taking valproate wants to conceive. Reasoning: do not abruptly stop seizure treatment; urgently coordinate specialist review of pregnancy prevention requirements, alternatives and preconception planning under current MHRA measures.

Case C — Seizure at 35 weeks

A pregnant patient has severe hypertension, headache and a generalised seizure. Reasoning: presume eclampsia until assessed; ABCDE, urgent obstetric/anaesthetic response, magnesium sulfate according to protocol, BP control and delivery planning after stabilisation.

Case D — Heavy bleeding after delivery

A patient develops heavy postpartum bleeding with a boggy uterus. Reasoning: activate major obstetric haemorrhage response, resuscitate, uterine massage and uterotonics per protocol, prompt tranexamic acid when indicated, and identify the 4 Ts.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Contraceptive counselling

Confirm the patient’s priorities, pregnancy intentions and relevant history. Ask specifically about migraine aura, thromboembolism, medicines, smoking, breastfeeding and STI exposure. Explain suitable choices, efficacy in typical use, common adverse effects and return of fertility. Discuss condoms for STI protection, missed doses, emergency contraception, when to test for pregnancy and when to seek urgent care. Use shared decision-making and teach-back.

    Educational overview; use current FSRH, NICE, BNF and local obstetric protocols for patient care.

    Choosing contraception

    Assess goals and pregnancy risk → screen UKMEC conditions and interactions → compare LARC, hormonal and nonhormonal options → explain STI protection and emergency contraception → shared choice and follow-up.

    Seizure or haemorrhage in pregnancy

    ABCDE and emergency obstetric help → eclampsia: magnesium sulfate and BP management per protocol; postpartum haemorrhage: resuscitation, 4 Ts, uterotonics and timely tranexamic acid → monitor and plan definitive care.

    Medicine review before and during pregnancy

    Confirm indication and gestational stage → assess maternal disease risk → check UKTIS/BNF and specialist advice → review teratogens and alternatives → monitor mother and fetus → document shared decision.

    Fertility treatment

    Assess both partners and ovulation/tubal/semen factors → identify underlying condition → specialist-led letrozole, clomifene or gonadotropin options → ultrasound monitoring → counsel OHSS and multiple pregnancy risk.
