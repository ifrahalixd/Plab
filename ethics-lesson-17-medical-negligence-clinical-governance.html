Medical Negligence, Clinical Governance & Legal Accountability

PLAB core: A poor outcome does not automatically prove negligence. Civil clinical negligence generally requires duty of care, breach of duty, causation and legally recognised harm.

1 · Four elements of negligence

  1. Duty: a clinician owes the patient a duty to take reasonable care.
  2. Breach: care falls below the applicable professional standard.
  3. Causation: the breach caused the alleged injury; distinguish factual from legal causation.
  4. Damage: the patient suffered compensable harm.

2 · Standard of care

Bolam: professional practice is assessed against a responsible body of relevant professional opinion. Bolitho: that opinion must withstand logical analysis. These are simplified exam principles, not a substitute for legal advice. Informed consent and material-risk disclosure are governed by patient-centred principles in Montgomery, rather than relying solely on Bolam.

3 · Consent and negligence

Explain material risks, reasonable alternatives and the option of no treatment. Materiality depends on what a reasonable person in this patient's position would consider significant and what this particular patient is likely to attach significance to. Document the dialogue; a signed form alone is insufficient.

4 · Clinical governance

Clinical governance is the framework through which organisations are accountable for continually improving quality and safeguarding standards. It includes audit, evidence-based practice, incident learning, risk management, education, patient experience, information governance and leadership.

5 · Audit versus research

Clinical audit: compares current practice against an explicit standard and repeats measurement after improvement. Research: seeks to generate new generalisable knowledge. Quality improvement: tests changes to improve a local system, often using iterative PDSA cycles. Governance/ethics requirements depend on project design.

6 · Errors and accountability

Prioritise immediate patient safety, seek help, communicate openly and apologise, record facts accurately, use local incident reporting, and cooperate with investigation. The professional duty of candour applies when things go wrong that cause or could cause harm or distress; organisational statutory duties have distinct thresholds.

7 · Legal and professional pathways

A civil claim for compensation is different from a GMC fitness-to-practise investigation, an NHS complaint, a coroner's investigation or a criminal case. The same event may trigger more than one process. Preserve records; do not alter notes to protect yourself.

Jurisdiction: The named negligence cases are chiefly England-and-Wales/UK exam reference points. UK nations have differing court systems, procedural rules and legislation.

Clinical decision pathways

A · Patient harmed by possible error

  1. Assess and treat immediate harm.
  2. Escalate to senior and relevant safety team.
  3. Explain known facts, apologise and provide a plan; avoid speculation.
  4. Document contemporaneously and report through local systems.
  5. Arrange review, investigation and learning.

B · Is negligence established?

  1. Was a duty of care owed?
  2. Was the relevant standard breached?
  3. Did that breach cause the injury?
  4. Is there legally recognised damage?
  5. Do not assume negligence solely from an adverse outcome.

C · Audit cycle

  1. Choose problem and agreed standard.
  2. Measure baseline performance.
  3. Analyse gaps and implement change.
  4. Re-audit to confirm improvement.

D · Material risk discussion

  1. Explore patient priorities and concerns.
  2. Explain benefits, material risks and reasonable alternatives.
  3. Check understanding and support decision.
  4. Record discussion and agreed plan.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Known complication

A patient develops a recognised complication despite appropriate technique and informed consent.

Reasoning: harm alone does not establish breach or causation. Review facts and support the patient.

Case 2 · Missed sepsis

Abnormal observations were ignored and antibiotics delayed; a patient deteriorated.

Action: stabilise and escalate immediately, disclose honestly, document and report. Whether legal negligence is proved requires separate analysis of breach, causation and harm.

Case 3 · Material risk omitted

A patient with specific concerns about hand function was not told of a relevant surgical nerve-injury risk.

Principle: a risk can be material because of this patient's priorities; a signed consent form does not cure inadequate discussion.

Case 4 · VTE prophylaxis audit

A ward finds only 65% of eligible patients have documented VTE assessment against a 95% target.

Action: identify causes, implement an intervention, then re-measure against the standard.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Explaining a clinical error

Station: A patient has experienced a delay in receiving indicated antibiotics. They are now stable and ask what happened.

Suggested consultation

  1. Introduce yourself, ensure privacy and ask what they already understand.
  2. Use plain language: “Your antibiotics were delayed. This should not have happened.”
  3. Apologise sincerely; acknowledge distress and avoid blaming colleagues.
  4. Explain known facts and uncertainty without guessing about consequences.
  5. Describe immediate clinical review, monitoring and treatment plan.
  6. Offer questions, support, written follow-up and complaint routes.
  7. Explain incident review and learning; document the conversation.

Examiner checklist

  • Patient safety first
  • Clear factual explanation and apology
  • Honesty about uncertainties
  • Empathy and opportunity for questions
  • Follow-up, escalation and documentation

Do not pronounce legal liability or guarantee an outcome in an OSCE; provide an honest clinical explanation.

Common PLAB traps

  • Assuming a complication automatically proves negligence.
  • Applying Bolam alone to informed-consent risk disclosure.
  • Confusing an NHS complaint with a civil negligence claim.
  • Assuming an apology is an admission of legal liability.
  • Calling a one-off data collection exercise a complete audit cycle.
