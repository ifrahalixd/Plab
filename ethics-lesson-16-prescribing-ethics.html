Prescribing Ethics, Controlled Drugs & Professional Responsibilities

PLAB core: Prescribe only when you have adequate knowledge of the patient, a clear clinical indication, sufficient competence and appropriate monitoring arrangements. Patient safety comes before convenience or pressure.

1 · Safe prescribing sequence

  1. Identify: confirm patient, indication, allergies, current medicines, pregnancy status when relevant, renal/hepatic function and interactions.
  2. Choose: use evidence-based guidance, consider non-drug alternatives and choose drug, dose, route, frequency and duration.
  3. Discuss: explain material benefits, risks, alternatives, monitoring and safety-netting; seek informed agreement.
  4. Prescribe: use clear, complete, legible and accurate prescriptions; check high-risk drugs and calculations.
  5. Review: monitor benefit, toxicity, adherence, repeat prescribing and deprescribing opportunities.

2 · Competence and responsibility

Prescribe within your competence, follow local policy, check reliable prescribing references and seek senior/pharmacist advice if uncertain. A prescriber remains responsible for prescriptions they sign, even when another person recommends the medicine. Do not prescribe simply because a patient demands it.

3 · Prescribing for yourself or someone close

GMC guidance says you must avoid prescribing for yourself or anyone with whom you have a close personal relationship wherever possible. Exceptionally, it may be necessary when no other prescriber is available and delay would risk serious deterioration or serious harm. You must be able to justify and document the decision and follow applicable notification requirements. Extra restrictions apply to controlled drugs.

4 · Controlled drugs

Controlled drugs have additional legal, record-keeping, prescribing and storage requirements. In England, most Schedule 2 and 3 controlled-drug prescriptions have specific formal requirements; validity periods and quantity guidance differ by schedule and jurisdiction. Check current national rules and local policy rather than assuming all controlled drugs follow one rule.

Exam trap: A request for opioids, benzodiazepines or other dependence-forming medicines requires clinical assessment, risk evaluation, appropriate monitoring and documentation—not automatic refusal or automatic prescribing.

5 · Errors, candour and escalation

When a prescribing error occurs, prioritise immediate clinical safety: assess the patient, withhold/correct unsafe treatment as clinically appropriate, inform seniors and pharmacy, arrange monitoring or antidote if indicated, communicate openly with the patient, report through local safety systems and document accurately. An apology does not mean admitting legal liability.

6 · Remote and repeat prescribing

Remote prescribing needs sufficient information and safeguards for safe assessment, identity verification, communication, monitoring and follow-up. Repeat prescriptions are not a substitute for medication review; check indication, current dose, adverse effects, required tests and ongoing need.

7 · Ethical dilemmas

Balance autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. Manage conflicts of interest and industry influence transparently. Do not let gifts, incentives or personal benefit influence prescribing.

Clinical decision pathways

A · Patient requests an inappropriate antibiotic

  1. Assess symptoms, observations and red flags.
  2. Explain why an antibiotic is not indicated and discuss harms such as resistance and adverse effects.
  3. Offer symptom relief, clear safety-netting and review if worsening.
  4. Document shared decision-making.

B · Prescription error discovered

  1. Assess immediate harm and secure urgent treatment.
  2. Stop or correct the unsafe prescription and involve the senior/pharmacist.
  3. Explain to the patient under duty of candour when applicable.
  4. Record facts, report incident, monitor and learn.

C · Family member asks for a controlled drug

  1. Do not issue routinely; advise independent assessment.
  2. Check for immediate danger and arrange emergency care if necessary.
  3. Follow the especially strict GMC controlled-drug safeguards; seek appropriate advice.
  4. Document decisions and follow applicable reporting rules.

D · Repeat prescription without monitoring

  1. Review clinical urgency and risk of interruption.
  2. Check monitoring, contraindications and recent changes.
  3. Arrange tests/review and prescribe only if safe and justified.
  4. Safety-net and document the plan.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Penicillin allergy

A patient prescribed amoxicillin has a documented previous anaphylactic reaction to penicillin.

Best action: do not administer; check clinical status and choose an appropriate alternative after confirming allergy and indication. Escalate if already given.

Case 2 · Insulin prescribing error

A junior doctor writes an insulin dose ten times higher than intended; the nurse notices before administration.

Best action: correct urgently, check patient safety, report the near miss, document and review how to prevent recurrence.

Case 3 · Relative asks for morphine

Your close relative requests morphine for severe pain, but independent medical assessment is available.

Best action: arrange independent clinical assessment rather than prescribing a controlled drug for someone close to you.

Case 4 · Methotrexate monitoring

A patient asks for another supply of methotrexate; required blood monitoring is overdue.

Best action: assess urgency, toxicity symptoms and monitoring, contact the responsible specialist/team and arrange safe review before further prescribing.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Explaining a prescribing error

Station: You are an FY2 doctor. A patient received an incorrect medication dose and is clinically stable. Explain the event and next steps.

Suggested approach

  1. Introduction: introduce yourself, verify identity, provide privacy and check the patient's understanding.
  2. Be clear: “We made an error with the dose of your medicine.” Avoid euphemisms or blame.
  3. Apologise: offer a sincere apology, acknowledge distress and answer questions honestly.
  4. Explain known facts: what happened, what is known and unknown, and potential consequences without speculation.
  5. Safety plan: explain assessment, monitoring, treatment, senior review and how the patient can get help.
  6. Follow-up: describe incident review, communication of findings and contact arrangements.
  7. Document: record discussion and follow local reporting/candour processes.
Examiner checklist: empathy; transparency; apology; immediate safety; no blame; monitoring; documentation; follow-up; checks understanding.

Do not falsely promise that no harm can occur. Do not speculate about fault or conceal the error.

Common PLAB traps

  • Prescribing antibiotics to satisfy demand.
  • Assuming the supervising doctor or pharmacist carries all responsibility for your signed prescription.
  • Giving a controlled drug to a relative as a favour.
  • Hiding a medication error because no harm occurred.
  • Repeating a high-risk medicine without appropriate monitoring.
