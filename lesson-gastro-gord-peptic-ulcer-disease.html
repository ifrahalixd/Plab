GORD & Peptic Ulcer Disease

Emergency reminder: Haematemesis, shock, severe abdominal pain with guarding or concerning chest pain requires urgent assessment.

GORD: definition and mechanism

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) occurs when reflux of gastric contents causes troublesome symptoms or complications. Transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxations and impaired barrier function contribute.

PLAB application: Typical symptoms are heartburn and acid regurgitation, often after meals or lying down.

GORD: symptoms and differentials

Heartburn, sour regurgitation, water brash, nocturnal cough and hoarseness can occur. Dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss or gastrointestinal bleeding need further assessment.

PLAB application: Do not automatically attribute chest pain to reflux: consider acute coronary syndrome when clinically indicated.

Alarm features and urgent referral

Ask about progressive dysphagia, persistent vomiting, haematemesis, melaena, unexplained weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia and palpable masses. Follow NICE NG12 suspected-cancer criteria and local referral pathways.

PLAB application: Dysphagia is a key trigger for urgent upper gastrointestinal assessment.

Initial GORD management

Offer lifestyle measures suited to the patient: weight management if appropriate, avoid personal triggers, stop smoking, avoid late meals and elevate the head of the bed for nocturnal symptoms.

PLAB application: NICE CG184 recommends a full-dose proton pump inhibitor (PPI) for 4 or 8 weeks for GORD, with review and step-down to the lowest effective dose when possible.

PPIs and H2 blockers

PPIs inhibit gastric acid secretion and are generally more effective for healing reflux oesophagitis than H2-receptor antagonists. Consider adherence, timing and alternative diagnoses if symptoms persist.

PLAB application: Review long-term PPI need periodically; use current BNF for contraindications, interactions and dosing.

Oesophagitis, strictures and Barrett's

Reflux can cause erosive oesophagitis, peptic stricture and Barrett's oesophagus (intestinal metaplasia associated with increased oesophageal adenocarcinoma risk).

PLAB application: Endoscopy is guided by alarm symptoms, persistent symptoms and risk assessment; not every person with uncomplicated GORD needs one.

Dyspepsia and peptic ulcer disease

Dyspepsia includes upper abdominal discomfort, early satiety or epigastric pain. Peptic ulcers are mucosal defects, commonly gastric or duodenal, caused by Helicobacter pylori or NSAID exposure.

PLAB application: Symptoms alone cannot reliably distinguish ulcer disease from functional dyspepsia.

H. pylori testing

For uncomplicated dyspepsia, NICE supports non-invasive H. pylori 'test and treat' where appropriate. Use a urea breath test or stool antigen test.

PLAB application: Allow a 2-week PPI washout before breath or stool antigen testing, and at least 4 weeks after antibiotics; follow laboratory guidance.

H. pylori eradication

If H. pylori is detected, offer an eradication regimen selected using current NICE antimicrobial guidance, allergy history and previous antibiotic exposure.

PLAB application: Do not memorise one universal antibiotic combination: local resistance, prior exposure and allergies change treatment.

NSAID-associated ulcer disease

NSAIDs impair prostaglandin-mediated mucosal protection. Review the need for NSAIDs, stop if possible and treat ulcers with an acid-suppressing PPI.

PLAB application: If NSAID therapy must continue, assess gastroprotection and bleeding risk.

Gastric vs duodenal ulcers

Both may cause epigastric pain or present with bleeding. Pain related to meals is not sufficiently reliable for diagnosis.

PLAB application: Gastric ulcers generally require endoscopic follow-up to document healing and exclude malignancy under specialist guidance.

Upper GI bleeding

Haematemesis, coffee-ground vomit, melaena, syncope or shock suggest upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Assess ABCDE, IV access, blood tests and resuscitation.

PLAB application: Use Glasgow-Blatchford score at first assessment where appropriate; urgent endoscopy and specialist care follow NICE CG141.

Perforated peptic ulcer

Sudden severe generalised abdominal pain with guarding, rigidity or sepsis may indicate perforation and peritonitis.

PLAB application: This is a surgical emergency: resuscitate, keep nil by mouth and seek urgent surgical assessment.

Persistent or refractory symptoms

Review adherence, drug timing, NSAIDs, red flags and alternative diagnoses. Consider endoscopy or specialist referral if symptoms persist despite appropriate management.

PLAB application: Consider functional dyspepsia, biliary disease, cardiac disease or oesophageal motility disorders.

PLAB 1 clinical reasoning

Separate uncomplicated reflux/dyspepsia from alarm features, ulcer complications and cardiac mimics. Know when to test for H. pylori and when to refer.

PLAB application: Classic exam distinctions: dysphagia needs urgent assessment; bleeding/perforation needs emergency management.

PLAB 2 counselling

Elicit ideas, concerns and expectations. Explain reflux and ulcer mechanisms simply, discuss PPI trial, H. pylori testing and medicines review.

PLAB application: Safety-net for swallowing difficulty, vomiting blood, black stools, severe abdominal pain, fainting or unintended weight loss.

PLAB 2: counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, check identity and explore symptoms and concerns.
  2. Ask about swallowing difficulty, bleeding, weight loss, NSAIDs and chest pain.
  3. Explain reflux versus ulcer disease and the rationale for testing or PPI therapy.
  4. Discuss H. pylori testing preparation and follow-up where appropriate.
  5. Safety-net for haematemesis, melaena, dysphagia, syncope or severe pain.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Acid physiology

Parietal cells secrete hydrogen ions via H+/K+-ATPase. PPIs inhibit this pump and suppress acid production.

H. pylori and ulcers

H. pylori causes chronic gastritis and changes acid regulation and mucosal defence; eradication reduces ulcer recurrence.

NSAID injury

Cyclo-oxygenase inhibition reduces prostaglandins, mucus and bicarbonate secretion and mucosal blood flow.

Barrett's metaplasia

Chronic acid exposure can lead to columnar intestinal-type epithelium replacing normal squamous epithelium, raising cancer risk.

Why test washout matters

PPIs and antibiotics reduce bacterial activity and can cause false-negative breath or stool antigen results.

Bleeding and perforation

Ulcers can erode vessels causing haemorrhage or penetrate the full wall causing peritonitis and sepsis.
