Abnormal development and developmental delay: from first concern to diagnosis

Development is the progressive acquisition of movement, communication, cognitive, social and adaptive skills. Developmental delay is a description, not an aetiological diagnosis. Start by confirming the nature and trajectory of the concern, looking for reversible sensory or environmental contributors, and identifying urgent neurological warning signs. Assess the child as a person within their family, not simply as a list of milestones.

Article audio
60-second summary. (1) Explore the parent/carer's concern in their words and review milestones across multiple domains. (2) Distinguish isolated speech, motor or social difference from global developmental delay (significant delays in ≥2 domains in a young child). (3) Correct age for prematurity where appropriate, generally up to 2 years. (4) Regression (loss of acquired skills) warrants urgent specialist evaluation—especially with seizures, weakness or deteriorating swallowing. (5) Test hearing and vision when indicated even if a child appears to respond. (6) Examine growth, head circumference, neurology and dysmorphic features. (7) Begin support and early intervention while investigating; don't wait for a final diagnostic label.

1. Classifying the developmental concern

Delay means a skill is acquired later than expected for developmental age. Deviation/difference describes an atypical pattern of development, not necessarily an illness. Regression is a loss of a previously acquired skill and is more concerning than isolated late acquisition. A plateau is cessation of forward progress; repeat history and surveillance are needed. Global developmental delay (GDD) is used principally in children under about 5 years when significant delay affects at least two domains; later assessment may establish an intellectual developmental disorder, but intellectual disability should not be inferred from one milestone alone.

Children do not all develop identically. Developmental screening tools describe probability of concern rather than establishing a diagnosis. Context, neurodiversity, culture and multilingual language exposure matter. Being bilingual does not itself cause a language disorder.

Persistent parental concern deserves assessment even when one individual milestone falls within a broad range.

3. Focused history: six layers

  1. Present concern and timeline: who noticed it, domains affected, earliest difference, progressive improvement, plateau or true loss, and impact on everyday life; obtain nursery/school observations with consent.
  2. Pregnancy and birth: gestation, fetal growth, infections/exposures, antenatal scans, neonatal complications, hypoxic-ischaemic injury, jaundice, NICU admission, newborn hearing screen.
  3. Medical history: seizures or staring episodes, febrile illness/encephalitis, head injury, recurrent ear infections, sleep, feeding, choking, constipation, sensory concerns, chronic disease, medicines.
  4. Family and genetics: similar developmental profiles, neurological disorders, learning needs, inherited diseases, consanguinity where relevant without judgement; family head circumference pattern.
  5. Psychosocial context: language environment, play opportunities, hearing access, childcare and schooling, caregiver stress, nutrition, social determinants, safeguarding concerns without presuming blame.
  6. Strengths and family priorities: communication methods, interests, existing therapies, school adaptations, family goals; use a professional interpreter if needed.

Ask specifically whether the child ever had the skill and then lost it. Parents may call lack of progress a regression; clarify the distinction with examples (previously spoke several meaningful words, now none). Consider onset after seizures, trauma or illness; do not dismiss a clear loss as a normal variant.

4. Examination and structured observation

Begin with general paediatric assessment, growth centiles, weight, height and head circumference where age-appropriate, and look for changing centile trajectory. Observe spontaneous play, eye contact and gestures without treating one observation as a diagnosis. Check hearing/vision response but arrange formal assessment if concerns persist. Examine skin for neurocutaneous signs, face/limbs for congenital differences, the spine and gait, and perform age-appropriate neurological examination (tone, strength, symmetry, reflexes, posture and coordination).

5. Red flags and urgency

Same-day / emergency assessment for acute neurological deterioration, reduced consciousness, prolonged/new recurrent seizures, meningitis/encephalitis features, bulging fontanelle with vomiting or signs of increased intracranial pressure, new focal deficit, or acute swallowing/respiratory compromise. Urgent specialist referral for genuine developmental regression, progressive weakness, early asymmetric motor findings, marked abnormal head-growth trajectory or suspected cerebral palsy. Stable isolated delay still deserves timely assessment, but not every referral is an emergency.

Additional factors: prematurity, neonatal encephalopathy, family history of progressive disease, significant feeding difficulties, poor weight gain, dysmorphism, and possible non-accidental injury. Consider safeguarding sensitively if a child is unsafe or there are unexplained injuries; it is a parallel safety responsibility, not a default explanation for developmental differences.

6. Investigations: phenotype-driven, not a blanket panel

  1. Standardised developmental assessment: community paediatrics / child development service, appropriate developmental measures and information from home and school.
  2. Sensory evaluation: formal audiology for language delay and vision assessment when appropriate, including children who previously passed a neonatal hearing screen.
  3. Genetics: for unexplained GDD or syndromic features, discuss genomic testing with paediatric/genetics teams, using current local pathway and consent; chromosomal microarray or other sequencing may be considered depending on phenotype and service.
  4. Laboratory: targeted thyroid, CK, lead exposure, anaemia/nutritional or metabolic tests when history/examination suggests a cause. Routine expansive metabolic testing has low yield in stable nonspecific isolated delays.
  5. Neuroimaging: MRI when focal neurological signs, abnormal head-growth, seizures or a specific structural concern warrants it. Do not order routinely for every uncomplicated language delay.
  6. EEG: when epileptic seizures or an epileptic contribution to regression is suspected, not as a routine autism diagnostic test.

Document an explicit interpretation and follow-up plan for every result. A normal screening test does not negate genuine developmental concerns.

7. Multidisciplinary management and family support

Referral may include community paediatrics, neurodevelopmental/autism team, audiology, speech and language therapy, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, specialist teaching, dietetics and clinical genetics. Start practical communication and motor supports based on functional need, rather than waiting for labels or genetic results. Discuss appropriate early-years support and UK SEND pathways, with family consent and agreed goals. Arrange regular review because developmental trajectories evolve.

Communicating uncertainty: “Your concern is important. At present we can describe which developmental areas need support, but one appointment may not tell us the cause. We will check hearing and other relevant issues, refer for a fuller assessment and begin help now.” Do not promise normal development, impose blame or announce autism/intellectual disability on a single screening feature.

8. Five worked PLAB clinical cases

Case A — speech delay at 2 years

A 25-month-old uses few clear words, follows instructions inconsistently and had recurrent otitis media. Reasoning: distinguish expressive from receptive delay, ask about gestures and play, examine ears, arrange audiology and speech-language assessment; review broader development and social communication. Trap: assuming hearing is normal because the child occasionally turns to music.

Case B — asymmetric motor development

A 10-month-old born prematurely reaches mainly with the right hand and holds the left arm flexed. Reasoning: early persistent asymmetry and altered tone suggest unilateral central motor disorder; refer promptly to child development team for suspected cerebral palsy assessment and early therapy, using corrected age as appropriate. Trap: labelling a definite handedness at infancy as reassuring.

Case C — skill loss and seizures

A 3-year-old previously spoke short phrases but over several weeks lost words and developed episodes of unresponsiveness. Reasoning: true regression plus possible seizures is a high-risk pattern; urgent paediatric neurology evaluation, EEG and further testing guided by assessment. Trap: waiting months for routine autism assessment without investigating a neurological cause.

Case D — global delay and dysmorphism

A 3-year-old has delayed language, motor and adaptive skills, microcephaly and distinctive facial features. Reasoning: assess growth and detailed phenotype, obtain family and birth history, involve community paediatrics and clinical genetics; individualise genomic and metabolic testing. Start speech and motor support during evaluation. Trap: ordering indiscriminate investigations without clinical direction.

Case E — social communication concerns

A 4-year-old struggles with reciprocal play and flexibility, has sensory sensitivities and strong non-verbal reasoning. Reasoning: take a developmentally sensitive history across settings, explore strengths and supports, consider autism assessment per NICE CG128 while checking hearing and coexisting language/mental health needs. Trap: inferring global intellectual disability from social differences alone.

9. PLAB 2 OSCE: discuss concerns with a parent

Station: A parent says their 2-year-old is not talking like peers and asks, “Is it autism? Did I do something wrong?” Start with empathy and permission. Elicit precise language and social milestones, hearing, growth, seizures and regression; ask what worries them most, and what the child can do well. Explain that the cause cannot be diagnosed from one sign; suggest audiology, speech-language evaluation and a broader developmental assessment. Encourage communication-rich play, reading and interaction without blame. Provide a written plan, review timeline and safety-net for regression, seizures or acute neurological concerns.

PLAB examiner checklist: open listening; confirm developmental trajectory; ask about regression; discuss hearing and social communication; avoid premature diagnosis; explain next assessments; begin support; agree follow-up and safety-net; communicate respectfully with family.

Fast localisation strategy

  1. Only speech? Check hearing and language; assess receptive and non-verbal communication.
  2. Only motor? Examine symmetry, tone and strength to distinguish central from peripheral causes.
  3. Several domains? Consider GDD, syndromic, genetic, metabolic and broader neurodevelopmental causes.
  4. Progressive loss? Urgently pursue neurological causes, seizures and metabolic disorders.
  5. Behaviour/social differences? Explore autism and other explanations; seek evidence across settings and assess coexisting needs.
Clinical pitfall: Early hand preference can be pathological due to hemiplegia; bilingualism is not a cause of language disorder; a normal newborn hearing screen does not exclude later acquired hearing loss; autism cannot be diagnosed by eye contact alone.

Neuroscience · genetics · decision making

Deep Dive: mechanisms, neurodevelopment and clinical reasoning

1. Development is a network process

Neurodevelopment integrates neuronal proliferation and migration, synaptogenesis, myelination, experience-dependent plasticity and environmental interaction. Different brain networks mature at different rates; a child's functional profile is therefore more informative than a single global “mental age”. Genetic and acquired influences can overlap.

2. Central versus peripheral motor delay

Upper-motor-neuron patterns can produce asymmetry, hypertonia and brisk reflexes, although early infancy may show hypotonia before spasticity emerges. Lower-motor-neuron and primary muscle disorders tend to produce weakness, reduced reflexes and possible muscle wasting; phenotypes vary. Progressive loss of strength is not typical of classic cerebral palsy and warrants a broader investigation.

3. Cerebral palsy: why early action matters

Cerebral palsy refers to lasting motor/postural disorders attributed to non-progressive disturbances of the developing brain. The clinical phenotype evolves even though the underlying disturbance is non-progressive. Risk factors include prematurity and neonatal hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy. NICE NG62 advises urgent multidisciplinary child-development assessment when CP is suspected. Early physiotherapy, occupational therapy and communication supports target participation and prevent secondary complications.

4. Speech and social cognition are not equivalent

Speech production, language comprehension and reciprocal social communication use overlapping but distinct functions. A child with hearing loss may appear inattentive to speech; a child with developmental language disorder can show strong non-verbal social reciprocity; an autistic child may have fluent speech but differences in pragmatic communication. Assessment must triangulate history, observations and collateral reports.

5. Genetics: choose tests based on phenotype

Copy-number variants, single-gene disorders, repeat expansions and inherited metabolic disorders can influence developmental trajectories. Diagnostic yield of genomic testing is higher in syndromic/unexplained multisystem presentations than in isolated mild delay. The appropriate first-line testing strategy depends on local genomic services, consent, clinical findings and family history. A negative test does not necessarily exclude a genetic cause.

6. Regression is not always neurodegeneration

True regression can occur in epileptic encephalopathies, developmental disorders, severe illness, neuroinflammatory disease or metabolic disorders. A child who has lost language after the emergence of seizures requires a different priority from a child with longstanding isolated articulation difficulty. Focus on timeline, episodes, neurological signs and systemic clues before selecting investigations.

7. Screening, referral, diagnosis and intervention are separate steps

A developmental screening score quantifies concern and helps decide referral, but does not diagnose autism, cerebral palsy, intellectual disability or genetic syndromes. A comprehensive assessment uses history, functional examination, validated instruments where indicated and multidisciplinary judgement. Therapy and family support can begin while the cause remains uncertain.

8. UK service navigation

Community paediatrics and child development teams coordinate assessment. Audiology and speech-language teams address communication needs, neurodisability/neurology teams address motor or seizure features, and education systems provide additional support when indicated. NICE CG128 specifies assessment and referral principles for possible autism; NICE NG62 covers suspected cerebral palsy. Plan follow-up, document who is responsible for referrals and act if access is delayed.

9. Examiner-level integrated reasoning

Situation: 20-month-old is not independently walking. Clarify prematurity and corrected age, pattern of progress, hearing/vision, general development and family history. Observe gait attempts, tone, symmetry and hip movement. An isolated late walker with otherwise normal development may need monitored referral; asymmetric spasticity or weakness shifts urgency. Immediate deterioration, seizures or swallowing compromise changes the plan to urgent evaluation.

10. Family-centred ethics and prognosis

Use language that describes a child's strengths and support needs without reducing identity to diagnosis. Discuss uncertainty transparently, respect culturally appropriate communication, ensure consent and protect privacy. Prognosis depends on underlying cause, functional profile and available support; it cannot be reliably predicted from one early milestone.
