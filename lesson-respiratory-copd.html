COPD: diagnosis, long-term care and exacerbations

Emergency reminder: Severe breathlessness, confusion, cyanosis or respiratory exhaustion requires immediate assessment and escalation. This lesson is educational, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

What is COPD?

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is a common, preventable and treatable condition with persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow obstruction, usually linked to exposure to tobacco smoke or other inhaled particles.

Clinical application: Ask about smoking, biomass exposure, occupational dust, chronic cough, sputum, dyspnoea and exacerbations.

Pathophysiology

Small airway inflammation and remodelling narrow the airways; emphysema destroys alveolar walls and reduces elastic recoil. Air trapping and hyperinflation increase the work of breathing.

Clinical application: Distinguish airflow obstruction from restriction and understand why patients may develop exercise limitation.

Clinical presentation

Progressive exertional breathlessness, chronic cough, sputum and wheeze are typical. Recurrent winter infections and reduced exercise tolerance are common.

Clinical application: Examine respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, chest expansion, breath sounds, wheeze, cachexia, cyanosis and signs of cor pulmonale.

Diagnosis: spirometry

Consider COPD in people over 35 with risk exposure and characteristic symptoms. Confirm persistent airflow obstruction with quality-assured post-bronchodilator spirometry, typically FEV1/FVC below 0.7.

Clinical application: Interpret borderline results carefully in older or younger adults; assess alternative diagnoses and obtain chest X-ray, blood count and BMI as appropriate.

Differentials and overlap

Asthma usually has more variable symptoms and obstruction, while COPD tends to have persistent obstruction. Bronchiectasis, heart failure, lung cancer and interstitial lung disease may mimic COPD.

Clinical application: A history of asthma or steroid-responsive features changes inhaler choices. Do not assume all wheeze is COPD.

Assess severity and impact

Assess breathlessness using the MRC dyspnoea scale, exacerbation frequency, hospital admissions, smoking, exercise capacity, oxygenation and comorbidities.

Clinical application: FEV1 alone does not capture disease burden; document functional impact and use structured reviews.

Smoking cessation and prevention

Stopping smoking is the most effective intervention to slow smoking-related decline in lung function. Vaccination, physical activity and avoiding exposures are important.

Clinical application: Offer behavioural support and evidence-based stop-smoking treatments; discuss influenza and pneumococcal vaccination under current UK schedules.

Pulmonary rehabilitation

Structured exercise and education improve symptoms and functional capacity for eligible patients limited by breathlessness.

Clinical application: Offer pulmonary rehabilitation when indicated; assess barriers, nutrition and psychological wellbeing.

Stable inhaler treatment

Short-acting bronchodilators may relieve intermittent symptoms. For persistent breathlessness or exacerbations, long-acting bronchodilator therapy is central; LABA/LAMA combinations are commonly recommended.

Clinical application: ICS is not routine for everyone with COPD; consider asthma features, exacerbation pattern and pneumonia risk under NICE NG115 and local guidance.

Inhaler technique and review

Poor technique and adherence can undermine therapy. Inhaler choice should consider inspiratory flow, dexterity, preference and environmental impact.

Clinical application: Ask the patient to demonstrate use; review technique, adherence, exacerbations and side effects before escalating.

Acute exacerbation

An exacerbation is a sustained worsening of baseline breathlessness, cough or sputum beyond usual daily variation, often triggered by infection or pollution.

Clinical application: Assess ABCDE, RR, pulse, SpO2, mental state, chest signs, blood gases if indicated and potential alternative causes such as pneumonia, PE or heart failure.

Oxygen in acute COPD

Patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure require controlled oxygen while awaiting blood gases. A common initial target is SpO2 88–92%, adjusted after blood-gas assessment and individual plans.

Clinical application: Avoid withholding oxygen from a critically hypoxaemic patient; titrate and reassess according to BTS/local emergency guidance.

Acute exacerbation treatment

Increase short-acting bronchodilator therapy, consider oral corticosteroids as recommended, and give antibiotics when bacterial infection is suspected, particularly with sputum purulence.

Clinical application: NICE NG114 covers antimicrobial decisions. Check severity, contraindications, local resistance and whether hospital treatment is needed.

Non-invasive ventilation

Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure with persistent respiratory acidosis despite optimal initial treatment may require NIV in an appropriately monitored setting.

Clinical application: Escalate promptly to senior respiratory/critical-care clinicians. NIV is not suitable for every patient; assess contraindications and goals of care.

Long-term oxygen and advanced care

Long-term oxygen therapy is considered after formal assessment in stable patients with chronic severe hypoxaemia; it is not prescribed solely for breathlessness.

Clinical application: Refer eligible patients for specialist oxygen assessment and discuss advance care planning, nutrition and palliative support when appropriate.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain COPD, the role of smoking cessation, inhalers, rehabilitation, vaccines and early recognition of exacerbations in plain language.

Clinical application: Provide an individualised self-management plan and advise urgent help for severe breathlessness, confusion, cyanosis, chest pain or deteriorating oxygenation.

PLAB 2: COPD counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and explore concerns.
  2. Ask about smoking, symptoms, exacerbations, work exposure and functional limitation.
  3. Explain spirometry, COPD and treatment goals in plain language.
  4. Demonstrate inhaler use; ask for teach-back and discuss smoking cessation and rehabilitation.
  5. Agree follow-up, self-management plan and emergency safety-net.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why expiration is difficult

Loss of elastic recoil and narrowed small airways limit expiratory airflow. Dynamic hyperinflation increases with faster breathing.

COPD and carbon dioxide

Some patients have chronic CO2 retention. Excess oxygen can worsen hypercapnia through several mechanisms, including ventilation–perfusion effects; hypoxaemia must still be treated.

Why NIV works

Positive pressure supports ventilation, decreases respiratory muscle workload and can improve acidosis in selected exacerbations.

Why ICS is selective

ICS can reduce exacerbations in appropriate phenotypes, but pneumonia and other adverse effects matter. A concurrent asthma diagnosis changes the balance.

Spirometry pitfalls

A fixed FEV1/FVC threshold can overdiagnose obstruction in older adults and underdiagnose it in younger adults; interpret clinically.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Consider inherited deficiency with early-onset emphysema, little smoking exposure or suggestive family history.
