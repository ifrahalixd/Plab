Acute Abdomen: Assessment & Initial Management

Surgery · Lesson 01 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Acute abdomen: recognise, resuscitate, refer

Core rule: In a patient with shock, peritonitis, or suspected catastrophic abdominal disease, begin ABCDE resuscitation and call the senior surgical team immediately. Do not delay lifesaving treatment for routine imaging.

1 · Focused assessment

  1. ABCDE: airway, respiratory effort and oxygen saturation; pulse, blood pressure, capillary refill, IV access; consciousness and glucose; exposure and temperature.
  2. Pain history: onset (sudden or gradual), site, migration, radiation, severity, colicky versus constant, vomiting, bowel movements, flatus, bleeding, urinary and gynaecological symptoms.
  3. Risk factors: previous abdominal surgery, hernias, vascular disease/atrial fibrillation, anticoagulants, pregnancy possibility, medication, allergies and comorbidities.
  4. Examination: general appearance and hydration; inspect, auscultate and gently palpate for focal tenderness, guarding, rigidity, distension and masses; examine hernial orifices and relevant pelvic/genital findings with consent and chaperone.
  5. Early actions: observations and repeat NEWS2, analgesia, antiemetic, IV access, fluids guided by perfusion and comorbidity, nil by mouth if surgery likely, and senior review.

3 · Investigations

Use targeted tests: FBC, U&Es, CRP, LFTs, glucose, VBG/lactate, urinalysis and pregnancy test when relevant. Add lipase, coagulation, group-and-save or crossmatch when indicated. Imaging depends on suspected pathology and stability: ultrasound for selected biliary/pelvic presentations, CT abdomen/pelvis for many adult surgical conditions, and CT angiography for suspected vascular or mesenteric pathology. A normal lactate does not exclude early bowel ischaemia.

4 · Management priorities

Reassess after each intervention. Treat suspected sepsis using current NICE guidance and local antimicrobial policy. Early antibiotics and urgent source control may be needed for intra-abdominal infection. Do not routinely administer antibiotics for all abdominal pain. Keep the patient informed, document changes and hand over with SBAR.

Decision pathways & revision slides

Pathway A · Acute abdominal pain

ABCDE + vital signs → unstable / peritonitic?

YES: call senior surgeon and anaesthetist, resuscitate, identify shock or sepsis, arrange urgent intervention; imaging only if safe and it will not cause harmful delay.

NO: focused history/exam → targeted labs and pregnancy testing as indicated → appropriate imaging → repeat assessment and clear disposition.

Pathway B · Suspected bowel obstruction

Vomiting + distension + obstipation → IV access, electrolyte correction, analgesia, nil by mouth, NG tube when indicated, CT to define cause and complications, urgent surgical review. Continuous severe pain, fever, tachycardia, peritonism or raised lactate raises concern for strangulation/ischaemia.

Slide 1 — Surgical red flags

Shock • rigid abdomen • sudden maximal pain • pain out of proportion • persistent vomiting with distension • gastrointestinal bleeding with instability • pregnancy-associated collapse.

Slide 2 — Investigations that change decisions

Pregnancy test, FBC, renal function, VBG/lactate, group and save/crossmatch, lipase and condition-specific CT/ultrasound. Do not let a normal initial test override a concerning clinical picture.

Slide 3 — PLAB safety net

Repeat observations, reassess pain and examination, record response to fluids/analgesia, involve senior decision-makers early, and communicate return precautions if discharge is appropriate.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 · Rigid abdomen and shock

A 68-year-old has sudden severe abdominal pain, BP 84/50, HR 128 and generalised rigidity. Action: ABCDE, urgent senior surgical and anaesthetic support, IV access, bloods/crossmatch, resuscitation and assessment for perforation or vascular catastrophe. Avoid waiting for routine CT if unsafe.

Case 2 · Pain out of proportion

A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation reports excruciating abdominal pain but mild tenderness. Action: suspect mesenteric ischaemia, urgent senior surgical/vascular review and CT angiography if clinically feasible; normal early lactate does not reassure.

Case 3 · Vomiting and distension

A 59-year-old with previous laparotomy has colicky pain, vomiting, abdominal distension and no flatus. Action: suspect adhesional small bowel obstruction; fluid/electrolyte assessment, nil by mouth, consider NG decompression, CT and surgical input. Watch for strangulation.

Case 4 · Right iliac fossa pain

A 24-year-old has migrating pain, anorexia, low-grade fever and right iliac fossa tenderness. Action: assess for appendicitis, check pregnancy status where relevant, give analgesia, perform appropriate investigations and discuss with surgery. Consider gynaecological and urinary alternatives.

PLAB 2 · Eight-minute acute abdomen station

Candidate brief: You are the FY2 doctor in ED. A 62-year-old presents with severe abdominal pain and vomiting. Assess and explain your immediate plan.

Opening script

“Hello, I'm one of the doctors. I can see you're in pain. I'll ask some focused questions, examine you with your permission and make sure we treat your pain while we work out what's happening.”

Structured checklist

  1. Introduce, identify, consent, assess pain and offer analgesia.
  2. Check immediate ABCDE red flags and observations.
  3. Ask onset, location, migration, character, severity, associated GI/urinary/pelvic symptoms.
  4. Ask prior surgery, hernia, vascular disease, drugs, anticoagulation and allergies.
  5. Explain need for abdominal examination, dignity and chaperone where appropriate.
  6. Outline blood tests, pregnancy test where relevant and targeted imaging.
  7. Escalate early if unstable or peritonitic; communicate uncertainty without false reassurance.
  8. Explain nil by mouth if surgery is possible, monitoring and reassessment; safety-net and document.

Examiner trap: Do not withhold analgesia to “preserve” abdominal signs. Do not send an unstable patient away for routine imaging without senior discussion.

Rapid revision & UK references

Five take-home rules

  1. Unstable acute abdomen = ABCDE + urgent senior surgical assessment.
  2. Rigidity and involuntary guarding indicate peritoneal irritation.
  3. Mesenteric ischaemia may have severe pain with a deceptively soft abdomen.
  4. Check pregnancy status when clinically relevant; ectopic pregnancy can be life-threatening.
  5. Reassess: deterioration can occur despite initially normal blood tests or observations.
