What is syncope?

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness due to global cerebral hypoperfusion, with rapid onset, short duration and spontaneous complete recovery. Distinguish it from seizure, hypoglycaemia, intoxication and head injury.

Assessment and management: Assess ABCDE, glucose if indicated, injuries, vital signs, a 12-lead ECG and a careful witness history. Determine whether a cardiac cause is possible.

Reflex (vasovagal) syncope

Common triggers include prolonged standing, heat, pain, fear and venepuncture. Prodrome can include warmth, nausea, sweating, visual dimming and light-headedness.

Assessment and management: Reassure after appropriate assessment, explain hydration, trigger avoidance and counter-pressure manoeuvres when safe. Consider further evaluation if recurrent or atypical.

Situational syncope

A reflex faint may occur during coughing, urination, defecation or swallowing.

Assessment and management: Identify the trigger, exclude cardiac red flags, and address contributing factors.

Orthostatic hypotension

A sustained fall in systolic BP of at least 20 mmHg or diastolic BP of at least 10 mmHg within 3 minutes of standing supports classical orthostatic hypotension. Symptoms include dizziness, visual disturbance, weakness and syncope.

Assessment and management: Measure BP and pulse lying and standing using a consistent protocol. Review dehydration, blood loss, medications, autonomic dysfunction and frailty.

Cardiac syncope

Arrhythmias, structural heart disease and outflow obstruction can reduce cerebral perfusion abruptly. Syncope during exertion or while supine, without warning, or with palpitations is concerning.

Assessment and management: Urgent assessment and ECG; consider monitoring, echo and specialist investigations guided by history and risk.

High-risk features

Red flags include exertional syncope, heart failure, new unexplained breathlessness, abnormal ECG, family history of sudden cardiac death under 40, heart murmur, and syncope with palpitations.

Assessment and management: NICE CG109 recommends urgent cardiovascular assessment within 24 hours for specified red flags. Use emergency pathways if unstable.

Syncope versus seizure

Seizures may involve prolonged confusion, lateral tongue biting, prolonged jerking or focal neurological features. Brief jerks can also occur in uncomplicated syncope, so movements alone are not diagnostic.

Assessment and management: Ask witnesses about colour, posture, prodrome, movements, duration, tongue biting and recovery. Refer appropriately if epilepsy is suspected.

Initial assessment

History should cover circumstances, posture, prodrome, duration, recovery, injury, drugs, family history and prior heart disease. Check cardiovascular and neurological findings and obtain a 12-lead ECG.

Assessment and management: Document ECG interpretation and decide on immediate referral versus outpatient assessment. Avoid indiscriminate CT brain or EEG for typical uncomplicated syncope.

Postural symptoms and medication

Diuretics, vasodilators and other drugs can contribute to orthostatic hypotension; dehydration, infection and blood loss are additional causes.

Assessment and management: Address reversible factors, review medicines with the prescriber and advise gradual position changes; treatment should reflect comorbidities.

POTS and orthostatic intolerance

Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome involves excessive heart-rate increase on standing with orthostatic symptoms, without the BP drop of classical orthostatic hypotension. It is not the same as syncope.

Assessment and management: Assessment requires careful orthostatic observations and exclusion of other causes; refer for specialist review when appropriate.

Investigations after a blackout

A 12-lead ECG is central. Ambulatory ECG monitoring is chosen based on event frequency when arrhythmic syncope is suspected; echocardiography is appropriate when structural disease is suspected.

Assessment and management: Do not use tilt testing as a routine first-line test for all patients; select tests according to clinical features and specialist guidance.

Driving and safety advice

Transient loss of consciousness can affect driving eligibility and safety-critical work. UK DVLA rules vary by diagnosis, recurrence and risk.

Assessment and management: Advise patients not to drive until the applicable DVLA guidance is checked and explain when they must notify DVLA. Give clear return precautions.

PLAB 2: focused OSCE approach

  1. Check ABCDE, injuries and observations.
  2. Ask about position, prodrome, triggers, witness account, recovery and family sudden death.
  3. Review medicines and perform cardiac and neurological examination plus lying/standing BP if safe.
  4. Explain the ECG and any urgent cardiovascular referral.
  5. Give tailored driving advice and clear safety-net instructions.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Why syncope occurs

A transient reduction in cerebral perfusion causes loss of consciousness. Reflex vasodilation and/or bradycardia, orthostatic BP drops and cardiac output obstruction are different mechanisms.

Vasovagal physiology

Autonomic reflexes can lower systemic vascular resistance and heart rate. The prodrome may include nausea, warmth and sweating.

Orthostatic physiology

Standing shifts blood to the lower body. Normal baroreflexes increase vascular tone and heart rate. Volume depletion, autonomic failure or medications may impair compensation.

Why cardiac syncope is dangerous

Arrhythmias and structural obstruction can cause abrupt loss of cardiac output, often without warning. Exertional or supine events merit particular attention.

Seizure versus syncope

Brief jerking can accompany syncope. Witness description and recovery time are often more useful than movement alone.

Monitoring choices

Frequent events may be captured with short-term monitoring; infrequent unexplained events may need longer monitoring selected by cardiology.

Driving and work

Fitness-to-drive and safety-critical work restrictions depend on cause, recurrence and treatment. Use current DVLA guidance rather than a universal timeframe.
