Paediatric Orthopaedics: Limping Child, Hip Disorders, Fractures & Bone Infections

Emergency red flags: Fever with refusal to bear weight, severe joint pain, neurovascular deficit, open fracture, disproportionate pain, suspected SUFE or safeguarding concern.

The limping child: first approach

Assess ABCDE if unwell. Clarify age, onset, injury, fever, pain location, ability to bear weight, preceding viral illness and night pain. Observe gait and inspect the spine, abdomen and both lower limbs. Examine hip, knee and ankle; hip pathology may present as knee pain. Record temperature and neurovascular findings.

PLAB application: A child unable to weight-bear, especially with fever, requires same-day emergency assessment.

Red flags and urgent differentials

Septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, malignancy, fracture and non-accidental injury must not be missed. Red flags include systemic illness, persistent night pain, focal bony tenderness, unexplained bruising, weight loss, pallor, neurological deficit and persistent symptoms. A normal initial radiograph does not exclude early infection or malignancy.

PLAB application: Do not label a febrile non-weight-bearing child as having transient synovitis.

Septic arthritis

Usually presents with acute severe joint pain, reduced range of motion, refusal to move or bear weight and sometimes fever. The hip may be held flexed, abducted and externally rotated. Obtain urgent paediatric and orthopaedic review, blood cultures and inflammatory markers; ultrasound can identify an effusion. Joint aspiration and drainage are specialist decisions.

PLAB application: Septic arthritis is a time-critical joint emergency; start appropriate IV antibiotics promptly according to specialist advice and local policy.

Transient synovitis

Often follows a viral illness in a child aged roughly 3–10 years. The child is usually systemically well, with a mild limp and limited hip movement. Diagnosis is clinical only after serious causes are considered. Provide analgesia, rest and planned review; escalate if fever, worsening pain or failure to improve.

PLAB application: Clinical improvement and ability to bear weight favour transient synovitis, but do not reliably exclude septic arthritis in isolation.

Perthes disease

Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease is avascular necrosis of the femoral head in childhood, often between 4 and 8 years. It causes gradual limp, hip or referred knee pain, and restricted abduction/internal rotation. AP pelvis and frog-leg lateral radiographs may help; early imaging can be normal. Refer to paediatric orthopaedics.

PLAB application: A persistent atraumatic limp with reduced hip internal rotation needs hip imaging and referral.

Slipped upper femoral epiphysis (SUFE/SCFE)

Typically affects adolescents, often with obesity or endocrine disorders. Symptoms include limp, hip, groin or knee pain and obligate external rotation with hip flexion. The affected leg may rest externally rotated. Stable and unstable slips both need urgent orthopaedic assessment; avoid weight-bearing and do not manipulate the hip.

PLAB application: Adolescent knee pain can originate in the hip. Make the patient non-weight-bearing and refer urgently.

Developmental dysplasia of the hip

Risk factors include breech presentation and family history. Newborn screening checks hip stability, while older infants may show limited abduction, leg-length asymmetry or gait abnormalities. Follow UK newborn examination and targeted ultrasound pathways; refer abnormal findings promptly.

PLAB application: A normal neonatal examination does not completely rule out later hip dysplasia.

Fractures: assessment and first aid

Assess mechanism, pain, swelling, deformity, open wounds and distal pulses, sensation and movement. Give timely analgesia, immobilise the injured limb and arrange appropriate imaging. Open fractures, neurovascular compromise and compartment syndrome need emergency orthopaedic input.

PLAB application: Document neurovascular status before and after splinting or reduction.

Paediatric fracture patterns

Children may sustain buckle/torus fractures, greenstick fractures and growth-plate (Salter–Harris) injuries. Management depends on location, displacement, age and local guidance. An apparently minor physeal injury can affect later growth.

PLAB application: Consider growth-plate injury when focal physeal tenderness persists despite equivocal radiographs.

Supracondylar humerus fracture

Common after a fall onto an outstretched hand. Check radial pulse, hand perfusion, median/anterior interosseous, radial and ulnar nerve function. Severe swelling, pain and vascular changes require urgent orthopaedic assessment. Avoid tight casts or splints.

PLAB application: A warm hand does not eliminate concern if the pulse is absent; specialist assessment is urgent.

Compartment syndrome

Disproportionate pain, pain on passive stretch, tense swelling and escalating analgesia requirements are early warning signs; paraesthesia and paralysis may occur later. Pulses can remain present. Remove constriction and obtain immediate surgical review.

PLAB application: Do not wait for pulselessness: it is a late sign.

Osteomyelitis

Bacterial bone infection can present with fever, focal bone pain, tenderness, reduced use of a limb or limp. Inflammatory markers and blood cultures support evaluation, but early plain radiographs may be normal. MRI is often most useful when diagnosis remains uncertain.

PLAB application: Urgent specialist assessment, microbiology sampling where feasible and appropriate antibiotics are key.

Discitis and spinal infection

Young children may refuse to walk, sit or bend, and may have back or abdominal pain with little fever. Check spinal tenderness, gait and neurology. Persistent symptoms or neurological signs warrant urgent specialist assessment and often MRI.

PLAB application: Consider spinal pathology in a child with unexplained refusal to walk.

Bone tumours and leukaemia

Persistent or night bone pain, swelling, systemic symptoms, pallor, bruising or weight loss are concerning. Osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma may cause local pain; leukaemia can present as diffuse bone pain and limp. Order appropriate initial tests and urgent referral according to suspected cancer pathways.

PLAB application: Do not repeatedly attribute persistent night pain to growing pains.

Non-accidental injury and safeguarding

Assess whether the history fits developmental ability and injury pattern. Multiple fractures at different healing stages, unusual bruising or delayed presentation may raise concern. Record factual observations and involve senior paediatric safeguarding teams; do not confront carers or make unsupported accusations.

PLAB application: Safeguarding and acute injury management must proceed together.

PLAB 2: explaining the plan and safety-netting

Use clear language: explain why serious infection or a slipped hip may need urgent assessment, seek consent for examination, offer age-appropriate analgesia, and communicate uncertainty. Advise urgent return for fever, inability to bear weight, worsening pain, numbness, cold limb or new swelling.

PLAB application: Reassure only after serious causes have been considered and a specific review plan is agreed.

PLAB 2 — Parent consultation

Take a structured limp history, assess pain and function, explain the need to exclude septic arthritis and SUFE, discuss imaging and analgesia, and give explicit return precautions with teach-back.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Paediatric Orthopaedics

Septic arthritis versus transient synovitis

A well child with a mild limp after viral illness may have transient synovitis, but fever, severe restriction of passive movement and inability to bear weight raise concern for septic arthritis. No single symptom, score or blood test safely excludes septic arthritis.

Hip disease by age

DDH in infancy, Perthes in younger school-age children and SUFE in adolescents are useful exam patterns, not absolute age cut-offs. Referred knee pain occurs in hip disease.

Imaging choices

Radiographs assess fracture and established hip pathology. Ultrasound detects hip effusion but cannot alone distinguish septic arthritis from transient synovitis. MRI may be required for early osteomyelitis, occult disease or spinal pathology.

Neurovascular assessment

After limb injury check perfusion, pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, motor function and sensation. Reassess after immobilisation. Escalate rapidly for worsening pain or neurological change.

Safeguarding nuance

Use neutral, accurate documentation of the history, examination, injuries and carers’ explanations. Follow local safeguarding policy and involve senior colleagues when concerned.

UK exam management priorities

Treat the sick child first; do not delay emergency referral for routine outpatient investigations. Follow local paediatric antibiotic and orthopaedic pathways, and current NICE guidance for fractures and suspected cancer.
