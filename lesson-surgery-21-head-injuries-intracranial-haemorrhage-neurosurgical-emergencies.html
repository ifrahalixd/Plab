Head injury: UK emergency assessment

Immediate danger: falling GCS, unequal pupils, seizure, focal deficit, suspected skull fracture or airway compromise after head trauma requires urgent senior assessment, resuscitation and appropriate neurosurgical discussion. Follow NICE NG232 and local major-trauma pathways.

1 · First assessment: <C>ABCDE

Assess and treat catastrophic haemorrhage, airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure; protect the cervical spine as indicated. Prevent hypoxia and hypotension, which worsen secondary brain injury. Record GCS as separate eye (E), verbal (V), motor (M) components, pupils, limb movement, glucose, observations and exact timing. Reassess frequently. Do not attribute confusion to alcohol until traumatic brain injury is assessed.

2 · Mechanism and history

Ask about fall/road collision/assault, loss of consciousness, amnesia, vomiting, seizures, headache, anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs, bleeding disorders, intoxication, previous neurosurgery, and safeguarding concerns. Consider non-accidental injury, especially in children and vulnerable adults. Examine for scalp wounds, signs of basal skull fracture (CSF leak, periorbital bruising, Battle sign, haemotympanum), depressed fracture and focal deficits.

3 · NICE CT head: adults aged 16 or over

CT within 1 hour of identification of any: GCS ≤12 on initial ED assessment; GCS <15 at 2 hours after injury; suspected open/depressed skull fracture; signs of basal skull fracture; post-traumatic seizure; focal neurological deficit; or more than one episode of vomiting. CT within 8 hours of injury (or within 1 hour of presentation if presenting after 8 hours) when there has been loss of consciousness or amnesia plus any: age ≥65, bleeding/clotting disorder, dangerous mechanism (pedestrian/cyclist struck, occupant ejected, fall >1 m or 5 stairs), or >30 minutes retrograde amnesia. Confirm the current NICE criteria in clinical practice.

4 · Anticoagulants and antiplatelets

For head injury in people taking anticoagulants (including DOACs, warfarin, heparin or LMWH) or antiplatelet therapy excluding aspirin monotherapy, consider CT head within 8 hours of injury, or within 1 hour if presentation is more than 8 hours after injury, even without other CT indications (NICE NG232). Discuss anticoagulant reversal urgently for suspected/confirmed intracranial bleeding according to agent and local protocol. Do not delay imaging for routine laboratory results.

5 · Types of traumatic intracranial haemorrhage

Extradural (epidural) haematoma

Often temporal skull fracture and middle meningeal artery injury; biconvex/lens-shaped CT collection, usually limited by sutures. A lucid interval may occur but is not universal. Urgent neurosurgical evacuation may be required.

Acute subdural haematoma

Typically bridging veins, often high-energy trauma or older adults; crescent-shaped collection crossing sutures but limited by dural reflections. May cause rapid mass effect; urgent neurosurgical assessment.

Traumatic subarachnoid haemorrhage

Blood in sulci/cisterns following trauma; monitor for deterioration and associated injuries. Distinguish from spontaneous aneurysmal SAH, often sudden thunderclap headache without preceding trauma.

Contusions, intraparenchymal haemorrhage and diffuse axonal injury

Contusions commonly affect frontal/temporal lobes; diffuse axonal injury may cause profound impairment with relatively subtle early CT findings. MRI may be used later after specialist review.

6 · Raised intracranial pressure and herniation

Warning signs: declining consciousness, headache/vomiting, unilateral dilated pupil, posturing, and late Cushing response (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations). Elevate head about 30° when safe, keep neck neutral, avoid hypoxia/hypotension, and urgently involve anaesthesia and neurosurgery. Hyperosmolar treatment (e.g. hypertonic saline) is specialist-directed; avoid routine prophylactic hyperventilation. A new fixed dilated pupil is an emergency.

7 · Severe traumatic brain injury management

Early expert airway management for GCS ≤8 or inability to protect airway; avoid secondary injury, treat seizures, ensure adequate cerebral perfusion and consider ICU and intracranial pressure monitoring according to neurosurgical advice. Avoid corticosteroids for traumatic brain injury. Tranexamic acid decisions follow current trauma/head injury guidance and local protocols; it is not a substitute for surgery or resuscitation.

8 · Other neurosurgical emergencies

Recognise spinal cord compression (new weakness, sensory level, bladder/bowel symptoms), cauda equina syndrome (saddle sensory change, urinary dysfunction, bilateral sciatica), brain abscess (headache, fever, focal deficit), and acute hydrocephalus (headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness). Seek urgent MRI or CT as indicated and immediate specialty advice; do not delay referral while awaiting routine tests.

9 · Observation, discharge and safety-netting

Observe with documented GCS components, pupils, vital signs and limb movements; any deterioration triggers repeat ABCDE and urgent review. Discharge only when clinically appropriate under NICE criteria, with responsible adult supervision where indicated and written/verbal advice on worsening headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, seizure, weakness and return precautions. Consider concussion recovery and return-to-activity guidance.

PLAB decision frameworks

Head injury pathway

<C>ABCDE + cervical spine consideration → GCS E/V/M, pupils, glucose, medication history → apply NICE NG232 CT criteria → urgent CT and neurosurgical discussion when indicated → repeat neuro observations → admission or safe discharge with advice.

CT head within 1 hour (age ≥16)

Initial GCS ≤12; GCS <15 at 2 hours; suspected open/depressed fracture; basal skull fracture signs; post-traumatic seizure; focal deficit; >1 vomiting episode.

CT within 8 hours when LOC/amnesia

Age ≥65; clotting disorder; dangerous mechanism; >30 min retrograde amnesia. If presenting more than 8 hours after injury, CT within 1 hour.

Anticoagulation caveat

Consider CT for anticoagulants or antiplatelets other than aspirin alone, even if no other CT trigger; consider within 8 hours or 1 hour for late presenters.

Neurological deterioration

Repeat ABCDE, prevent hypoxia/hypotension, check pupils/GCS/glucose → urgent senior/anaesthetic/neurosurgical help → urgent imaging or intervention according to stability and specialist advice.

CT pattern memory aid

Extradural = lens/biconvex; subdural = crescent; subarachnoid = sulci/cisterns; contusion = intraparenchymal focal injury.

Four interactive-style clinical decision cases

Case 1 — Older adult taking apixaban

A 78-year-old falls, hits her head, GCS 15, no vomiting or LOC, taking apixaban. Decision: assess fully and consider CT head within 8 hours under NICE anticoagulant guidance even without other CT indications; discuss risk, observation and discharge plan.

Case 2 — Lucid interval

A 22-year-old has temporal trauma, initially speaks normally then becomes drowsy with unequal pupils. Decision: suspect expanding intracranial haematoma, including extradural bleed; resuscitate, urgent CT when safe and immediate neurosurgical escalation.

Case 3 — Elderly person with delayed confusion

A 76-year-old has worsening headache and confusion days after a fall. CT shows a crescent collection. Decision: suspect subdural haematoma; review anticoagulants, seek neurosurgical advice and monitor deterioration.

Case 4 — Severe TBI

A 30-year-old after motorcycle collision has GCS E1 V2 M4, vomiting and hypoxia. Decision: call trauma/anaesthetic team for airway protection, protect cervical spine, correct hypoxia/hypotension, urgent CT and neurosurgical discussion.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Head injury and urgent escalation

Station: A 68-year-old taking warfarin fell and struck their head 2 hours ago. They are now confused with headache.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, assess immediate safety and call senior help.
  2. Perform ABCDE, check vital signs, glucose, GCS components, pupils and focal neurology; consider cervical spine injury.
  3. Ask about mechanism, time, loss of consciousness, amnesia, vomiting, seizure, anticoagulants, other medication and baseline function.
  4. Recognise urgent CT need if GCS is reduced or another 1-hour criterion is present; anticoagulation alone warrants consideration of CT under NICE.
  5. Arrange appropriate CT urgently, FBC/coagulation and group-and-save as indicated; seek neurosurgical advice if bleed or deterioration suspected.
  6. Explain simply: “A head injury can cause bleeding around the brain. Because of your symptoms and blood thinner, we need urgent assessment and a scan.”
  7. Discuss anticoagulant reversal with senior clinicians if intracranial haemorrhage is suspected/confirmed; document and hand over.
  8. Continue neurological observations, communicate with family appropriately, and provide safety-netting only if eventual discharge is safe.

Examiner focus: patient safety, NICE CT timing, anticoagulant awareness, GCS documentation, early escalation and clear communication.

Rapid revision: 12 essentials

  1. Use <C>ABCDE and prevent hypoxia/hypotension.
  2. Record GCS as eye, verbal and motor components, not only a total.
  3. CT within 1 hour for key adult NICE red flags including seizure, focal deficit, skull fracture signs, reduced GCS and repeated vomiting.
  4. LOC/amnesia plus age ≥65, clotting disorder, dangerous mechanism or >30 min retrograde amnesia may trigger 8-hour CT.
  5. Anticoagulants or antiplatelets other than aspirin alone can warrant CT even without other criteria.
  6. Extradural = biconvex, classically middle meningeal artery.
  7. Subdural = crescent, commonly bridging veins.
  8. Traumatic SAH appears in sulci/cisterns.
  9. Declining GCS or unequal pupils: immediate reassessment and neurosurgical escalation.
  10. GCS ≤8 often requires expert airway protection.
  11. Avoid corticosteroids for traumatic brain injury.
  12. Discharge requires appropriate observation, supervision and written safety-netting.
