End-of-Life Care & Ethical Decision-Making

Objectives: Distinguish DNACPR from ceilings of care; apply consent and capacity principles; approach withholding/withdrawing treatment, advance decisions, symptom relief and family disagreements with empathy and sound reasoning.

1 · The ethical foundations

Autonomy: a capacitous adult can refuse treatment, even life-sustaining treatment. Beneficence and non-maleficence: offer treatment with an overall benefit, weighing burdens against benefits. Justice: make fair, non-discriminatory decisions. Explain uncertainties and involve the patient as much as possible.

2 · DNACPR is specific

A Do Not Attempt Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation decision concerns CPR if cardiac or respiratory arrest occurs. It does not mean 'do not treat', 'no antibiotics', 'no fluids' or 'no admission'. Continue appropriate assessment, comfort, and other treatments according to goals of care. DNACPR decisions require individual clinical assessment, appropriate consultation and clear recording. A patient cannot demand clinically inappropriate CPR, but must be involved and informed where practicable; seek senior review if disputed.

3 · Capacity, advance decisions and proxies

Presume capacity; assess it for the specific decision at the relevant time. In England and Wales, a valid and applicable advance decision to refuse treatment (ADRT) can be binding. An ADRT refusing life-sustaining treatment must meet additional formalities, including being written, signed, witnessed and expressly applying even if life is at risk. A health and welfare lasting power of attorney may have relevant authority if validly appointed and authorised; check its scope. Rules differ in other UK nations.

4 · Best interests and overall benefit

When a patient lacks capacity, follow the applicable legal framework and consider prior wishes, values, beliefs, family and carers' perspectives, likely benefits and burdens, and less restrictive options. Relatives usually provide vital information but are not automatically entitled to decide for the patient. In England and Wales the Mental Capacity Act 2005 uses best interests; GMC guidance also discusses overall benefit.

5 · Withholding and withdrawing treatment

There is no blanket ethical requirement to start or continue a treatment that offers no overall benefit. Withdrawal of an existing intervention can be ethically and legally appropriate after careful assessment, consultation and documentation. Do not confuse withdrawal of burdensome treatment with intentionally causing death. Consider second opinions, senior/palliative care advice and legal advice when serious disagreement persists.

6 · Palliative care

Focus on symptom relief, dignity, psychosocial and spiritual needs, and shared priorities. Proportionate opioids and sedatives may be used for distressing symptoms when clinically indicated and appropriately titrated; the intention is symptom relief, not hastening death. Avoid assuming that all dying patients need the same medication or route.

7 · Communication and documentation

Explore the patient's understanding and preferences, use plain language, allow silence, check understanding, include chosen supporters, and document the clinical reasoning, discussions, decision, review plan and escalation contacts.

PLAB traps: DNACPR ≠ stopping all care; relatives ≠ automatic decision-makers; an unwise refusal ≠ incapacity; and a capacitous refusal must be respected.

End-of-life decision pathway

  1. Recognise: Is the patient deteriorating or nearing end of life? Treat reversible problems and assess urgent needs.
  2. Clarify: What decision is required—CPR, ventilation, nutrition, admission or symptom control?
  3. Capacity: Assess decision-specific capacity and ask the patient about their wishes.
  4. Check authority: Review any applicable advance decision, legally authorised proxy or relevant court decision.
  5. Balance: Consider clinical benefits, burdens, the person's values and overall benefit/best interests where relevant.
  6. Communicate: Explain options, seek senior/MDT input and consult those close to the patient appropriately.
  7. Record and review: Document the rationale, DNACPR status separately from other care plans, and when to reassess.

Disagreement: listen → clarify the dispute → offer second opinion/ethics or mediation support → seek legal advice where needed; do not abandon symptom relief.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · DNACPR and pneumonia

A patient with a DNACPR form develops treatable pneumonia. Best approach: assess and discuss antibiotics and other appropriate treatment; DNACPR alone does not preclude them.

Case 2 · Capacitous refusal

A patient with advanced cancer understands the consequences and refuses further chemotherapy. Best approach: confirm understanding and voluntariness, respect the refusal, and offer symptom support.

Case 3 · Family demands CPR

The team judges CPR will not work; relatives demand 'everything'. Best approach: explain the clinical assessment compassionately, involve a senior and offer review; continue other beneficial care.

Case 4 · Advance decision

An unconscious adult has a documented refusal of ventilation. Best approach: urgently verify validity, applicability and any later contradictory decision or authorised proxy before determining treatment under relevant law.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Discussing DNACPR with family

Station: An elderly patient with advanced heart failure has deteriorated. Their daughter says: 'If you put DNACPR on the chart, you'll stop treating Mum.'

Consultation structure

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm the daughter's relationship and the patient's wishes about sharing information.
  2. Ask what the family understands and what concerns them most.
  3. Explain that DNACPR applies only to CPR at arrest, not to other appropriate treatment or comfort care.
  4. Explain the patient's condition, likely outcome and uncertainty in simple terms.
  5. Clarify the patient's capacity, previously expressed wishes and any relevant advance plan.
  6. Explain how the team will decide and involve the patient, senior clinicians and family appropriately.
  7. Offer a second opinion or senior review if there is disagreement.
  8. Summarise the active care plan, answer questions and document the discussion.
