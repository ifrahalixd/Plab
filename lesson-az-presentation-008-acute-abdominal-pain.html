Acute abdominal pain: from symptom to safe disposition

Acute abdominal pain ranges from benign self-limiting illness to catastrophic bleeding, bowel ischaemia, perforation and ectopic pregnancy. The first task is not naming the disease: it is deciding how unwell the patient is, whether a time-critical cause exists and what must happen before investigations.

1. Pain mechanisms, patterns and anatomy

Visceral pain results from stretching or inflammation of internal organs and tends to be dull, poorly localised and midline. Parietal peritoneal pain is sharper and better localised; guarding and pain on movement suggest peritoneal irritation. Referred pain arises from shared neural pathways (for example, diaphragmatic irritation may cause shoulder-tip pain). Distinguish colicky pain from constant pain, and sudden maximal-onset pain from progressive pain.

2. Focused history: SOCRATES plus discriminators

  1. Start and time course: sudden maximal-onset? migration from umbilicus to RIF? recurrent previous episodes? Duration, progression and relation to meals.
  2. Quality and radiation: colicky waves suggest hollow-organ obstruction; constant progressively worsening pain may suggest inflammation or ischaemia; pain to the back suggests pancreatitis or vascular disease.
  3. Associated symptoms: vomiting (particularly bilious), inability to pass flatus/stool, diarrhoea, melaena, haematemesis, rectal bleeding, fever, urinary symptoms and anorexia.
  4. Gynaecological and obstetric: last menstrual period, pregnancy possibility, contraception, vaginal bleeding/discharge, STI risks and ovarian history; ask sensitively and privately.
  5. Risks: previous abdominal operations (adhesions), AF (embolism), vascular disease/AAA, gallstones, alcohol use, NSAIDs, anticoagulation, hernia, immunosuppression and recent procedures.
  6. Other systems: exertional chest symptoms or diabetes (atypical MI), breathlessness/pneumonia, testicular or scrotal pain, and diabetes-related ketoacidosis symptoms.

3. Examination: assess physiology before the abdomen

Record temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, SpO₂, NEWS2 where used, mental state, perfusion and hydration. Look for pallor, jaundice and distress. Inspect for scars, distension, visible hernias, skin changes and movement with respiration. Auscultate if appropriate, then gently palpate away from maximal pain for localised tenderness, guarding, rigidity, rebound/percussion tenderness and masses. Examine hernial orifices and consider flank tenderness. Rectal, pelvic and testicular examination should be clinically indicated, consented and appropriately chaperoned, rather than routine in everyone. Repeat examination as the case evolves.

4. Red flags — the cannot-miss diagnoses

Emergency: haemodynamic instability; sudden severe pain with collapse; generalised rigidity or peritonitis; suspected AAA rupture; pain out of proportion to findings with AF/vascular risk (mesenteric ischaemia); fever with jaundice and sepsis (cholangitis); obstructed kidney plus infection; ectopic pregnancy or ovarian torsion; bowel obstruction with strangulation signs; acute testicular torsion; DKA; or an older adult with unexplained severe pain. Escalate early to appropriate emergency, surgical, vascular, gynaecological or urological teams.

For a child or pregnant person, ultrasound is often preferred initially when suitable, and imaging must be tailored with senior and radiology advice. In adults with suspected appendicitis and diagnostic uncertainty, CT is frequently useful. Investigations must not delay resuscitation or emergency source control.

6. Initial management and disposition

  1. ABCDE and escalation: treat shock, hypoxia and immediately reversible threats; call senior/specialty help early.
  2. Access, monitoring and samples: IV access and blood tests/cultures when appropriate; crossmatch for bleeding risk. Monitor fluid balance and urine output in severe illness.
  3. Pain and nausea: provide timely, titrated analgesia and antiemetics; reassess response. Do not postpone analgesia solely because the diagnosis is unclear.
  4. Fluids: give crystalloid if indicated, with reassessment; avoid indiscriminate fluid overload (especially in cardiac/renal disease).
  5. Nil by mouth: when emergency surgery, obstruction or aspiration risk is possible, until the team clarifies plan.
  6. Antibiotics: for suspected sepsis or intra-abdominal infection when indicated, guided by local antimicrobial policies; antibiotics are not indicated for every abdominal pain.
  7. Source control: surgery, endoscopy, drainage, embolisation or other intervention may be necessary. Decide with the relevant team.
  8. Reassessment and safety net: repeat vital signs/abdominal examination, document uncertainty, explain warning symptoms, and arrange specific follow-up and pending-result review.

7. Classic PLAB traps

Don't miss: an ectopic pregnancy because contraception is used; AAA because no mass is palpable; mesenteric ischaemia because lactate is normal; perforation because plain X-ray shows no free gas; pancreatitis because CT is not yet abnormal; ACS presenting with epigastric pain; testicular torsion in a patient with lower abdominal pain; and worsening peritonitis despite an earlier reassuring examination. Do not anchor on a positive urine dipstick.

8. Worked cases: five clinical decisions

  1. 19-year-old, migrating central → RIF pain, nausea and focal guarding: suspect appendicitis; assess pregnancy possibility, observations, inflammatory markers and surgical/imaging pathway; consider ectopic or torsion where relevant.
  2. 76-year-old with AF, sudden intense diffuse pain, relatively soft abdomen: suspect acute mesenteric ischaemia; resuscitate, call vascular/general surgery and obtain urgent CTA if stable enough; do not await high lactate.
  3. 31-year-old with missed period, unilateral pelvic pain and dizziness: perform urgent pregnancy test and assess for ectopic pregnancy; shock/collapse mandates emergency gynaecological resuscitation and intervention.
  4. 66-year-old with old laparotomy scar, colicky pain, distension, vomiting, no flatus: suspect adhesive bowel obstruction; IV access, analgesia, NBM, fluids, surgical review and CT if appropriate; escalating constant pain/rigidity means possible strangulation.
  5. 54-year-old with fever, RUQ pain, jaundice and confusion: suspect severe cholangitis; sepsis resuscitation, antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage assessment (often ERCP).

9. PLAB 2: acute abdominal pain station

8-minute objective: introduce yourself, assess immediate safety and obtain consent. Ask onset, severity, location, migration, vomiting/obstipation, fever, urinary and gynaecological symptoms, surgery and vascular risk; review medication, allergies and pregnancy possibility. Explain you will check observations, examine sensitively with chaperone as indicated and arrange appropriate tests. State red flags and escalation clearly. Communicate a provisional working diagnosis and important alternatives, not unfounded certainty. Summarise the immediate plan and invite questions.

Five decision-making axes

  1. Unstable versus stable: address shock, sepsis, bleeding and peritonitis first.
  2. Sudden versus gradual: abrupt onset raises vascular, perforation and torsion causes.
  3. Colicky versus constant: obstruction or stone versus inflammation/vascular injury; patterns overlap.
  4. Pregnancy possible? Test early and consider ectopic even when there is contraception.
  5. Extra-abdominal sources: MI, DKA, lower-lobe pneumonia and testicular emergencies.
Safety: rigid abdomen, pain out of proportion, sudden collapse, septic shock, pregnancy-related pain with instability or an acutely painful testis warrants urgent escalation. A normal test alone must not override clinical concern.

Deep Dive: why acute abdominal pain can be deceptive

1. Visceral versus somatic innervation

Visceral afferents respond to distension, traction, ischaemia and inflammation. Their broad spinal convergence causes midline poorly localised pain. Irritation of parietal peritoneum recruits somatic nerves, giving sharper localised pain and involuntary guarding. Appendicitis classically begins near the umbilicus then localises in the RIF when parietal peritoneum becomes involved; not all patients follow this sequence.

2. Referred pain and shared pathways

Diaphragmatic irritation can produce shoulder pain via C3–C5 pathways. Biliary pain may radiate to the right shoulder or scapula. Pancreatic inflammation often radiates to the back. Location alone does not establish diagnosis and may mislead.

3. Why bowel ischaemia has deceptively few signs

Early intestinal hypoperfusion stimulates intense visceral pain before inflammation reaches parietal peritoneum; examination may still be soft. The bowel may progress to infarction, peritonitis, septic shock and multiorgan failure. Lactate rises inconsistently and may be normal early; CTA and urgent specialist involvement are critical when suspected.

4. Visceral inflammation and peritonitis

Inflammation increases tissue pressure and cytokine signalling. Once visceral perforation leaks luminal contents into the peritoneum, chemical then bacterial peritonitis can cause guarding, rigidity and septic deterioration. Absence of visible free air on plain X-ray cannot reliably exclude perforation.

5. Obstruction and strangulation

Proximal bowel distension triggers peristaltic colic and vomiting; distal blockage can cause marked distension and obstipation. In a closed loop or strangulated hernia, venous congestion precedes impaired arterial inflow, leading to bowel oedema, ischaemia and necrosis. Constant worsening pain, fever, tachycardia or guarding are serious warning signs.

6. Peritoneal signs are imperfect

Older, immunosuppressed or pregnant patients may have less typical tenderness. Analgesia should be given while evaluation continues. Serial observations and repeated examination often reveal evolution missed during a single initial assessment.

7. Pancreatitis: pancreatic autodigestion and systemic injury

Premature digestive-enzyme activation triggers pancreatic inflammation and systemic cytokine release. Hypovolaemia, AKI, ARDS and organ failure can follow severe disease. NICE NG104 supports enteral nutrition in severe or moderately severe pancreatitis; do not routinely rely on early CT to grade severity if diagnosis is clear and the patient is responding.

8. Diagnostic probabilities and cognitive bias

The location-based differential produces hypotheses; the patient’s risk, history and physiology modify them. A positive urine dipstick can coexist with appendicitis, normal inflammatory markers can occur early and a previous functional diagnosis does not exclude a new emergency. Reassess actively rather than anchoring on the first convenient result.
