Head & Neck Cancer

PLAB red flags: persistent oral ulcer, unexplained neck lump, persistent hoarseness, progressive dysphagia or stridor.

Overview

Head and neck cancer includes malignancies of the oral cavity, oropharynx, nasopharynx, larynx, hypopharynx, salivary glands and other head and neck structures. Most mucosal cancers are squamous cell carcinomas.

Risk factors

Tobacco and alcohol increase risk, particularly together. HPV is associated with many oropharyngeal cancers; Epstein–Barr virus is linked with nasopharyngeal carcinoma. Risk is not excluded in non-smokers.

Symptoms that matter

Persistent unexplained hoarseness, a non-healing mouth ulcer, unexplained neck lump, persistent dysphagia, odynophagia, unilateral ear pain with normal otoscopy, or persistent unilateral nasal symptoms need evaluation.

Oral cancer examination

Inspect lips, tongue (including lateral borders), floor of mouth, gingiva, palate and buccal mucosa. Palpate suspicious lesions and examine cervical lymph nodes. Document ulceration, induration and fixation.

NICE oral referral

NICE NG12 recommends considering a suspected cancer pathway referral for unexplained oral ulceration lasting more than 3 weeks or a persistent unexplained neck lump; a red/red-white oral patch warrants urgent dental assessment for possible suspected cancer pathway referral.

Laryngeal cancer

Persistent unexplained hoarseness is a key symptom. NICE NG12 advises considering suspected cancer pathway referral in people aged 45 and over with persistent unexplained hoarseness or an unexplained neck lump.

Oropharyngeal cancer

Tonsillar or base-of-tongue cancer can present with dysphagia, odynophagia, referred otalgia or a painless cervical node. HPV-related cancers may present with a neck mass.

Nasopharyngeal cancer

Possible features include persistent unilateral nasal obstruction, epistaxis, neck nodes, unilateral middle-ear effusion in an adult or cranial neuropathy. Urgent specialist evaluation is important.

Neck lump approach

Ask about duration, growth, pain, infections, smoking, alcohol, weight loss and B symptoms. Examine all cervical nodal levels, salivary glands, oral cavity, pharynx, skin and thyroid.

Urgent airway red flags

Stridor, respiratory distress, rapidly progressive swelling, inability to swallow secretions or significant bleeding require immediate emergency assessment and senior airway support.

Initial investigations

ENT assessment may include flexible nasendoscopy, ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration/core biopsy of nodes, biopsy of a primary lesion and CT/MRI imaging. Do not delay referral while arranging nonessential tests.

Histology and staging

Tissue diagnosis confirms tumour type. TNM staging assesses tumour extent, nodal disease and metastasis; multidisciplinary teams use this with imaging and patient factors to plan care.

Treatment overview

Treatment may involve surgery, radiotherapy, systemic therapy or combinations depending on site and stage. Supportive care includes speech/swallow therapy, dietetics, dental care and smoking cessation.

Treatment complications

Radiotherapy may cause mucositis, xerostomia, dental problems, dysphagia and hypothyroidism after neck irradiation. Surgery may affect voice, swallowing or appearance.

Communication and safety net

Explain that most symptoms do not mean cancer but persistent or unexplained features need prompt assessment. Avoid premature reassurance; explain referral urgency and what to do if symptoms worsen.

PLAB 2 OSCE structure

Take a focused history, assess smoking/alcohol and red flags, examine mouth and neck with consent, explain the need for urgent specialist assessment without declaring a diagnosis, and address anxiety sensitively.

PLAB 2 — suspected cancer consultation

Ask permission to discuss symptoms and concerns. Explore duration, progression, tobacco/alcohol, swallowing, voice, pain, weight loss and neck lumps. Explain that the symptoms need urgent specialist assessment but do not prove cancer. Offer clear follow-up and emergency safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Why a neck node can be the first sign

Oropharyngeal primaries can be small or difficult to see, while metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy is noticeable.

Referred ear pain

Sensory nerve pathways shared by the ear and pharynx/larynx can cause otalgia despite a normal ear examination.

HPV and oropharynx

HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer differs in epidemiology and biology from many tobacco-associated cancers; diagnosis still requires tissue assessment.

Airway priority

Stridor and inability to manage secretions suggest upper airway compromise; airway stabilisation precedes routine work-up.

Why histology matters

Symptoms and imaging identify suspicious lesions, but tissue assessment defines tumour type and informs treatment.

Survivorship

Long-term follow-up addresses speech, swallowing, nutrition, dental health, dry mouth, thyroid dysfunction and psychological effects.
