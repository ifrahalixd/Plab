Acute Mesenteric Ischaemia & Ischaemic Bowel

Surgery · Lesson 04 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Acute mesenteric ischaemia: a time-critical surgical emergency

PLAB priority: Severe abdominal pain disproportionate to examination, especially with atrial fibrillation or vascular disease, requires immediate senior surgical/vascular input and urgent CT angiography when feasible. A normal lactate does not exclude early disease.

1 · What is mesenteric ischaemia?

Inadequate intestinal blood flow causes mucosal injury, infarction, perforation, sepsis and death if not rapidly treated. Main mechanisms are arterial embolism (often atrial fibrillation), arterial thrombosis (atherosclerosis), non-occlusive mesenteric ischaemia (shock, low flow, vasoconstriction), and mesenteric venous thrombosis (hypercoagulable states, inflammation).

3 · Initial emergency assessment

  1. Perform ABCDE, continuous observations, IV access, oxygen if indicated, prompt analgesia and urgent senior general/vascular surgical review.
  2. Keep nil by mouth; give appropriate IV crystalloid guided by haemodynamics and reassessment; correct electrolytes. Consider urinary catheter to monitor output in critically ill patients.
  3. Send FBC, U&Es, CRP, coagulation, glucose, blood gas/lactate, group and save/crossmatch. Blood tests support assessment but cannot rule out ischaemia.
  4. Request urgent multiphase CT angiography of abdomen/pelvis when feasible; do not wait for a lactate rise. Discuss contrast concerns with senior radiology/surgical teams rather than allowing avoidable delay.
  5. Start broad-spectrum IV antibiotics when bowel infarction, sepsis or bacterial translocation is suspected, according to local protocol. Consider therapeutic anticoagulation when appropriate under specialist direction, balancing bleeding risk and operative plans.

4 · Definitive treatment

Arterial occlusion: urgent revascularisation (endovascular or open) when feasible, with bowel viability assessment and resection of non-viable bowel. Peritonitis or perforation: urgent laparotomy and source control. Mesenteric venous thrombosis: anticoagulation is central in suitable patients; surgery if infarction/peritonitis. Non-occlusive ischaemia: restore perfusion, treat shock and reduce contributing vasoconstriction where possible; assess for infarction.

5 · Important differential: ischaemic colitis

Ischaemic colitis often presents with crampy abdominal pain followed by rectal bleeding, frequently in older adults after hypotension. It is often less catastrophic than acute small-bowel mesenteric ischaemia but severe disease with peritonitis, gangrene or haemodynamic instability needs urgent surgery.

Exam traps: Normal lactate, normal early abdominal examination and absence of bloody stool do not exclude acute mesenteric ischaemia. Do not mistake it for simple gastroenteritis in a high-risk patient.

Decision pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Suspected acute mesenteric ischaemia

Sudden severe pain ± AF/vascular disease → ABCDE + analgesia + IV access + bloods → immediate senior surgical/vascular discussion → urgent CT angiography when feasible → specialist revascularisation/operation or anticoagulation according to mechanism.

Pathway B — Signs of infarction

Guarding/rigidity, shock, perforation or deteriorating organ function → resuscitate, broad-spectrum antibiotics when indicated, urgent theatre assessment; imaging must not delay life-saving intervention in an unstable patient.

Slide 1 — Four mechanisms

Embolic arterial occlusion · arterial thrombosis · non-occlusive low-flow ischaemia · mesenteric venous thrombosis.

Slide 2 — Diagnostic rule

CT angiography is the key imaging investigation in suspected acute mesenteric ischaemia. Serum lactate is neither sufficiently sensitive nor specific to exclude it.

Slide 3 — Treatment priorities

Restore perfusion, prevent progression, treat sepsis if present, assess bowel viability, remove non-viable bowel and monitor closely.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Embolic arterial ischaemia

A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation has abrupt, excruciating abdominal pain but minimal tenderness. Plan: immediate resuscitation, analgesia, urgent surgical/vascular involvement and CT angiography; consider revascularisation with specialist team.

Case 2 — Infarcted bowel

A 69-year-old with severe pain develops guarding, hypotension and rising lactate. Plan: manage shock, urgent theatre decision and broad-spectrum antibiotics if sepsis/infarction suspected; do not delay surgery for routine testing.

Case 3 — Mesenteric venous thrombosis

A 43-year-old with a thrombophilic disorder has progressive abdominal pain. CT shows mesenteric venous thrombosis without peritonitis. Plan: urgent specialist review, anticoagulation if appropriate, monitor for deterioration; surgery if infarction develops.

Case 4 — Ischaemic colitis

A 79-year-old develops cramping left-sided pain and bloody diarrhoea after an episode of hypotension. Plan: assess stability, investigate and obtain specialist review; recognise peritonitis or shock as signs of severe disease.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute severe abdominal pain station

Brief: You are an FY2 in ED. A 76-year-old with atrial fibrillation reports sudden severe abdominal pain. Take a focused history, identify danger signs and explain your immediate plan.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, check identity, consent and pain; call for help if unstable.
  2. ABCDE, full observations, perfusion and sepsis/shock assessment.
  3. Ask about onset, severity, radiation, vomiting, stool, bleeding and prior similar episodes.
  4. Ask about AF, anticoagulants, vascular disease, smoking, hypotension and thrombosis history.
  5. Examine abdomen for tenderness/peritonism and assess cardiovascular status.
  6. Explain IV access, blood tests, analgesia, nil by mouth and urgent CT angiography.
  7. Arrange immediate senior surgical/vascular input and explain possibility of urgent intervention.
  8. Reassess frequently, communicate empathetically and use structured SBAR handover.

Patient explanation: “I am worried that the blood supply to part of your bowel may be reduced. This is an emergency. We will treat your pain and circulation while urgently arranging scans and specialist review.”

Rapid revision & reference resources

  1. Pain out of proportion + AF = think acute mesenteric ischaemia.
  2. Normal lactate does not rule out early disease.
  3. Urgent CT angiography is the principal diagnostic imaging test when feasible.
  4. Peritonitis or shock suggests advanced disease: resuscitate and escalate immediately.
  5. Arterial occlusion may need revascularisation; venous thrombosis usually requires anticoagulation if suitable.
  6. Ischaemic colitis often causes pain and bloody diarrhoea; severe cases may need surgery.
