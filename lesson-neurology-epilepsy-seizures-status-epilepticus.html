Epilepsy, Seizures & Status Epilepticus

PLAB priority: A convulsive seizure lasting 5 minutes or more is an emergency. ABCDE, glucose, prompt benzodiazepine and escalation.

01. Seizure versus epilepsy

A seizure is a transient episode of abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity. Epilepsy is a tendency to recurrent unprovoked seizures; one seizure does not always mean epilepsy.

PLAB application: Distinguish an acute symptomatic seizure from an unprovoked seizure.

02. First seizure: immediate assessment

Use ABCDE, protect from injury, time the event, check capillary glucose and vital signs. Treat hypoglycaemia and assess for head injury, infection or toxic causes.

PLAB application: Do not restrain the patient or put anything in their mouth.

03. Focused witness history

Ask about onset, aura, focal movements, loss of awareness, tonic–clonic activity, tongue biting, incontinence, duration, recovery, triggers and previous events. Obtain an eyewitness or video account if available.

PLAB application: Postictal confusion and lateral tongue biting support epileptic seizure but are not definitive.

04. Differential diagnosis

Consider syncope, cardiac arrhythmia, psychogenic non-epileptic seizures, migraine, sleep disorders, movement disorders and metabolic disturbance.

PLAB application: A 12-lead ECG is important after a first suspected seizure to identify cardiac mimics.

05. Seizure classification

Focal seizures start in one hemisphere and may preserve or impair awareness; focal-to-bilateral tonic–clonic seizures spread. Generalised seizures include absence, myoclonic, tonic–clonic and atonic types.

PLAB application: Accurate seizure type guides antiseizure medicine choice.

06. Investigations after a first seizure

Check glucose, electrolytes and other blood tests as clinically indicated; obtain ECG. Specialist assessment may include EEG and MRI with an epilepsy protocol when indicated.

PLAB application: A normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy; do not use EEG to rule it out.

07. Referral and diagnosis

Arrange urgent assessment by a clinician with expertise in first seizures, ideally within 2 weeks, after a first suspected seizure. Diagnose clinically using history, witness information and investigations.

PLAB application: Provide interim safety advice while awaiting specialist review.

08. Status epilepticus recognition

Convulsive status epilepticus is a seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, or recurrent seizures without recovery between them. This is a medical emergency.

PLAB application: Call for emergency help at 5 minutes; do not wait for 30 minutes.

09. Status epilepticus: initial management

ABCDE, oxygen if needed, airway positioning, IV access, glucose, ECG monitoring, look for reversible causes. Give a benzodiazepine promptly according to local emergency protocol; buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam may be used in community plans.

PLAB application: Time doses and monitor respiration; repeated benzodiazepines can depress breathing.

10. Ongoing convulsive status

If seizures continue after recommended benzodiazepine doses, urgent hospital treatment uses IV antiseizure medication such as levetiracetam, phenytoin or sodium valproate according to protocol and patient factors. Refractory status requires anaesthetic/ICU support.

PLAB application: Escalate early to senior emergency, neurology and critical-care teams.

11. Long-term antiseizure treatment

Choice depends on seizure syndrome, age, sex, pregnancy potential, comorbidities, interactions and preferences. NICE NG217 includes lamotrigine or levetiracetam options for focal seizures and syndrome-specific choices for generalised seizures.

PLAB application: Avoid choosing a medicine before classifying the seizure and reviewing reproductive risks.

12. Valproate reproductive safety

Valproate has substantial risks in pregnancy and important UK restrictions for initiation and use. Follow current MHRA safeguards, specialist review and pregnancy prevention requirements when applicable.

PLAB application: Never abruptly stop established medication without urgent specialist advice.

13. Breakthrough seizures and adherence

Ask about missed doses, sleep deprivation, illness, alcohol, recreational drugs, interactions and new neurological symptoms. Review medicine levels when clinically appropriate.

PLAB application: Avoid abrupt antiseizure medicine withdrawal.

14. Driving, work and lifestyle

Explain DVLA rules: after a first unprovoked seizure or epilepsy diagnosis, driving must stop and the DVLA must be notified; licence eligibility depends on the exact situation. Discuss bathing, swimming, heights, machinery and sleep.

PLAB application: Do not invent a universal driving ban duration; check current DVLA guidance.

15. Pregnancy and contraception

Preconception specialist review is essential. Antiseizure medicines can affect fetal risk and some hormonal contraception; folic acid and medicine optimisation should be individualised.

PLAB application: Do not stop antiseizure medication suddenly during pregnancy.

16. Safety-netting and SUDEP

Teach family seizure first aid, rescue-medication plans when prescribed, when to call emergency services and the importance of adherence. Discuss sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) sensitively and ways to reduce risk.

PLAB application: Call emergency services for a seizure lasting 5 minutes, repeated seizures, injury, pregnancy or breathing problems.

Deep Dive

Electrical basis of seizures

An imbalance of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal networks can produce synchronised abnormal firing. Focal and generalised onset describe networks involved at seizure onset.

Seizure versus syncope

Syncope may have brief jerks. A detailed prodrome, duration, recovery, witness description and ECG are more useful than any single symptom.

Why 5 minutes matters

Long convulsions become less likely to stop spontaneously and increase risks of hypoxia, aspiration and neuronal injury. Prompt protocol-based therapy improves outcomes.

EEG and MRI roles

EEG supports epilepsy classification; a normal EEG cannot exclude epilepsy. MRI may identify a structural cause and is requested according to syndrome and specialist assessment.

Medication and reproductive risk

Antiseizure drugs have syndrome-specific benefits and adverse effects. Valproate requires particular safeguards; other agents also need pregnancy and contraception review.

PLAB 2 communication

Use clear language, acknowledge uncertainty after a first seizure, invite questions, provide a written safety plan and document driving and emergency advice.
