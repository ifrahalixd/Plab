Arrhythmias · Clinical deep dive

Understand the electrophysiology behind bedside decisions.

1 · Cardiac conduction anatomy

The sinoatrial node initiates atrial depolarisation. The AV node delays conduction to allow ventricular filling; His bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje fibres rapidly distribute the impulse. Accessory pathways bypass the AV node and can enable re-entry.

2 · Action potentials and ion channels

Fast-response atrial/ventricular myocytes depolarise mainly through sodium influx (phase 0), plateau through calcium entry (phase 2), and repolarise through potassium currents (phase 3). SA/AV nodal tissue depends more on calcium currents and spontaneous pacemaker depolarisation. Potassium, calcium and magnesium disturbances affect automaticity and repolarisation.

3 · Three mechanisms

Re-entry: a circulating impulse sustains AVNRT, AVRT and some VT. Enhanced automaticity: ectopic pacemakers discharge rapidly. Triggered activity: early afterdepolarisations contribute to torsades in prolonged QT.

4 · Why adenosine works

Adenosine transiently suppresses AV nodal conduction, interrupting AV-node-dependent re-entry. Its extremely short duration explains rapid onset and offset, but transient flushing, chest discomfort and bronchospasm risk require careful selection and monitoring.

5 · Beta-blockers and calcium-channel blockers

Beta-blockers reduce sympathetic drive and AV nodal conduction. Verapamil/diltiazem inhibit L-type calcium channels and slow AV nodal conduction; avoid in HFrEF and pre-excited AF. Antiarrhythmic drugs have proarrhythmic risks and should be chosen with specialist guidance.

6 · AF and clot formation

Loss of organised atrial contraction promotes stasis, particularly in the left atrial appendage, raising embolic stroke risk. Anticoagulants target coagulation; rate control alone does not eliminate the need for stroke-risk assessment.

7 · QT prolongation and torsades

Delayed ventricular repolarisation predisposes to early afterdepolarisations and polymorphic VT. Low potassium/magnesium, congenital channel variants and QT-prolonging drugs increase risk. Check QTc rather than raw QT when possible.

8 · PLAB 2 communication

Explain that an abnormal rhythm may cause palpitations, dizziness or breathlessness. For AF, discuss stroke prevention and bleeding risk in understandable terms; check patient preferences and give clear safety-net advice for syncope, chest pain and severe breathlessness.

Arrhythmias are disturbances of cardiac rhythm. In PLAB and clinical practice, the first decision is not the rhythm name: it is whether the patient has a pulse and whether they are unstable.

In 60 seconds

  • Assess ABCDE, pulse, blood pressure, consciousness and signs of shock. Treat the patient, not just the ECG.
  • Cardiac arrest: start CPR and follow the current Resuscitation Council UK ALS algorithm; VF and pulseless VT are shockable.
  • Unstable tachyarrhythmia with a pulse: urgent synchronised cardioversion by a trained team, with sedation where feasible without delaying lifesaving care.
  • Irregularly irregular rhythm: think atrial fibrillation; assess stroke risk and anticoagulation.
  • Broad-complex tachycardia: treat as VT until specialist assessment establishes otherwise.
  • Symptomatic bradycardia: assess adverse features, reversible causes and the need for atropine or pacing under ALS guidance.

1. First assessment: stable or unstable?

Perform ABCDE, check a central pulse if collapse is suspected, record vital signs, establish IV access when appropriate and obtain cardiac monitoring and a 12-lead ECG without delaying resuscitation. Ask about chest pain, breathlessness, syncope, palpitations, onset, drugs, stimulants and previous cardiac disease.

Unresponsive and not breathing normally?

Call the resuscitation team, start CPR and attach a defibrillator. VF/pulseless VT follows the shockable cardiac-arrest pathway; asystole/PEA follows the non-shockable pathway. Follow current ALS guidance.

Pulse present but adverse features?

Look for shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia and severe heart failure. Escalate immediately; unstable tachyarrhythmia generally needs synchronised electrical cardioversion. Do not delay for routine blood tests.

Stable?

Assess rate, regularity and QRS width; obtain ECG and look for reversible causes such as hypoxia, electrolyte disturbance, sepsis, thyroid disease, medication effects and ischaemia.

Safety: Do not use carotid sinus massage casually. Avoid AV-nodal blocking drugs in pre-excited atrial fibrillation (e.g., irregular wide-complex tachycardia with WPW), as ventricular fibrillation can result. Seek emergency specialist advice.

2. Read the ECG systematically

Check calibration and artefact. Determine heart rate, regularity, presence and relationship of P waves, PR interval, QRS width and QT interval. Narrow QRS is usually less than 120 ms; broad QRS is 120 ms or more. A regular narrow-complex tachycardia often represents SVT, but rhythm diagnosis requires full assessment.

3. Atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter

AF is disorganised atrial electrical activity producing an irregular ventricular rhythm. Symptoms include palpitations, fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness and sometimes stroke. AF can be asymptomatic. Confirm with a 12-lead ECG (or appropriately interpreted ambulatory ECG), and assess precipitants, structural disease and comorbidities.

Investigations: FBC, U&Es including potassium, thyroid function, and other tests guided by symptoms; echocardiography when it will inform management. Consider ambulatory monitoring for suspected paroxysmal AF not captured on ECG.

Stroke prevention: NICE NG196

Use CHA₂DS₂-VASc to estimate stroke risk in AF and ORBIT to assess bleeding risk. NICE recommends offering a direct-acting oral anticoagulant (DOAC) for eligible people with AF and a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 2 or more, taking bleeding risk into account. Consider a DOAC for men with a score of 1; decisions are individualised. Do not use aspirin alone for stroke prevention in AF. Mechanical heart valves and moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis require different anticoagulation decisions, usually involving warfarin and specialist care.

CHA₂DS₂-VASc: C = heart failure (1), H = hypertension (1), A₂ = age ≥75 (2), D = diabetes (1), S₂ = prior stroke/TIA/systemic embolism (2), V = vascular disease (1), A = age 65–74 (1), Sc = female sex (1). Female sex alone does not justify anticoagulation.

Rate control versus rhythm control

For many stable patients, rate control is an appropriate initial approach. NICE generally recommends a standard beta-blocker (other than sotalol) or rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker (diltiazem/verapamil), selected according to clinical circumstances. Avoid verapamil/diltiazem in HFrEF. Digoxin may be considered in selected sedentary people or when other options are unsuitable. Rhythm control (electrical or pharmacological cardioversion, antiarrhythmics or ablation) may be considered for symptoms, recent onset or other indications with cardiology input.

Cardioversion carries thromboembolic risk; timing of AF, anticoagulation status and possible left atrial thrombus matter. Do not assume a newly discovered episode is safe to cardiovert without a specialist plan.

Atrial flutter

Typical flutter may show sawtooth atrial activity (often in inferior leads), frequently with ventricular rate near 150/min with 2:1 conduction. Stroke-risk assessment and anticoagulation principles are similar to AF. Catheter ablation can be highly effective for typical flutter.

4. Regular narrow-complex tachycardia / SVT

AVNRT and AVRT often cause sudden-onset, regular palpitations. In a stable patient with a regular narrow-complex tachycardia, a modified Valsalva manoeuvre can terminate AV-node-dependent SVT. If unsuccessful, adenosine may be used by a trained team with ECG monitoring and resuscitation equipment, following current ALS guidance and checking contraindications (including relevant asthma/bronchospasm history and drug interactions).

Adenosine briefly blocks AV nodal conduction and may reveal flutter waves. It is not a general treatment for irregular wide-complex tachycardia. Recurrent symptomatic SVT may warrant electrophysiology assessment and catheter ablation.

5. Ventricular tachycardia, VF and torsades

Monomorphic VT is a regular broad-complex rhythm arising from the ventricles, often in structural heart disease or after MI. If there is no pulse, treat as cardiac arrest (shockable). If a pulse is present and the patient is unstable, arrange immediate synchronised cardioversion. Stable broad-complex tachycardia still requires urgent expert assessment and protocol-based treatment.

Ventricular fibrillation causes cardiac arrest: immediate CPR and defibrillation are essential. Torsades de pointes is polymorphic VT associated with prolonged QT; identify and correct low potassium/magnesium and QT-prolonging drugs, use IV magnesium and specialist/ALS management as appropriate. Pulseless torsades is managed as a shockable arrest.

Exam trap: Defibrillation (unsynchronised shock) is for VF/pulseless VT and some exceptional emergencies. Synchronised cardioversion is used for many unstable tachyarrhythmias with a pulse. Follow the current ALS algorithm and local equipment protocol.

6. Bradycardia and AV block

Sinus bradycardia can be physiological (e.g., sleep or athletes) or due to medication, hypothermia, myocardial ischaemia or conduction disease. Assess symptoms and adverse features rather than treating a number alone.

7. Other important rhythms and causes

Wolff–Parkinson–White (WPW)

An accessory pathway may produce short PR, delta wave and broad QRS in sinus rhythm. Pre-excited AF may be irregular, very rapid and broad; AV-nodal blockers can be dangerous. Seek emergency expert management.

Long QT and inherited arrhythmia syndromes

Congenital channelopathies and acquired causes (drugs, electrolyte abnormalities) predispose to torsades and syncope. Review family history of sudden death, medication list and electrolytes; refer suspected inherited disease for specialist assessment.

Ectopic beats

Premature atrial or ventricular complexes are common. Evaluate frequency, symptoms, structural disease and red flags such as syncope or exertional symptoms; reassurance may be appropriate after assessment in low-risk cases.

8. Worked clinical cases

Case A — 78-year-old with AF, hypertension and previous TIA

Calculate CHA₂DS₂-VASc (age ≥75 = 2; hypertension = 1; previous TIA = 2; plus other applicable factors). Stroke risk is high; assess bleeding risk and offer appropriate anticoagulation after checking contraindications, renal function and shared preferences. Aspirin alone is not adequate AF stroke prevention.

Case B — sudden regular narrow tachycardia, BP 125/80

ABCDE, monitor, ECG. If stable and rhythm is regular narrow-complex SVT, try modified Valsalva; consider adenosine with appropriate monitoring and contraindication checks if unsuccessful.

Case C — broad-complex tachycardia, BP 70/40, chest pain

Unstable tachyarrhythmia with a pulse: call for immediate senior/resuscitation help and synchronised cardioversion. If pulse is lost, switch to cardiac-arrest algorithm.

Case D — recurrent syncope, HR 32, AV dissociation

Suspect complete heart block. Assess perfusion and adverse features, monitor, treat reversible causes and obtain urgent pacing/cardiology support according to ALS guidance.

9. PLAB 1 and PLAB 2 priorities

In PLAB 1, identify instability before choosing medication. Know ECG patterns, AF stroke prevention, AV block, and shockable versus non-shockable cardiac arrest. In PLAB 2, explain the ECG findings in plain language, discuss benefits and risks of anticoagulation, check bleeding and medication history, and use structured escalation and handover.
