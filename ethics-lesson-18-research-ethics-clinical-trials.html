Research Ethics, Clinical Trials & Evidence-Based Medicine

PLAB core: Research must have scientific and social value, a sound design, fair participant selection, proportionate risk, valid consent and independent oversight. Patient care takes priority over recruitment targets.

1 · Core ethical principles

Autonomy: respect voluntary informed decisions. Beneficence and non-maleficence: maximise potential benefits and minimise foreseeable harm. Justice: select participants fairly and avoid exploitation. Integrity: report findings accurately and disclose conflicts.

2 · Research versus audit and service evaluation

Research aims to generate new, transferable or generalisable knowledge. Clinical audit measures care against an agreed standard. Service evaluation assesses how an existing service performs. Classification depends on actual design; use the UK Health Research Authority (HRA) decision tools and local research governance teams.

3 · Ethics review and approvals

UK research may require an NHS Research Ethics Committee (REC) favourable opinion, HRA/HCRW approvals and site-level arrangements; requirements vary by project and nation. Clinical trials of investigational medicinal products (CTIMPs) require appropriate regulatory authorisation and ethics review. Do not assume every audit needs REC approval or every research project has identical approvals.

4 · Consent for participation

Give clear information about purpose, procedures, randomisation, foreseeable risks and benefits, alternatives, confidentiality, compensation arrangements where relevant, and the right to decline or withdraw without losing routine care. Allow questions and time. A signed form alone is not adequate consent.

5 · Participants needing additional safeguards

Children, adults lacking capacity and people in dependent relationships may require specific legal safeguards and protections from undue influence. In England and Wales, research involving adults lacking capacity has a distinct Mental Capacity Act framework for qualifying non-CTIMP research; CTIMPs have separate rules. Never assume a relative can simply provide ordinary proxy consent for every study.

6 · Trials, bias and evidence

Randomisation reduces selection bias; allocation concealment prevents prediction of assignment; blinding limits performance or detection bias. Intention-to-treat analysis preserves randomised group allocation. Assess effect sizes, confidence intervals, harms, applicability and absolute risk rather than relying only on p-values.

7 · Research misconduct and conflicts

Fabrication invents data; falsification manipulates data or methods; plagiarism misappropriates others’ work. Selective reporting and undisclosed financial interests undermine trust. Raise concerns using appropriate governance channels, preserve evidence and protect participants.

Research decision pathways

A · Before beginning a project

  1. Define the question and whether activity is research, audit or evaluation.
  2. Confirm scientific quality, participant risk and data protection arrangements.
  3. Check HRA/local governance, REC and regulatory requirements.
  4. Obtain all necessary approvals before recruitment or data collection.
  5. Train the team, plan monitoring and adverse-event reporting.

B · Consent conversation

  1. Check eligibility and any special safeguards.
  2. Explain purpose, procedures, randomisation, risks and alternatives.
  3. Confirm voluntary choice and right to withdraw.
  4. Use appropriate interpreters/accessibility support and assess understanding.
  5. Document consent and provide a copy of information.

C · Safety concern during trial

  1. Prioritise immediate clinical care.
  2. Inform investigator and follow protocol for safety reporting.
  3. Consider pausing intervention where appropriate under protocol.
  4. Record facts and notify oversight bodies as required.
  5. Keep participant informed and document follow-up.

D · Appraise a trial

  1. Ask whether the question and comparison are relevant.
  2. Assess randomisation, concealment, blinding and attrition.
  3. Compare absolute benefits and harms with uncertainty.
  4. Consider generalisability and patient preferences.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Recruitment pressure

A junior doctor is told that ward funding depends on enrolling patients in a trial.

Best response: explain participation is voluntary, separate clinical care from recruitment pressure and raise inappropriate coercion with the research lead.

Case 2 · Withdrawal

A participant wishes to leave a randomised trial after two visits.

Best response: respect withdrawal, explain any relevant follow-up/data arrangements described in the consent materials, and continue normal care. Do not threaten treatment access.

Case 3 · Suspected fabricated data

Several patient observations in a trial appear copied, although the patients were never assessed.

Best response: preserve factual evidence, protect participants and report promptly through research-integrity and governance routes; do not quietly edit records.

Case 4 · Unwell trial participant

A participant develops a potentially serious adverse reaction after receiving an investigational drug.

Best response: stabilise and treat, alert the investigator, document, and use protocol-defined safety reporting. Research goals never override clinical safety.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Explaining a clinical trial

Station: A patient with hypertension has been invited to a randomised medication trial and asks whether refusing will affect their NHS care.

Suggested consultation

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm understanding and ask what concerns the patient.
  2. Explain the research question and why a trial is being done.
  3. Describe randomisation in plain language: assignment by chance to a study group.
  4. Explain expected procedures, uncertainties, risks, potential benefits and reasonable alternatives.
  5. Clarify privacy, who may access data and whom to contact with concerns.
  6. State explicitly that participation is voluntary and refusing or withdrawing will not jeopardise ordinary care.
  7. Check understanding, offer written information and time to decide; avoid pressure.

Examiner checklist

  • Non-coercive approach and empathy
  • Clear explanation of randomisation and uncertainty
  • Balanced discussion of risks and benefits
  • Right to refuse or withdraw
  • Checks understanding and documents discussion

Do not promise the experimental intervention will work or imply ethics approval means zero risk.

Common PLAB traps

  • Assuming REC approval substitutes for participant consent.
  • Assuming refusing research can justify withholding routine care.
  • Confusing audit, service evaluation and research.
  • Thinking a small p-value automatically means a large clinical benefit.
  • Believing a relative can always consent on behalf of an adult lacking capacity.
  • Ignoring adverse-event reporting because the intervention is experimental.
