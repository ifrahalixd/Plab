Tropical Infections: Dengue, Typhoid & Schistosomiasis

PLAB priority: In febrile returning travellers, assess sepsis and urgently exclude malaria when epidemiologically indicated. Recognise severe dengue, typhoid complications and freshwater-related schistosomiasis.

01. Returned traveller assessment

Ask about exact destinations, dates, mosquito and freshwater exposure, food and water, vaccines, prophylaxis, sexual exposure, animal bites, and symptom onset. Record vitals, hydration, rash, bleeding, jaundice, organomegaly and neurological signs.

PLAB application: A fever after tropical travel needs same-day assessment and malaria exclusion where exposure is possible.

02. Prioritise malaria and sepsis

Malaria can coexist with other infections and may be rapidly fatal. Arrange urgent malaria testing when epidemiologically indicated, alongside blood cultures, FBC, U&Es, LFTs and other tests guided by illness. Assess for sepsis and shock using ABCDE.

PLAB application: Do not anchor on dengue or typhoid before ruling out malaria and stabilising the patient.

03. Dengue transmission and course

Dengue virus is spread mainly by Aedes mosquitoes, often biting during daylight. Incubation is commonly 4–10 days. Febrile illness can feature high fever, headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, nausea and rash.

PLAB application: Ask about travel within the previous two weeks and mosquito exposure.

04. Dengue warning signs

Warning signs include severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, fluid accumulation, lethargy or restlessness, liver enlargement and rising haematocrit with falling platelets. Severe dengue involves major plasma leakage, severe bleeding or organ impairment.

PLAB application: The critical phase may begin around defervescence: falling fever is not always recovery.

05. Dengue investigations

Use dengue PCR or NS1 antigen early in illness, with IgM serology later according to timing and local laboratory advice. Monitor FBC, haematocrit, platelets, renal function and clinical fluid balance; test for malaria when indicated.

PLAB application: Thrombocytopenia supports suspicion but does not diagnose dengue or determine bleeding risk alone.

06. Dengue management

There is no routine specific antiviral treatment. Provide careful oral or IV isotonic fluids according to severity and haemodynamic status; monitor for fluid overload as capillary leakage resolves. Use paracetamol for pain and fever; avoid aspirin and NSAIDs because of bleeding risk.

PLAB application: Escalate warning signs, shock, severe bleeding or organ impairment for urgent hospital care.

07. Enteric fever: typhoid and paratyphoid

Salmonella Typhi and Paratyphi spread through contaminated food or water. Symptoms can include sustained fever, headache, abdominal discomfort, diarrhoea or constipation, malaise and sometimes rose spots or hepatosplenomegaly.

PLAB application: A returning traveller with persistent fever and abdominal symptoms needs blood cultures and assessment.

08. Typhoid diagnosis

Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics when feasible and safe; yield may be lower after prior antibiotics. Stool cultures may help later. Serological Widal testing is unreliable and should not replace culture-based diagnosis.

PLAB application: Never delay resuscitation or necessary antibiotics for a culture in an unstable patient.

09. Typhoid treatment and resistance

Treat with antibiotics selected using travel region, resistance patterns, susceptibility results and infectious disease advice. Extensively drug-resistant typhoid affects empirical choices in some regions. Assess dehydration and complications.

PLAB application: Do not assume ciprofloxacin is effective: resistance is common in many travel-associated cases.

10. Typhoid complications and prevention

Potential complications include intestinal haemorrhage, perforation, encephalopathy and sepsis. Prevention includes food and water hygiene, sanitation and typhoid vaccination for appropriate travellers; vaccination is not fully protective.

PLAB application: Peritonitis, gastrointestinal bleeding or shock requires urgent surgical and medical escalation.

11. Schistosomiasis lifecycle

Freshwater cercariae released by infected snails penetrate skin during swimming, bathing or wading in endemic waters. Adult worms live in venous systems and eggs trigger inflammatory damage. It is not acquired from drinking freshwater.

PLAB application: A freshwater exposure history is essential even when the patient had no immediate symptoms.

12. Acute schistosomiasis

Katayama syndrome may develop weeks after first exposure, with fever, urticarial rash, cough, myalgia and marked eosinophilia. Early serology and egg microscopy may be negative before worms mature.

PLAB application: Consider schistosomiasis in a traveller with freshwater exposure and eosinophilia.

13. Chronic schistosomiasis

S. haematobium may cause haematuria, urinary tract fibrosis and increased bladder cancer risk. S. mansoni and S. japonicum may cause intestinal disease, periportal fibrosis and portal hypertension.

PLAB application: Painless haematuria after endemic freshwater exposure needs urinary schistosomiasis assessment.

14. Schistosomiasis testing and treatment

Testing can include stool or urine microscopy for eggs, serology and specialist investigations; optimal timing depends on exposure and parasite maturation. Praziquantel is standard treatment for established infection, with specialist timing and repeat treatment decisions.

PLAB application: Early negative tests do not reliably exclude infection after recent exposure.

15. Infection control and public health

Dengue requires mosquito bite prevention to reduce onward vector transmission; typhoid needs enteric precautions, safe food handling and public health notification according to local requirements. Schistosomiasis does not spread by routine person-to-person contact.

PLAB application: Discuss occupational food handling and contact tracing with public health teams when appropriate.

16. PLAB 2 communication and safety-netting

Explain likely diagnoses without certainty before testing. Give clear return advice for bleeding, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, reduced urine output, fainting, confusion, worsening breathlessness or inability to drink. Ask about pregnancy and immunosuppression.

PLAB application: Use an empathetic travel history, avoid stigma and communicate why urgent malaria testing matters.

Deep Dive

Dengue capillary leak

Immune and endothelial responses can produce transient plasma leakage, haemoconcentration, effusions and shock. Over-resuscitation becomes dangerous as vascular permeability normalises.

Dengue testing by illness day

Viral RNA and NS1 are more useful early; serological antibodies become more informative later. Cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses can complicate interpretation.

Enteric fever resistance

Salmonella Typhi susceptibility differs by geography. Fluoroquinolone non-susceptibility and extensively drug-resistant strains make travel-specific antibiotic selection essential.

Schistosome species and organ injury

S. haematobium typically affects urinary tract venous plexuses; S. mansoni and S. japonicum predominantly affect intestinal and hepatic portal circulation. Eggs drive granulomatous inflammation.

Why early schistosomiasis tests fail

Immature worms have not yet laid eggs, and antibody responses may not yet be detectable. Exposure date determines when repeat investigations are worthwhile.

Avoiding diagnostic anchoring

Fever, rash, thrombocytopenia, diarrhoea and eosinophilia overlap across tropical infections. Use exposure windows and confirmatory tests; urgent malaria exclusion is a safety-critical parallel step.
