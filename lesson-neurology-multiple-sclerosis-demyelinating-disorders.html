Multiple Sclerosis & Demyelinating Disorders

PLAB priority: Recognise optic neuritis and CNS demyelination, exclude infection before diagnosing a relapse, and identify neurological emergencies.

01. What is multiple sclerosis?

MS is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (brain, spinal cord and optic nerves). Focal demyelination and axonal injury cause neurological deficits separated in time and anatomical location. It is not a peripheral nerve disorder.

PLAB application: Recognise a young adult with episodic neurological symptoms affecting different CNS sites.

02. Who develops MS?

MS commonly begins in early adulthood and is more common in women. Genetic susceptibility, prior Epstein–Barr virus infection, smoking and low vitamin D exposure are associated with risk; none alone confirms diagnosis.

PLAB application: Do not diagnose MS from demographic risk factors alone.

03. Clinical presentations

Optic neuritis causes painful monocular visual loss and reduced colour vision. Other presentations include sensory loss, limb weakness, ataxia, diplopia, bladder urgency and fatigue. Symptoms usually evolve over hours to days.

PLAB application: Ask about previous neurological episodes that resolved.

04. Optic neuritis

Typical optic neuritis presents with subacute unilateral visual loss, pain on eye movement, impaired red colour perception and sometimes a relative afferent pupillary defect. A normal-looking optic disc does not exclude retrobulbar neuritis.

PLAB application: Urgent ophthalmology/neurology assessment is needed for acute visual loss; consider other causes.

05. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia

A medial longitudinal fasciculus lesion causes impaired adduction of one eye on horizontal gaze, often with nystagmus in the abducting eye. Bilateral INO in a young adult suggests MS but is not diagnostic.

PLAB application: Describe the eye movements accurately in OSCEs.

06. Lhermitte phenomenon and Uhthoff phenomenon

Lhermitte phenomenon is an electric-shock sensation down the spine on neck flexion; it can occur with cervical cord pathology other than MS. Uhthoff phenomenon is temporary worsening of pre-existing symptoms with heat or exertion, not necessarily a new relapse.

PLAB application: Distinguish transient heat-related worsening from new inflammatory activity.

07. Disease courses

Relapsing–remitting MS has attacks followed by partial or complete recovery. Secondary progressive MS involves sustained disability accumulation after a relapsing course. Primary progressive MS worsens gradually from onset.

PLAB application: Disease phenotype informs specialist disease-modifying treatment choices.

08. Recognising a relapse

A relapse is new or worsening neurological symptoms typical of inflammatory demyelination lasting more than 24 hours, in the absence of infection or another cause, after a stable period (usually at least one month). Fever and UTI may cause pseudo-relapse.

PLAB application: Check temperature, urine and infection symptoms before labelling relapse.

09. MRI and diagnostic criteria

MRI brain and often spinal cord with MS protocol can show typical lesions in periventricular, juxtacortical/cortical, infratentorial and spinal cord locations. Diagnosis uses specialist application of current McDonald criteria, including dissemination in space and time and exclusion of better explanations.

PLAB application: MRI abnormalities alone do not prove MS.

10. Lumbar puncture and additional tests

CSF oligoclonal bands may support MS diagnosis in the right clinical context. Blood tests and sometimes aquaporin-4/MOG antibodies help exclude mimics. Visual evoked potentials may support selected cases.

PLAB application: Interpret tests with the clinical history and MRI; do not rely on one biomarker.

11. Treating an acute relapse

First exclude infection and assess functional impact. NICE recommends oral methylprednisolone 0.5 g daily for 5 days for relapses affecting usual activities; IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3–5 days may be considered when oral therapy fails/is not tolerated or hospital admission is needed. Specialist oversight and adverse-effect counselling are important.

PLAB application: Steroids shorten relapse recovery but do not prevent long-term progression.

12. Disease-modifying therapies

Neurology specialists offer disease-modifying therapies according to disease activity, phenotype, eligibility and patient preferences. Options include interferons, glatiramer, fumarates, S1P modulators, anti-CD20 therapies and other high-efficacy treatments. Screening, vaccines, pregnancy planning and infection monitoring are treatment-specific.

PLAB application: Never abruptly stop or start specialist therapies without guidance.

13. Symptom management and rehabilitation

Manage fatigue, spasticity, pain, mood, bladder/bowel problems, sexual dysfunction and mobility with a multidisciplinary team. Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, exercise adapted to ability and specialist continence input can improve function.

PLAB application: Ask about hidden symptoms and daily living, not just limb power.

14. Important mimics: NMOSD and MOGAD

Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (often AQP4-IgG positive) and MOG antibody-associated disease can cause optic neuritis and transverse myelitis. Severe/bilateral optic neuritis, longitudinally extensive myelitis or area postrema symptoms should prompt specialist evaluation. Treatments differ from MS.

PLAB application: Do not assume every demyelinating attack is MS.

15. Red flags and emergencies

Sudden focal deficits require stroke assessment; rapidly progressive weakness, sensory level, urinary retention or severe back pain require urgent evaluation for spinal cord compression/inflammation. Severe visual loss, infection on immunotherapy or rapidly evolving neurological deficits warrant urgent referral.

PLAB application: Use emergency pathways rather than outpatient MS review for red flags.

16. PLAB 2 communication and follow-up

Explore symptom chronology, previous attacks, function, mood, bladder symptoms and concerns. Explain that MS is treatable but variable, avoid promising outcomes, discuss neurology referral and safety-net new weakness, vision loss, urinary retention or severe deterioration.

PLAB application: Use empathetic language and shared decisions; address pregnancy and driving advice individually.

PLAB 2 OSCE: possible MS consultation

Opening: introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and concerns. Ask onset, duration, vision, weakness, sensation, gait, bladder, fatigue and past episodes.

Examination: visual acuity, pupils, colour vision, eye movements, cranial nerves, limb tone/power/reflexes, coordination, sensation and gait where safe.

Explain: “These symptoms may come from inflammation affecting nerves in the brain or spinal cord. Several conditions can cause this, so a specialist assessment and MRI may be needed.”

Safety net: sudden stroke symptoms, worsening weakness, new urinary retention or major visual loss need urgent medical assessment.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Immune injury and plaques

Autoreactive immune pathways produce CNS inflammation, oligodendrocyte injury and focal plaques; neuroaxonal loss contributes to disability.

Dissemination in space and time

Typical neurological events or lesions in different CNS regions and at different times support MS, but modern diagnostic criteria require expert application.

Why optic neuritis hurts

Inflammation of the optic nerve often causes pain with eye movement and reduced colour vision; severe or atypical cases require evaluation for other inflammatory diseases.

Pseudo-relapse physiology

Heat and systemic illness temporarily worsen conduction in previously demyelinated axons without necessarily causing new lesions.

AQP4 versus MOG disorders

NMOSD and MOGAD have overlapping presentations but different diagnostic antibodies, relapse risks and treatments.

DMT safety

Immunomodulatory therapies differ in monitoring, infection risk, vaccination planning and reproductive safety; specialist supervision is essential.
