Abnormal eating or exercising behaviour: a whole-person clinical assessment

Changes in food intake, compensatory behaviour or exercise can signal a potentially life-threatening eating disorder even when a person does not look underweight. The PLAB task is to recognise the presentation, establish immediate medical safety, assess mental health and arrange age-appropriate multidisciplinary treatment without blame, weight stigma or assuming that body mass index alone determines risk.

Article audio
60-second summary: Explore restriction, binge episodes, vomiting, laxative/diuretic use, exercise driven by guilt, body-image concerns and rapid weight change. Check cardiovascular observations (including lying/standing where safe), hydration, temperature, ECG and electrolytes when indicated. Severe bradycardia, QTc abnormalities, electrolyte disturbance, syncope, dehydration, rapid weight loss or suicidal intent require urgent specialist/acute assessment. A normal BMI does not rule out serious illness. For a suspected eating disorder, NICE NG69 recommends immediate referral to an age-appropriate eating disorder service. Treat physical and psychological illness together; avoid simplistic advice to “just eat more”.

1. Classify the behaviour before applying a diagnosis

Restriction means limiting food amount, type or timing; ask whether fear of weight gain, sensory aversion, gastrointestinal symptoms, fear of choking or a medical condition drives it. Binge eating involves episodes of eating accompanied by a sense of loss of control; distinguish subjective experiences from objectively large episodes when applying diagnostic criteria. Compensatory behaviours include self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives or diuretics, fasting and exercise used to offset eating. Compulsive exercise is rigid and distressing to miss, often continued despite injury or illness; exercise volume alone does not define pathology.

2. Sensitive history: ask permission, then explore the pattern

  1. Opening: “I've noticed eating and activity have become stressful. Would it be okay if I ask some questions about what a typical day is like?” Use a private, non-judgemental space.
  2. Food and weight trajectory: recent and past intake, skipped meals, food rules, fasting, vegetarian/vegan changes with context, dietary diversity, growth trajectory in children, rapid change rather than only today's weight.
  3. Loss of control and compensation: binges, purging, laxatives, diuretics, diet pills, fluid loading, vomiting after meals, exercise despite fatigue or injury; distinguish prescribed medicines.
  4. Thoughts and emotions: fear of weight gain, body image, compulsive rituals, guilt, shame, anxiety, low mood, OCD traits, trauma, bullying or social media pressures without presuming a cause.
  5. Physical complications: dizziness/syncope, palpitations, chest pain, weakness, constipation, reflux, dental pain, menstrual changes, sexual health, fractures, cold intolerance, poor sleep or injury.
  6. Safety and context: suicidal thoughts, self-harm, substance use, safeguarding, bullying, family dynamics, social supports, sport or dance training, functional impairment and current medicines.

For under-18s, use developmentally appropriate conversation; offer time both with and without parents/carers when suitable and explain confidentiality and safeguarding limits. SCOFF can prompt further assessment but must not be used alone to rule in or rule out an eating disorder. Assess anyone with clinical concern irrespective of sex, ethnicity, age or weight.

3. Physical assessment: assess current instability and trajectory

Record pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation, hydration and consciousness. Consider lying/standing observations when safe; ask about orthostatic symptoms. Measure height and weight respectfully, using growth charts in children and adolescents, and consider rate of weight loss. Examine cardiovascular perfusion, muscle wasting, peripheral oedema, skin/hair changes, dental enamel, parotid enlargement, Russell sign (knuckle calluses from vomiting), bone/joint injury and signs of abuse or alternative organic illness. A patient may be medically unstable with a BMI that looks unremarkable.

4. Medical and psychiatric red flags

Do not delay acute care: collapse or syncope, haemodynamic instability, severe bradycardia (NICE gives heart rate below 40/min as an example requiring concern), significant QTc prolongation/arrhythmia, severe electrolyte disturbances, severe dehydration or malnutrition, organ dysfunction, inability to maintain intake, ongoing rapid deterioration, severe self-harm or suicidal intent. Assess overall MEED risk; no single normal measurement guarantees safety. Children may deteriorate more rapidly and need age-specific thresholds.

Arrange acute medical/paediatric assessment and emergency psychiatric input according to the presentation. If risk is not acute, do not postpone eating-disorder service referral until BMI falls or routine blood results return. Consider coexisting diabetes mellitus, especially if insulin omission for weight-control is suspected; diabetic ketoacidosis is a medical emergency.

5. Investigations: targeted to risk, not a screening substitute

Consider FBC, U&Es including potassium, magnesium, phosphate, calcium, glucose, renal function and liver tests according to context; request ECG for cardiovascular risk, purging, bradycardia, rapid weight loss, electrolyte disturbance or QT-prolonging medicines. Pregnancy testing may be appropriate with consent and clinical indication. Additional tests such as TFTs, coeliac serology, inflammatory markers or stool studies are selected when symptoms support organic differential diagnoses. Normal results do not exclude an eating disorder or risk from continued deterioration.

Refeeding risk: after prolonged restriction, insulin-driven cellular uptake of phosphate, potassium and magnesium during nutritional restoration can cause dangerous shifts, fluid overload and cardiac/neurological complications. Refeeding must be planned by clinicians experienced in eating disorders and nutrition, with monitoring tailored to risk; avoid delaying nutrition unnecessarily while arranging appropriate supervised care.

6. Management: medical safety + psychological treatment

NICE NG69 recommends immediate referral to a community-based, age-appropriate eating-disorder service when an eating disorder is suspected. Provide emergency care when physically or psychiatrically unsafe. Psychological treatment is disorder- and age-specific: adult anorexia may involve eating-disorder-focused CBT, MANTRA or specialist supportive clinical management; adolescents commonly receive family therapy for anorexia (FT-AN) where appropriate. For bulimia, guided self-help or eating-disorder-focused CBT can be used; for binge-eating disorder, guided self-help or CBT approaches address binge behaviour. The specialist team chooses treatment jointly with the patient. Medication is not the sole treatment for anorexia nervosa; any medicine must be considered with cardiac/electrolyte risks. Avoid framing treatment as simply achieving a target number.

Coordinate physical monitoring, nutritional restoration, psychiatric care, dental needs, fracture/bone health and social or educational supports. Discuss confidentiality, capacity and consent, particularly for young people; involve families/carers with the patient's agreement or under relevant safeguarding/legal frameworks. Build an explicit safety plan, document named services and follow-up, and communicate how to seek urgent help.

7. Exercise behaviour: healthy training versus a symptom

Ask why the person exercises and what happens if they cannot: distress, punishment for eating, rigid quotas, exercising through pain, inability to rest, secrecy and relationship impact indicate compulsive patterns. In athletes, consider relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S): prolonged insufficient energy availability can impair endocrine, immune, skeletal and cardiovascular function, with effects on performance and wellbeing. Do not use periods as the sole marker: amenorrhoea may be absent and RED-S occurs across sexes. Medical instability warrants restriction of strenuous exercise and urgent advice; supervised return-to-activity planning should be multidisciplinary.

8. Five worked PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — “Normal BMI” but rapid decline: A 17-year-old has lost substantial weight rapidly, avoids meals and now faints on standing. Despite a BMI in the reference range, the trajectory and syncope are high risk. Check observations and ECG, urgent same-day medical/paediatric assessment and eating-disorder specialist involvement. Exam trap: reassuring by BMI.
Case 2 — recurrent vomiting: A 23-year-old describes large binge episodes, vomiting and guilt; potassium is low and palpitations are present. Diagnose potential bulimia only after thorough assessment; address immediate electrolyte/ECG risk, ask about self-harm and refer for specialist psychological treatment. Trap: treating potassium alone and missing the ongoing behaviour.
Case 3 — sensory avoidance: A 12-year-old eats only a narrow range of textures, struggles with growth and denies body-shape concerns. Consider ARFID, nutritional deficiencies, autism-associated sensory needs and dysphagia or gastrointestinal pain. Refer to specialist child eating/nutrition services and investigate contributing medical problems. Trap: calling all restriction anorexia.
Case 4 — endurance athlete: A runner continues double sessions through a stress fracture, is fatigued and has reduced intake. Consider RED-S and compulsive exercise. Assess medical status, injury, bone/endocrine health and psychological drivers; advise temporary activity restriction if unsafe and specialist multidisciplinary input. Trap: praising discipline without assessing harm.
Case 5 — dangerous refeeding: A severely malnourished inpatient starts nutritional rehabilitation and develops weakness, oedema and low phosphate. Suspect refeeding syndrome, obtain urgent medical assessment and specialist-guided correction/monitoring. Trap: simply increasing calories without monitoring, or inappropriately stopping all nutrition without a plan.

9. PLAB 2 communication station: concerned parent and adolescent

Station: A parent reports their 16-year-old is restricting food and exercising after midnight. Ask the young person for permission to explore eating, exercise and distress; offer private time to discuss safety and mental health, explain confidentiality and its limits. Validate concern without commenting on appearance. Ask about dizziness, fainting, purging, suicidal thoughts, bullying and recent weight changes. Explain that serious complications can occur even when weight seems typical, and recommend medical observations plus urgent specialist referral. If danger signs are present arrange same-day hospital assessment, not a routine appointment.

OSCE closing script: “I'm concerned these behaviours may be affecting both your physical health and how you're feeling. You deserve support; we don't need to wait for your weight to change further. I'd like to check your heart and blood tests where needed, arrange a specialist assessment, and agree together what to do if you faint, feel unwell or have thoughts of harming yourself.”

Decision framework

  1. Is the person medically or psychiatrically unsafe now? Assess syncope, cardiovascular status, electrolytes, dehydration and self-harm.
  2. What drives food and exercise behaviour? Shape/weight fear versus sensory aversion, low appetite, anxiety or organic illness.
  3. Is there a binge-purge cycle? Assess regularity, loss of control, vomiting/laxatives and risks.
  4. What is the trajectory? Rapid changes, falling growth centiles and deterioration matter independently of BMI.
  5. Are there coexisting causes? Endocrine, GI, mood, neurodevelopmental or substance-related disorders may contribute.
Exam traps: A normal BMI does not exclude anorexia-spectrum illness; menstruation alone does not prove adequate nutrition; vomiting can be concealed; physical instability may occur without obvious emaciation; exercise can be compulsive even in a competitive athlete.

Deep Dive · pathophysiology, risk and clinical decisions

Deep Dive: how starvation, purging and energy deficiency produce harm

1. Adaptive starvation physiology

Prolonged insufficient energy intake reduces resting energy expenditure and alters autonomic and endocrine function. Bradycardia and hypotension may reflect adaptation but must not be dismissed, as they can indicate life-threatening compromise. Peripheral vasoconstriction, cold intolerance, slowed gut motility and reduced bone formation are common. Malnutrition can impair cognition and increase obsessive food-related thinking, complicating psychological treatment.

2. Electrolytes and cardiac electrophysiology

Self-induced vomiting tends to lose gastric hydrogen and chloride, causing chloride-depletion metabolic alkalosis; volume depletion activates renin–angiotensin–aldosterone pathways and can increase renal potassium loss. Laxative misuse can cause fluid loss, potassium disturbance and variable acid–base patterns. Hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia and QT-prolonging medicines can increase arrhythmic risk. The ECG may be abnormal even when the patient reports few symptoms.

3. Refeeding: the insulin–phosphate shift

During prolonged restriction the body reduces insulin secretion and relies more on fat-derived fuels. Carbohydrate reintroduction stimulates insulin and drives phosphate, magnesium and potassium into cells. Total body stores may already be depleted. Severe hypophosphataemia can impair myocardial contractility, respiratory muscle function, blood cell integrity and neurological function. Fluid and sodium retention may exacerbate cardiac compromise. Risk-based refeeding and monitoring should be led by an experienced team.

4. Hormonal and skeletal consequences

Energy deficiency can suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, reduce sex hormones and harm bone accrual, especially in adolescents. Amenorrhoea, where present, is not required for diagnosis or risk assessment. Low bone density and stress fractures may occur even with athletic appearance or non-low BMI. Recovery requires addressing energy availability and the eating disorder, not prescribing isolated supplements as a substitute for treatment.

5. Why the number on the scale is insufficient

Risk depends on the degree and speed of nutritional decline, physical observations, electrolyte shifts, purging frequency, ECG, psychiatric state, medical comorbidity and age. A young person may show cardiovascular compromise before becoming visibly underweight. NICE NG69 explicitly advises against using BMI or illness duration alone to decide eligibility for treatment; MEED provides emergency risk checklists.

6. Psychology and reinforcement

Restrictive patterns may initially reduce anxiety and reinforce rigid food rules. Binges may follow deprivation, distress or loss of control; vomiting and excessive exercise can transiently reduce distress, reinforcing recurrence. Avoid moralising labels such as lack of willpower. Treatment targets underlying cognitive, behavioural, interpersonal and nutritional processes while keeping the person safe.

7. Exercise and RED-S physiology

Relative energy deficiency in sport occurs when energy availability is inadequate for physiological function after exercise expenditure. Consequences may involve reproductive hormones, bone health, immune function and cardiovascular resilience. Training stress alone does not establish diagnosis; clinicians must assess nutrition, health, behaviour and context. Return-to-sport requires a risk-based, multidisciplinary plan.

8. Assessment of psychiatric safety

Depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive features, self-harm and substance misuse can coexist. Assess suicidal thoughts directly, including intent, plans and available means when relevant, as well as protective factors and immediate supports. Do not rely on a scoring instrument alone to predict future self-harm; use formulation and clinical judgement, consistent with NICE NG225.

9. Why multidisciplinary coordination matters

Medical teams manage unstable physiology; dietitians and specialist clinicians supervise nutritional rehabilitation; psychological therapists provide disorder-specific treatment; primary care provides continuity; child services involve families, school and safeguarding as appropriate. Failure to assign ownership for ECG results, electrolyte monitoring and referrals can be as dangerous as missing the initial diagnosis.

10. Examiner-integrated example

A 19-year-old has vomiting after meals, exercises despite ankle injury, feels ashamed and has palpitations. Assess immediate cardiac risk and self-harm, obtain ECG/electrolytes where indicated, explore binge/purge and restriction cycles, and refer promptly to age-appropriate eating-disorder services. Explain that risk is assessed from medical status and behaviour, not body size. Set an explicit follow-up and emergency return plan.
