Hernias: Inguinal, Femoral, Umbilical & Incisional

Surgery · Lesson 14 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Abdominal wall and groin hernias

Emergency: a painful irreducible hernia with vomiting, obstruction, skin changes, peritonitis or systemic toxicity may be strangulated. Keep nil by mouth, start ABCDE/resuscitation and seek urgent surgical assessment. Do not repeatedly force reduction.

1 · Definitions and clinical assessment

A hernia is protrusion of tissue through a defect in its containing wall. Reducible: contents return spontaneously or with gentle manipulation. Irreducible/incarcerated: cannot be returned, but blood supply may still be intact. Obstructed: bowel passage impaired. Strangulated: compromised blood supply, risking ischaemia, necrosis and perforation. History: onset, pain, variation with standing/coughing, reducibility, vomiting, constipation/obstipation, previous operations and comorbidity. Examine standing and supine where safe, inspect skin, cough impulse and tenderness, abdomen for obstruction, and both groins. A cough impulse may be absent in strangulation. Consider testicular/scrotal pathology and lymph nodes as alternatives.

2 · Groin anatomy: direct, indirect and femoral

TypeKey anatomyClinical significance
Indirect inguinalLateral to inferior epigastric vessels, through deep inguinal ring; may enter scrotumCommon; may be congenital due to patent processus vaginalis
Direct inguinalMedial to inferior epigastric vessels through Hesselbach triangleAcquired weakness of posterior inguinal wall; often older adults
FemoralBelow inguinal ligament, typically inferolateral to pubic tubercle, through femoral canalDisproportionately high incarceration/strangulation risk; urgent repair planning

Examination cannot always reliably distinguish direct from indirect inguinal hernia; imaging or operative findings may clarify. A groin lump below the inguinal ligament should raise suspicion of femoral hernia, especially in women.

3 · Umbilical, epigastric and incisional hernias

Umbilical: defect at umbilical ring; in adults associated with obesity, pregnancy and ascites. Paediatric umbilical hernias often close spontaneously, unlike symptomatic adult defects. Epigastric: linea alba defect between xiphisternum and umbilicus, often containing preperitoneal fat. Incisional: through a previous operative scar; risks include wound infection, obesity, smoking and poor healing. Assess defect size, symptoms, skin and prior repair. A stoma-related bulge may be a parastomal hernia.

4 · Investigation and differential

Most obvious uncomplicated hernias are clinical diagnoses. Ultrasound is useful for an uncertain groin mass; CT may help in complex recurrent/incisional hernias or suspected obstruction/complications, but do not delay emergency surgery for imaging in an unstable patient with peritonitis. Bloods for an acute presentation: FBC, U&E, CRP, venous blood gas/lactate, coagulation and group-and-save/crossmatch as appropriate. Normal lactate does not exclude early strangulation. Differentials include lymphadenopathy, saphena varix, femoral aneurysm, lipoma, hydrocele, undescended testis and abscess.

5 · Management

Uncomplicated inguinal hernia: discuss symptoms, fitness, preference and elective mesh repair (open or laparoscopic) with the surgical team. Watchful waiting may be reasonable for selected asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic men with inguinal hernias, after counselling about warning signs; symptomatic hernias and groin hernias in women generally merit surgical assessment because of femoral hernia risk. Femoral hernia: offer timely surgical repair due to greater strangulation risk. Umbilical/incisional: individualise repair based on symptoms, size, risk and patient preference; mesh may reduce recurrence in selected adults. Suspected strangulation: urgent operative assessment; resuscitation, analgesia, NBM, IV access/fluids, nasogastric decompression if obstructed and vomiting, and antibiotics if bowel ischaemia/perforation is suspected, following local policy. Specialist-led manual reduction (taxis) may be considered only in selected non-strangulated cases; avoid if signs of strangulation.

6 · Operative concepts and complications

Open mesh repair (e.g. Lichtenstein) and laparo-endoscopic approaches (TEP/TAPP) are common for inguinal hernias; choice depends on patient factors and surgical expertise. Risks: recurrence, wound infection, haematoma/seroma, urinary retention, chronic postoperative groin pain and testicular/vascular injury. In a contaminated field with non-viable bowel, mesh decisions are individualised. Postoperative increasing pain, fever, vomiting, wound erythema or a new irreducible lump requires reassessment.

7 · PLAB pitfalls

  • Femoral hernia is below the inguinal ligament and has high strangulation risk.
  • Irreducible does not automatically mean strangulated; assess pain, obstruction and systemic signs.
  • Strangulated hernia is a surgical emergency, even if initial blood tests are reassuring.
  • Do not label every groin lump a hernia without examination and appropriate differential.

Decision pathways and high-yield anatomy

Pathway A — Groin lump

History + groin/scrotal examination → determine relation to inguinal ligament, reducibility, pain and obstruction → if acutely painful/irreducible or systemic signs, urgent surgical review → otherwise ultrasound if uncertain and elective surgical referral according to type and symptoms.

Pathway B — Possible strangulation

ABCDE → IV access, analgesia, NBM, fluid resuscitation and bloods → urgent senior general surgery assessment → antibiotics if ischaemia/perforation suspected → emergency operation as indicated. Do not attempt repeated forceful reduction or wait for lactate elevation.

Pathway C — Elective repair counselling

Confirm diagnosis → assess symptoms and impact → explain observation in selected men versus repair → discuss mesh/open/laparoscopic approaches, anaesthetic fitness, recurrence and chronic pain → shared decision and safety-netting.

Memory aids

Direct = Medial to inferior epigastric vessels; Indirect = Lateral. Femoral = Below inguinal ligament. Femoral canal borders: inguinal ligament anteriorly, pectineal ligament posteriorly, lacunar ligament medially, femoral vein laterally.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Painful femoral lump

A 76-year-old woman has a tender irreducible lump below her right inguinal ligament, vomiting and abdominal distension. Answer: suspected obstructed/strangulated femoral hernia. NBM, ABCDE, IV access, fluids, analgesia and immediate surgical review.

Case 2 — Reducible inguinal swelling

A 52-year-old man reports a groin bulge on lifting that disappears when lying down, without pain or bowel symptoms. Answer: likely reducible inguinal hernia. Confirm examination, discuss warning signs, observation versus elective repair according to symptoms and preferences.

Case 3 — Scar bulge

A 61-year-old with previous midline laparotomy has an enlarging bulge at the scar, especially when coughing. Answer: incisional hernia. Assess defect, reducibility, skin, symptoms and risk factors; surgical assessment and selective imaging for repair planning.

Case 4 — Severe pain after reduction attempt

A patient with a previously reducible inguinal hernia now has persistent severe pain, fever and vomiting despite an apparent reduction. Answer: possible ongoing bowel compromise, including reduction en masse; urgent surgical evaluation and imaging if stable, without delaying necessary intervention.

PLAB 2 — Groin swelling consultation

Station: A 58-year-old patient reports a right groin lump that appears when lifting and asks whether an operation is necessary.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, gain consent and explore ideas/concerns.
  2. Ask onset, size, pain, cough/standing changes, reducibility, occupation and effect on daily life.
  3. Screen for vomiting, inability to pass stool/flatus, constant severe pain, fever, skin discolouration and irreducibility.
  4. Ask previous hernia repair, abdominal surgery, chronic cough, constipation, urinary straining and comorbidities.
  5. Offer examination with chaperone, explaining groin and scrotal assessment when appropriate.
  6. Explain likely hernia in simple language, distinguishing elective treatment from emergency warning signs.
  7. Discuss options: watchful waiting for selected minimally symptomatic men, or elective repair; femoral suspicion changes urgency.
  8. Explain mesh/open/keyhole repair broadly, including recovery, recurrence and chronic groin pain.
  9. Safety-net: attend emergency care for sudden severe pain, irreducible tender swelling, vomiting or skin colour change.
  10. Check understanding, shared decision and follow-up/referral.

Patient-friendly phrase: “A hernia means tissue is pushing through a weak spot in the abdominal wall. We can discuss whether monitoring or an operation best suits your symptoms, but a suddenly painful lump that will not go back needs emergency assessment.”

Rapid revision and UK references

  1. Indirect inguinal hernia: lateral to inferior epigastric vessels.
  2. Direct inguinal hernia: medial, through Hesselbach triangle.
  3. Femoral hernia: below inguinal ligament, higher strangulation risk.
  4. Reducible ≠ incarcerated ≠ obstructed ≠ strangulated.
  5. Strangulation signs: persistent pain, tenderness, irreducibility, vomiting, systemic illness and later skin changes.
  6. Normal lactate cannot safely exclude strangulation.
  7. Suspected strangulation requires immediate surgical assessment and resuscitation.
  8. Watchful waiting is an option for selected minimally symptomatic men with inguinal hernia, not a blanket recommendation.
  9. Incisional hernia develops through a prior surgical scar.
  10. Elective repair counselling includes recurrence and chronic postoperative pain.
