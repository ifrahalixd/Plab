Childhood Respiratory Conditions: Asthma, Bronchiolitis, Croup & Pneumonia

Red flags: Apnoea, central cyanosis, exhaustion, silent chest, altered consciousness, severe stridor or respiratory failure require emergency assessment.

First assessment

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Record respiratory rate, work of breathing, oxygen saturation, heart rate, temperature, hydration and mental state.

PLAB application: An exhausted or drowsy child with respiratory distress is critically unwell.

Age-specific respiratory assessment

Interpret respiratory rate and oxygen saturation in clinical context. Observe recession, nasal flaring, grunting, wheeze, stridor and ability to feed or speak.

PLAB application: Silent chest is not reassuring in severe asthma.

Asthma presentation

Recurrent variable wheeze, cough, breathlessness and chest tightness, often with triggers and atopy. Consider alternative diagnoses in very young children.

PLAB application: Not every preschool wheeze is asthma.

Asthma diagnosis

Use age-appropriate objective testing where feasible, including spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility and FeNO under current NICE/BTS/SIGN guidance; clinical review is vital.

PLAB application: Do not rely on one isolated symptom or test.

Acute asthma severity

Look for hypoxaemia, inability to speak/feed, marked recession, exhaustion, altered consciousness, poor respiratory effort and silent chest. Severity classification depends on age and local protocols.

PLAB application: Life-threatening signs need immediate senior and emergency help.

Acute asthma management

Give oxygen for hypoxaemia, inhaled salbutamol by spacer or oxygen-driven nebuliser according to severity, early systemic corticosteroids when indicated, and add ipratropium in severe attacks. Reassess repeatedly.

PLAB application: Escalate for persistent severe features; do not delay transfer.

Long-term asthma care

Use age-appropriate inhaled corticosteroid-containing regimens, check adherence and inhaler technique, address smoke exposure, and provide a written action plan with follow-up.

PLAB application: Avoid SABA-only management in established asthma when controller therapy is indicated.

Bronchiolitis presentation

Usually affects children under two, particularly infants, after viral coryza. Cough, tachypnoea, crackles or wheeze and poor feeding are common; apnoea may occur in young infants.

PLAB application: Bronchiolitis is a clinical diagnosis.

Bronchiolitis management

Supportive care: hydration, oxygen when indicated, nasal suction only if secretions cause difficulty, and respiratory support if needed. Routine bronchodilators, steroids and antibiotics are not recommended.

PLAB application: Use NICE age/risk-specific oxygen thresholds.

Bronchiolitis admission risks

Consider young age, prematurity, congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease, immunodeficiency, apnoea, dehydration and persistent low oxygen saturation.

PLAB application: Ask how much of normal feeds the infant takes.

Croup presentation

Viral laryngotracheitis causes barking cough, hoarseness and inspiratory stridor, often worse at night. Severity is assessed by stridor at rest, recession and agitation or fatigue.

PLAB application: Stridor at rest is concerning.

Croup management

Keep child calm; give dexamethasone according to local paediatric guidance. Severe croup may require nebulised adrenaline, oxygen and observation with senior airway support.

PLAB application: Avoid distressing examinations in significant upper-airway obstruction.

Other causes of stridor

Consider epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, foreign body and anaphylaxis. Sudden onset choking suggests foreign body; drooling and toxic appearance raise concern for epiglottitis.

PLAB application: Do not examine the throat forcibly in suspected epiglottitis.

Childhood pneumonia

Fever, cough, tachypnoea, focal crackles, chest indrawing or hypoxaemia may suggest pneumonia. Viral and bacterial causes overlap; assess severity and hydration.

PLAB application: Consider pneumonia with persistent fever and increased work of breathing.

Pneumonia treatment

Supportive care and antibiotics when bacterial community-acquired pneumonia is suspected, selected by age, severity, allergy and local/NICE guidance. Admit if severe distress, hypoxaemia or inability to hydrate.

PLAB application: Do not give antibiotics routinely for uncomplicated bronchiolitis.

Discharge and safety-netting

Explain warning signs: blue lips, apnoea, severe recession, exhaustion, poor intake, reduced wet nappies, worsening breathing or altered responsiveness. Arrange follow-up and check caregiver understanding.

PLAB application: Use teach-back and document the safety-net plan.

PLAB 2 — Breathless child consultation

Introduce yourself, assess ABCDE if unwell, ask about onset, fever, cough, wheeze, stridor, feeding, wet nappies, previous episodes, atopy, prematurity, immunisations and exposures. Explain the likely diagnosis in plain language, check understanding, discuss treatment and provide explicit emergency safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Asthma versus viral wheeze

Preschool wheeze is common and does not always imply persistent asthma. Reassess pattern, interval symptoms, triggers, family history and response to prescribed therapy.

Why bronchiolitis differs from asthma

Bronchiolitis involves small airway inflammation, mucus and debris, explaining why routine salbutamol and steroids do not reliably improve outcomes.

Oxygen thresholds in bronchiolitis

NICE NG9 distinguishes persistent saturation below 90% in most children aged 6 weeks and older from below 92% in infants under 6 weeks or children with underlying health conditions; assess the whole child.

Upper airway obstruction

Stridor suggests upper airway narrowing; wheeze usually reflects lower airway obstruction. Severe croup can deteriorate rapidly and requires calm handling.

Pneumonia diagnostics

Chest radiographs and blood tests are not routine for all mild community cases; use clinical severity, complications and hospital setting to guide investigations.

Discharge safety

Respiratory illnesses evolve. Ensure caregivers know red flags, can administer medications or inhalers correctly, and have clear routes for urgent reassessment.
