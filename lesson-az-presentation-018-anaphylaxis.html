Anaphylaxis: recognition, resuscitation and prevention

Anaphylaxis is a potentially fatal systemic hypersensitivity reaction, usually developing rapidly after exposure to an allergen. It is a clinical diagnosis: sudden airway, breathing or circulation compromise with a likely trigger, sometimes with skin or mucosal changes. Skin changes may be absent; do not wait for a rash before treating a life-threatening reaction.

Article audio
Emergency priority: Call for help and manage ABCDE, immediately give IM adrenaline 500 micrograms (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL, 1:1000) into the anterolateral thigh for most adults. Repeat after 5 minutes if airway, breathing or circulation problems persist. Doses differ in children: follow the age-appropriate Resuscitation Council UK algorithm. Do not confuse the 1 mg/mL IM preparation with IV cardiac-arrest adrenaline. IV adrenaline for anaphylaxis requires experienced specialist supervision.

1. Diagnostic recognition

  • Sudden onset over minutes to hours, often following food, medicine, insect venom or latex exposure.
  • Airway: tongue or throat swelling, hoarse voice, stridor or difficulty speaking.
  • Breathing: wheeze, persistent cough, tachypnoea, hypoxaemia or reduced respiratory effort.
  • Circulation: hypotension, collapse, confusion, syncope, pallor or poor perfusion.
  • Skin/mucosa: widespread urticaria, flushing or angioedema; may be absent in a meaningful minority of cases.
  • Severe abdominal cramps or repeated vomiting can accompany multisystem reactions, especially after exposure to a likely allergen.

2. Immediate first five minutes — emergency algorithm

  • Call the resuscitation team/ambulance. Stop suspected trigger if safe (e.g. discontinue an infusion); do not delay adrenaline.
  • Position lying flat with legs raised if tolerated; avoid standing or walking. If breathing is difficult, allow a position that eases breathing while protecting perfusion; in pregnancy use left lateral positioning.
  • Give IM adrenaline promptly into mid-outer thigh: adult/teenager usual healthcare-provider dose 500 micrograms of 1 mg/mL (1:1000). Reassess airway, breathing, circulation, oxygen saturation, pulse and blood pressure.
  • Give high-flow oxygen when indicated and IV crystalloid bolus for shock, with reassessment and local protocol. Prepare for expert airway support if swelling/stridor worsens.
  • Repeat IM adrenaline after 5 minutes if ABC compromise persists. If no response after two appropriate doses, seek expert management for refractory anaphylaxis; a monitored IV adrenaline infusion may be required.
  • Nebulised bronchodilator can supplement treatment for persistent bronchospasm but never replaces adrenaline. Antihistamines treat residual skin symptoms only, after stabilisation; corticosteroids are not recommended routinely for acute anaphylaxis.

4. History once stable

  • Time of onset and suspected exposures: foods, new antibiotics, NSAIDs, anaesthetics, insect stings, exercise, latex and contrast agents.
  • Sequence of skin, respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms; previous similar events, asthma and mast-cell disorders.
  • Exact medication name, formulation, dose, route, timing and co-factors (exercise, alcohol or infection).
  • Prior adrenaline auto-injector prescription, training, emergency plan and access to emergency care.
  • Any beta-blocker or ACE-inhibitor use, and comorbid asthma, cardiovascular disease or pregnancy that may affect risk.

5. Investigations: treat first, investigate second

Diagnosis and initial treatment must not be delayed to await tests. Consider mast-cell tryptase after stabilisation, with blood sampling timed according to the updated NICE NG258 pathway; capture the precise onset and sample times. A normal tryptase result does not rule out anaphylaxis, particularly food-triggered episodes. Check glucose, blood gas, ECG or other tests when they help assess alternative diagnoses or complications. Specialist follow-up may use targeted skin-prick or serum specific-IgE tests based on the history, not a broad screening panel.

6. After stabilisation, safe discharge and prevention

  • Observation duration is risk-stratified following local policy and NICE NG258; severity, repeated adrenaline, comorbid asthma and access to help affect decisions. Do not assume a fixed observation period for all patients.
  • Document the suspected trigger, clinical ABC findings, adrenaline dose/time and response; add an allergy alert to medical records.
  • Arrange referral to specialist allergy services, explain avoidance strategies and when urgent re-presentation is needed.
  • Provide appropriate adrenaline auto-injector(s) and hands-on technique training where indicated by current guidance; teach how and when to use them.
  • Advise prompt emergency help after suspected recurrent anaphylaxis, correct positioning and use of the next auto-injector if symptoms persist.
  • Offer a written emergency action plan, discuss school/work/carer training and recheck expiries and device access.

7. Five detailed PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Antibiotic-associated shock

Presentation: A 32-year-old has wheeze, facial swelling and BP 78/45 mmHg within minutes of IV co-amoxiclav.

Reasoning and action: Stop infusion, summon resuscitation help, give IM adrenaline immediately, position appropriately, oxygen/fluids and repeat at 5 minutes if ABC symptoms persist. Later document exact culprit and refer for drug allergy assessment.

Case 2 — No rash

Presentation: A 55-year-old becomes hoarse, hypotensive and breathless after a wasp sting. No urticaria is present.

Reasoning and action: Absence of rash does not exclude anaphylaxis. Treat the airway and circulation emergency immediately; do not wait for skin signs or blood tests.

Case 3 — Persistent wheeze

Presentation: A patient with food-triggered urticaria, wheeze and dizziness receives an antihistamine but becomes more short of breath.

Reasoning and action: Antihistamines cannot treat airway obstruction or shock. IM adrenaline is urgent first-line therapy; a bronchodilator may be an adjunct only.

Case 4 — Two adrenaline doses

Presentation: After two correct IM adrenaline doses, a patient remains hypotensive and hypoxic.

Reasoning and action: This is refractory anaphylaxis: secure expert critical care/anaesthesia support; follow local refractory algorithm with monitoring and specialist IV adrenaline infusion rather than unsupervised IV boluses.

Case 5 — Discharge conversation

Presentation: A 19-year-old treated after peanut exposure has recovered and is anxious about recurrence.

Reasoning and action: Explain the event, allergen-avoidance principles, auto-injector demonstration, individualised observation and clear emergency plan; organise allergy specialist referral.

8. PLAB 2 — concise patient counselling framework

Explain that this was a severe allergic reaction affecting breathing or circulation. Describe the suspected trigger without calling it proven; outline what was done, why adrenaline works, how to use prescribed auto-injectors, when to call emergency services, the need to avoid standing during a reaction and the purpose of allergy referral. Ask the patient to demonstrate injector technique and use teach-back.

Decision rules worth memorising

  • Wheeze + hypotension after an exposure is anaphylaxis until treated or convincingly excluded.
  • Bradycardia with a brief faint after a needle may be vasovagal, but persistent airway symptoms or cardiovascular instability requires a different response.
  • Angioedema without urticaria after ACE-inhibitor use suggests bradykinin-mediated disease; protect airway urgently.
  • A low or normal tryptase cannot retrospectively exclude anaphylaxis.
  • Adrenaline is first line for ABC compromise; antihistamines and steroids must not delay it.

Deep Dive · immunology / pharmacology / resuscitation

Why anaphylaxis causes sudden multisystem collapse

1. Mast cells and mediator release

In many allergic reactions, antigen cross-links allergen-specific IgE on mast cells and basophils, triggering release of histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins and cytokines. Not every anaphylactic event is IgE-mediated; direct mast cell activation and other pathways may produce clinically similar reactions.

2. Vascular leak and distributive shock

Histamine and other mediators cause peripheral vasodilation and increased microvascular permeability. Intravascular fluid rapidly shifts into interstitial spaces, reducing venous return and cardiac output. Circulatory collapse may occur quickly, explaining why prompt adrenaline and IV fluid resuscitation are so important.

3. Why adrenaline works

Alpha-1 activity constricts peripheral blood vessels and reduces mucosal oedema; beta-1 effects support cardiac output; beta-2 activity relaxes bronchial smooth muscle and can reduce mediator release. Intramuscular injection in the thigh provides rapid absorption; the intravenous route carries much greater risk of arrhythmia and dosing error outside specialist settings.

4. Respiratory physiology

Upper airway oedema narrows the laryngeal lumen, producing stridor and a progressively more difficult airway. Bronchoconstriction and mucus secretion cause wheeze and increased work of breathing. Severe cases can have a quiet or silent chest due to critically limited airflow, which is more dangerous than audible wheezing.

5. Histamine versus bradykinin swelling

Histaminergic angioedema often occurs with urticaria and may accompany anaphylaxis; ACE-inhibitor and hereditary angioedema involve bradykinin and often lack itching or hives. These conditions share the threat of airway obstruction but differ in their response to antihistamines and adrenaline; expert airway assessment is vital in both.

6. Biphasic reactions and post-event risk

Symptoms sometimes recur after an apparent recovery without re-exposure. Risk factors and the optimal observation time are variable, so follow a risk-stratified approach and current NICE NG258/local protocols. The patient needs a practical plan for recurrence rather than reassurance based solely on normal vital signs at one time point.

7. Frequent exam traps

  • No skin rash does not exclude anaphylaxis.
  • Tryptase sampling must not delay adrenaline.
  • Antihistamine and hydrocortisone do not treat acute life-threatening shock.
  • Adrenaline 1:1000 refers to 1 mg/mL, appropriate for IM injection; do not confuse with dilute IV formulations.
  • Adult healthcare-provider dose is not the same as every auto-injector dose; follow the relevant device and patient-specific protocol.
  • If symptoms persist after two IM doses, escalate for refractory anaphylaxis rather than attempting unsupervised IV boluses.
