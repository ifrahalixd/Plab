Professional Boundaries & Doctor–Patient Relationships

Learning outcomes: Recognise boundary violations; manage gifts, social media, conflicts of interest and intimate relationships; protect patients from exploitation; escalate concerns and maintain professional trust.

1. Why boundaries matter

Doctors hold a position of trust. A clinical relationship can involve a power imbalance, vulnerability, access to sensitive information and dependency. Maintain clear professional limits even when a patient initiates contact.

2. Sexual and romantic boundaries

You must not pursue a sexual or improper emotional relationship with a current patient. Consent by the patient does not remove the professional power imbalance. Relationships with former patients require careful consideration of vulnerability, time since care, nature of the former relationship and whether influence persists; they are not automatically acceptable.

3. Social media and messaging

Keep professional and personal identities appropriately separate. Do not disclose identifiable patient information or clinical images without appropriate authority. Avoid informal private messaging that undermines safe records, continuity or boundaries. Use approved clinical channels and document clinically relevant communications.

4. Gifts, money and favouritism

Small tokens of appreciation may sometimes be acceptable under local policy, but gifts that could influence, or appear to influence, clinical judgement should be declined. Never solicit gifts, loans, money or personal benefits from patients. Treat patients fairly regardless of gifts.

5. Conflicts of interest

Declare financial and personal interests that may affect professional decisions, including referrals, research, commercial relationships or prescribing. Do not let personal interests affect care. Follow employer procedures for disclosure and management.

6. Colleagues and professional conduct

Maintain respectful relationships, challenge harassment and discrimination, avoid bullying, and raise concerns about conduct that threatens patient safety or trust. Seek senior advice early when boundaries become blurred.

7. Chaperones and intimate examinations

Explain the examination, obtain consent, offer an appropriate chaperone, respect the patient's preferences and document the offer, response and chaperone's identity where relevant. A chaperone does not replace consent.

PLAB trap: A patient requesting a personal relationship does not make it professionally appropriate. Be empathetic, set a clear boundary, and arrange continuing safe care.

Decision-making pathways

Boundary concern: STOP → ASSESS → PROTECT → DOCUMENT

  1. STOP: Do not accept or initiate an inappropriate interaction.
  2. ASSESS: Consider patient vulnerability, power imbalance, coercion, conflict of interest and risks.
  3. PROTECT: State boundaries respectfully, seek senior or safeguarding advice if needed, ensure care continues.
  4. DOCUMENT: Record clinically relevant events and actions accurately, using local reporting pathways where necessary.

Gift offered?

Consider its value, timing, motive, perceived influence, local policy and whether declining would be appropriate. Never accept gifts conditional on preferential care.

Social media contact?

Do not conduct sensitive clinical consultations through an unapproved personal account. Redirect to official channels, address urgent clinical needs appropriately and record relevant contact.

Conflict of interest?

Declare it → seek impartial oversight → offer clinically justified choices → document decisions.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · Patient asks for a date

A patient you currently treat invites you to dinner and says they are comfortable with a romantic relationship.

Best approach: Decline clearly and kindly, explain professional boundaries without shaming them, and ensure ongoing appropriate care. Do not rely on the patient's consent as justification.

Case 2 · Expensive gift

A patient offers a costly watch before you decide on their referral.

Best approach: Politely decline, explain impartiality and follow organisational policy. Provide clinically appropriate care without favouritism.

Case 3 · Social media photo

A colleague posts a ward selfie with a patient's name visible on a bedside chart.

Best approach: Act to limit further disclosure, raise the concern promptly through local information-governance and clinical channels, and support appropriate incident management.

Case 4 · Private clinic referral

A doctor recommends a clinic they own without telling the patient of their financial interest.

Best approach: Declare the interest, provide balanced options and avoid allowing financial incentives to influence advice.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Setting a professional boundary

Station: A patient under your ongoing care says they have romantic feelings for you and asks to meet socially. Respond professionally while preserving dignity and access to care.

Suggested structure

  1. Thank them for being open; acknowledge feelings without encouraging intimacy.
  2. State clearly that you cannot pursue a personal or romantic relationship with a patient.
  3. Explain that the boundary exists to protect trust and safe care.
  4. Ask whether they feel comfortable continuing treatment; consider an appropriate transfer if clinically indicated.
  5. Assess distress or safety concerns without assuming pathology.
  6. Seek advice and document relevant matters discreetly.

Practice checklist

Useful phrase: “I appreciate you telling me. Because I'm your doctor, it's important that our relationship stays professional. I want to make sure you still receive the care you need.”
