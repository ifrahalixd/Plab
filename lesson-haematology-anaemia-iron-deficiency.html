Anaemia & Iron Deficiency Anaemia

Red flags: Severe breathlessness, syncope, chest pain, haemodynamic instability or active bleeding require urgent assessment.

Definition and classification

Anaemia is haemoglobin below the appropriate reference range. Classify using MCV: microcytic, normocytic or macrocytic; then use reticulocyte count and clinical context.

PLAB application: Always interpret Hb with age, sex, pregnancy and local laboratory ranges.

Symptoms and examination

Fatigue, dyspnoea, palpitations, dizziness and pallor are common. Ask about chest pain, syncope, bleeding and functional limitation.

PLAB application: Severe symptoms or haemodynamic instability require urgent assessment.

Microcytic differential

Iron deficiency, thalassaemia trait and anaemia of chronic inflammation are common causes. Sideroblastic anaemia and lead toxicity are less common.

PLAB application: Do not assume every low MCV represents iron deficiency.

Normocytic differential

Consider acute blood loss, renal disease, chronic inflammation, haemolysis and early iron deficiency.

PLAB application: Reticulocyte count helps distinguish poor production from loss or destruction.

Macrocytic differential

Consider vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, medicines and marrow disorders.

PLAB application: Check B12 before giving folate alone when deficiency is possible.

Iron physiology

Iron is needed for haemoglobin synthesis. Ferritin reflects iron stores but rises with inflammation; transferrin saturation can support assessment.

PLAB application: A normal ferritin in inflammatory disease does not always exclude deficiency.

Iron deficiency causes

Menstrual blood loss, gastrointestinal bleeding, pregnancy, dietary insufficiency and malabsorption including coeliac disease are key causes.

PLAB application: Find and treat the cause as well as replacing iron.

Full blood count pattern

Iron deficiency often causes low Hb, low MCV and low MCH, with raised RDW; early disease may be normocytic.

PLAB application: A blood film may show hypochromic microcytes and pencil cells.

Ferritin and tests

Low ferritin strongly supports iron deficiency. Interpret ferritin alongside CRP and transferrin saturation if inflammation is suspected.

PLAB application: Consider reticulocytes, renal function, B12/folate and haemolysis tests as appropriate.

GI evaluation

Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia in adult men and postmenopausal women warrants investigation for gastrointestinal causes, including malignancy.

PLAB application: Follow UK referral pathways and assess bowel symptoms, weight loss and family history.

Coeliac disease

Coeliac disease can cause iron malabsorption even without prominent gastrointestinal symptoms.

PLAB application: Consider coeliac serology as part of unexplained iron deficiency work-up.

Oral iron treatment

Oral ferrous salts are usual first-line treatment when appropriate. British Society of Gastroenterology guidance suggests one tablet daily initially; alternate-day dosing may improve tolerability.

PLAB application: Use current local formulary, check response and counsel about dark stools, nausea and constipation.

Monitoring response

A haemoglobin rise over the first few weeks supports response. Continue iron after Hb normalises to replenish stores, commonly around three additional months.

PLAB application: Reassess adherence, ongoing bleeding and alternative diagnoses if response is inadequate.

IV iron and transfusion

IV iron may be needed if oral treatment fails, is not tolerated or rapid repletion is required. Red-cell transfusion is reserved for selected severe or symptomatic cases, guided by clinical status.

PLAB application: Do not treat a stable patient based on haemoglobin alone; seek senior guidance.

Pregnancy and special groups

Iron needs increase during pregnancy. Use pregnancy-specific thresholds and maternity guidance; consider chronic kidney disease and inflammatory disorders separately.

PLAB application: Avoid applying adult nonpregnant cutoffs indiscriminately.

PLAB 2 counselling and safety-net

Explain anaemia and likely causes, ask about bleeding and diet, discuss tests and iron therapy, and arrange follow-up.

PLAB application: Urgent review for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, black tarry stool or significant ongoing bleeding.

PLAB 2 — Anaemia consultation

Introduce yourself and obtain consent. Explore fatigue, breathlessness, diet, menstrual or GI blood loss, medications, family history and red flags. Explain blood tests, the need to identify the cause, oral iron side effects and follow-up. Safety-net chest pain, syncope, severe breathlessness and significant bleeding.

Deep Dive

MCV and reticulocytes

MCV helps categorise anaemia; reticulocytes distinguish inadequate production from loss or destruction. Both must be interpreted with the clinical picture.

Iron absorption and hepcidin

Iron is absorbed primarily in the duodenum. Inflammation increases hepcidin, limiting iron availability and contributing to functional iron deficiency.

Ferritin caveat

Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, so inflammatory disease can mask depleted iron stores. Consider CRP and transferrin saturation.

Why look for a cause?

Iron replacement alone can miss ongoing gastrointestinal bleeding, coeliac disease or cancer. Investigate according to age, sex, symptoms and UK pathways.

Oral iron schedules

Lower-frequency regimens can improve tolerability and absorption. Follow current BSG, NICE and local prescribing guidance.

Transfusion versus iron replacement

Transfusion decisions depend on severity, symptoms, active bleeding and comorbidity. Transfusion does not remove the need to replace iron and identify its cause.
