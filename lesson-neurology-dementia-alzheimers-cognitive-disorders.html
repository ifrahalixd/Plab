Dementia, Alzheimer's Disease & Cognitive Disorders

PLAB priority: Sudden confusion is delirium until assessed; investigate acute causes rather than assuming dementia progression.

01. Dementia: definition and approach

Dementia describes acquired, persistent decline in one or more cognitive domains that interferes with everyday independence. It is a syndrome, not a single disease. Assess onset, course, function and reversible contributors.

PLAB application: Distinguish dementia from delirium, depression and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

02. Recognising the presentation

Common concerns include repeated questions, lost belongings, difficulty with finances or medication, getting lost, word-finding problems and personality change. Obtain a collateral history with consent.

PLAB application: Ask about activities of daily living, safety and the impact on carers.

03. Alzheimer disease

Alzheimer disease often begins insidiously with episodic memory impairment and progresses gradually to affect language, orientation, executive skills and daily function. Amyloid and tau pathology are associated with neurodegeneration.

PLAB application: Typical gradual amnestic presentation contrasts with acute confusion.

04. Vascular cognitive impairment

Vascular dementia may follow strokes or accumulate through small-vessel disease; executive dysfunction, slowed processing, focal neurological signs or a stepwise course may occur, although presentations vary.

PLAB application: Assess vascular risk factors and stroke history.

05. Dementia with Lewy bodies

Core clinical clues include fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism and REM sleep behaviour disorder. Marked sensitivity to antipsychotics is a major safety concern.

PLAB application: Avoid routine antipsychotic prescribing and seek specialist advice.

06. Frontotemporal dementia

Behavioural variant frontotemporal dementia can cause early disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviour or dietary change. Primary progressive aphasia presents with progressive language difficulties. Memory may initially be relatively spared.

PLAB application: Think of FTD with early behavioural or language changes, particularly in younger patients.

07. Mild cognitive impairment

MCI involves objective cognitive decline with largely preserved independence; some patients progress to dementia, some remain stable and some improve. Follow-up and evaluation of contributing factors are important.

PLAB application: Do not label every forgetful patient as having dementia.

08. Delirium versus dementia

Delirium develops over hours to days, often fluctuates and features impaired attention and awareness. Common triggers include infection, medication, dehydration, pain and metabolic disturbances. Delirium may coexist with dementia.

PLAB application: Acute confusion requires urgent search for underlying causes; use 4AT where appropriate.

09. History and examination

Establish onset, trajectory, affected domains, mood, sleep, substance use, medications, sensory impairment and functional consequences. Assess cognition, mood, neurological signs, gait and cardiovascular health.

PLAB application: Include collateral history and assess driving, falls, wandering and safeguarding.

10. Cognitive assessment

Brief validated tools such as 6CIT, Mini-Cog or GPCOG may support initial assessment; more detailed tools such as MoCA or ACE-III can be used by trained clinicians. Interpret results in context of education, language and sensory difficulties.

PLAB application: A single score does not diagnose or exclude dementia.

11. Investigations and imaging

Consider full blood count, renal and liver function, thyroid function, calcium, glucose/HbA1c, B12/folate as clinically indicated, and medication review. Structural CT or MRI helps exclude other causes and may support subtype diagnosis; specialist assessment guides further tests.

PLAB application: Investigate potentially treatable causes; do not order every test indiscriminately.

12. Diagnosis and specialist referral

NICE NG97 recommends referral to a specialist dementia diagnostic service when dementia remains suspected after initial assessment and reversible causes are investigated. Explain findings sensitively, involve the patient and offer appropriate support.

PLAB application: Document capacity for the specific decision, not a global judgement of incapacity.

13. Pharmacological treatment

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine) are options for mild to moderate Alzheimer disease; memantine is used in selected moderate disease when inhibitors are unsuitable and in severe disease. Prescribing follows specialist guidance and eligibility.

PLAB application: Check for bradycardia, syncope and other adverse effects with cholinesterase inhibitors.

14. Behavioural and psychological symptoms

First assess pain, delirium, environment, unmet needs and communication difficulties. Offer personalised psychosocial and environmental interventions. Antipsychotics have important risks and are reserved for specific severe distress or risk situations after careful discussion and review.

PLAB application: Never treat agitation reflexively without checking causes and safety.

15. Support, capacity and safeguarding

Offer cognitive stimulation therapy when appropriate, occupational therapy, carer education and support, advance care planning and community services. Under the Mental Capacity Act, presume capacity unless shown otherwise for the decision at the time.

PLAB application: Ask about medication management, finances, driving and carer strain.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and red flags

Explain that memory problems have several causes and require assessment; ask permission to involve family. Urgent red flags include sudden confusion, rapidly progressive decline, new focal deficits, seizures or suspected safeguarding concerns.

PLAB application: Use empathetic language and a clear follow-up and safety-net plan.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Amyloid and tau in Alzheimer disease

Extracellular amyloid plaques and intracellular tau pathology are characteristic, but clinical diagnosis still requires careful evaluation and exclusion of alternatives.

Why Lewy body symptoms fluctuate

Alpha-synuclein pathology affects multiple neural networks; fluctuating alertness, visual hallucinations and movement symptoms help distinguish this syndrome.

Executive versus memory dysfunction

Executive function covers planning, set-shifting and inhibition; early deficits can be prominent in vascular disease or FTD, whereas episodic memory deficits are often prominent in Alzheimer disease.

Delirium on dementia

People with dementia are vulnerable to superimposed delirium. A sudden deterioration is not necessarily natural disease progression and should prompt urgent assessment.

Antidementia medication choices

Cholinesterase inhibitors increase synaptic acetylcholine; memantine modulates glutamatergic signalling. Benefits are symptomatic and do not reverse neurodegeneration.

Ethics and person-centred care

Presume capacity, maximise supported decision-making and respect confidentiality; include family with consent and assess best interests when capacity for a decision is absent.
