Paediatric Neurology: Febrile Seizures, Epilepsy, Cerebral Palsy & Developmental Disorders

Emergency red flags: seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, recurrent seizures without recovery, reduced consciousness, suspected meningitis, focal deficit, infantile spasms, or developmental regression. Follow local emergency protocols.

Neurological assessment in children

Start with ABCDE for any acutely unwell child. Establish age, baseline development, onset and evolution of symptoms, fever, trauma, medications, toxin exposure and family history. Examine consciousness, pupils, posture, tone, reflexes, gait where appropriate, head circumference and signs of infection.

PLAB application: A child who is not returning to baseline after a seizure needs urgent evaluation.

Seizure recognition and mimics

A seizure may involve tonic stiffening, clonic jerking, impaired awareness, automatisms or focal symptoms. Ask witnesses about onset, duration, colour, breathing, asymmetry, eye deviation, incontinence and recovery. Consider syncope, breath-holding, sleep phenomena and movement disorders.

PLAB application: Witness videos can help; never diagnose epilepsy from one vague event alone.

Simple febrile seizure

Usually occurs between 6 months and 5 years with fever, is generalised, lasts less than 15 minutes, occurs once in 24 hours and has full neurological recovery. Evaluate the cause of fever and the child’s clinical state; exclude CNS infection when indicated.

PLAB application: A simple febrile seizure does not automatically require EEG, neuroimaging or long-term antiseizure medication.

Complex febrile seizure

Features include focal seizure, duration 15 minutes or longer, or recurrence within 24 hours. Persistent focal deficit, prolonged altered consciousness, meningeal signs or age outside the typical range increase concern.

PLAB application: Complex features prompt senior paediatric review and targeted investigation.

Acute seizure management

Protect from injury, time the seizure, position safely, check airway and oxygenation, assess glucose and obtain urgent help. Do not restrain or place anything in the mouth. A convulsive seizure lasting 5 minutes or longer is an emergency requiring the local status epilepticus pathway.

PLAB application: Use age- and weight-appropriate rescue medication according to current local/BNFC protocol.

Status epilepticus

Convulsive status epilepticus requires immediate emergency treatment and escalation. Address reversible causes including hypoglycaemia, infection and electrolyte abnormalities. Repeated benzodiazepine doses can cause respiratory depression; follow a structured protocol and prepare airway support.

PLAB application: Never delay emergency treatment while waiting for an EEG.

Epilepsy diagnosis

Epilepsy is a clinical diagnosis based on history, event description, recurrence risk and specialist assessment. ECG can identify cardiac mimics. EEG supports classification but a normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy. MRI is indicated according to seizure type and specialist guidance.

PLAB application: Refer children with a first suspected seizure promptly to an epilepsy-experienced clinician.

Focal versus generalised seizures

Focal seizures begin in one cerebral hemisphere and may have preserved or impaired awareness; they can spread bilaterally. Generalised seizures involve bilateral networks from onset. Classification influences antiseizure medication selection.

PLAB application: Do not assume every bilateral convulsion is primary generalised.

Absence seizures

Typical absence seizures cause brief abrupt pauses, staring and unresponsiveness, often many times daily, with immediate recovery and no postictal confusion. Hyperventilation may provoke events under supervision.

PLAB application: Distinguish absence seizures from daydreaming; arrange specialist assessment and EEG.

Infantile spasms

Brief clusters of flexion or extension spasms, often on waking, may accompany developmental arrest or regression. Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome is a neurological emergency requiring urgent specialist assessment and EEG.

PLAB application: Spasms in clusters plus loss of milestones must never be dismissed as reflux or startle.

Cerebral palsy: recognition

Cerebral palsy is a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture due to a non-progressive disturbance in the developing brain; manifestations can evolve as the child grows. Early signs include asymmetric movement, abnormal tone, delayed motor milestones and persistent primitive reflexes.

PLAB application: Regression or progressive weakness suggests an alternative or additional diagnosis.

Cerebral palsy: types and comorbidities

Spastic, dyskinetic and ataxic patterns may occur; distribution can be unilateral or bilateral. Assess feeding, swallowing, nutrition, pain, hip displacement, epilepsy, vision, hearing, learning and communication.

PLAB application: Management is multidisciplinary and focuses on participation and function.

Developmental delay and regression

Development is assessed across gross motor, fine motor, language, social and adaptive domains. Global developmental delay involves multiple domains. Loss of previously acquired skills is a red flag for neurological or metabolic disease.

PLAB application: Ask specifically whether the child has lost speech, walking ability or social engagement.

Autism spectrum disorder

Autism involves differences in social communication and interaction together with restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour or interests, with presentations varying widely. Consider hearing assessment, developmental history, school/nursery reports and coexisting conditions.

PLAB application: Do not infer autism from isolated speech delay; use a multidisciplinary assessment pathway.

ADHD and learning concerns

ADHD features developmentally inappropriate inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity causing impairment across settings. Consider sleep, hearing, vision, anxiety, trauma and learning difficulties. School observations and family perspectives are important.

PLAB application: Diagnosis and medication initiation follow specialist-led NICE pathways.

Counselling, safety-netting and safeguarding

Explain the likely diagnosis and uncertainty in clear language; discuss seizure first aid, fever management, rescue plans when indicated, nursery/school communication and follow-up. Explore caregiver worries, access to services and safeguarding concerns.

PLAB application: Urgent return for prolonged seizure, breathing difficulty, persistent confusion, meningitis signs or new weakness.

PLAB 2 — Parent consultation after a first seizure

Introduce yourself, check immediate safety, invite the parent’s description of the event and concerns, clarify fever and recovery, explain likely possibilities without overdiagnosing epilepsy, teach seizure first aid and clear emergency thresholds, and arrange appropriate paediatric follow-up. Ask about developmental progress and safeguarding concerns sensitively.

Deep Dive

Seizure semiology

The beginning of an event is often most diagnostic. Ask about aura, eye/head deviation, unilateral movements, automatisms, impaired awareness and recovery. Postictal sleepiness supports but does not prove an epileptic seizure.

Fever does not exclude meningitis

Febrile seizures are common, but prolonged altered consciousness, neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle, petechiae, poor perfusion or a toxic appearance require urgent assessment for serious infection.

Why a normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy

Routine EEG records a short sample of brain activity. Interictal epileptiform activity may be absent during the test; diagnosis depends on the whole clinical picture.

Infantile spasms and developmental outcomes

Clusters of brief spasms may be subtle. Prompt recognition and treatment are important because delay can affect development. Request urgent paediatric neurology advice and EEG.

Cerebral palsy is non-progressive, but needs change

The initial brain injury is not progressive; however, contractures, pain, hip displacement, feeding challenges and changing functional needs may develop. Regular multidisciplinary surveillance matters.

Counselling and rescue plans

Explain that caregivers should time seizures, protect the head, turn the child onto their side when safe, avoid putting objects in the mouth and call emergency services for prolonged seizures or breathing problems. Individual rescue plans require specialist prescribing and training.
