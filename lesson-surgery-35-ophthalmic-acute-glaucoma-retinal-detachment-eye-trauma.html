Ophthalmic Surgical Emergencies: Acute Glaucoma, Retinal Detachment & Eye Trauma

Surgery · Lesson 35 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Eye emergencies: vision can be lost within hours

Urgent red flags: sudden loss of vision, painful red eye with reduced acuity, chemical exposure, penetrating injury, new flashes/floaters with a curtain, or proptosis after trauma. Check visual acuity in each eye, protect the eye, and seek same-day emergency ophthalmology advice as appropriate.

1 · Structured eye assessment

ABCDE after major trauma. Record visual acuity separately in both eyes (with usual correction and pinhole if available), pupils and relative afferent pupillary defect, visual fields by confrontation, ocular movements, external inspection and fluorescein staining when safe. Ask about contact lenses, trauma, chemicals, surgery, glaucoma, diabetes and anticoagulants. Do not measure intraocular pressure or manipulate an eye with suspected open globe.

2 · Acute angle-closure glaucoma

Severe painful red eye, blurred vision and halos, headache, nausea/vomiting, hazy cornea and often a mid-dilated poorly reactive pupil. Intraocular pressure is markedly raised. This is an ophthalmic emergency: immediate ophthalmology referral, antiemetics/analgesia and pressure-lowering treatment according to specialist/local protocol (often topical aqueous suppressants and systemic acetazolamide if appropriate); definitive laser peripheral iridotomy after stabilisation. Check contraindications and do not delay referral.

3 · Retinal detachment

Typically painless new flashes, floaters, and progressive peripheral shadow or curtain across vision; central acuity may remain normal before macula involvement. Dilated retinal examination and urgent retinal specialist assessment; treatment may involve laser for retinal tears, pneumatic retinopexy, scleral buckle or vitrectomy. Macula-on suspected detachment needs immediate urgent specialist assessment to preserve central vision.

4 · Posterior vitreous detachment and retinal tears

New flashes and floaters may arise from posterior vitreous detachment, but a retinal tear or detachment must be excluded with urgent dilated retinal examination. Vitreous haemorrhage and a visual field defect increase concern. Safety-net for any curtain, increasing floaters or reduced vision.

5 · Chemical eye injuries

Start immediate copious irrigation with saline or clean water; do not wait for detailed history or visual acuity. Remove contact lenses and particulate material if safe, evert lids when trained, repeatedly check ocular surface pH until neutral and stable, and obtain emergency ophthalmology input. Alkali burns may penetrate deeply. Do not attempt chemical neutralisation with another chemical.

6 · Open globe / penetrating trauma

Suspect with high-velocity impact, irregular/peaked pupil, uveal prolapse, severe vision loss or positive Seidel test (only if safe to assess). Place a rigid eye shield without pressure, keep NBM, give analgesia/antiemetics, consider tetanus and systemic antibiotics per specialist advice, and obtain immediate ophthalmology/surgical assessment. Do not remove embedded objects, patch tightly, press on globe, perform tonometry or ultrasound if rupture suspected.

7 · Blunt trauma and orbital compartment syndrome

Retrobulbar haemorrhage after trauma may cause tense proptosis, reduced vision, RAPD, restricted eye movement and raised IOP: emergency ophthalmic/maxillofacial help and immediate decompression (lateral canthotomy/cantholysis by trained clinician) when clinically indicated—do not delay for CT. Orbital floor fracture may cause diplopia, restricted upgaze and infraorbital numbness; evaluate for muscle entrapment, especially in children.

8 · Corneal abrasion, ulcer and keratitis

Abrasion causes pain, tearing, photophobia and fluorescein uptake; check for retained foreign body and avoid topical anaesthetic prescriptions for home use. Contact lens wearers with painful red eye or corneal opacity require urgent same-day ophthalmology for possible microbial keratitis, including Pseudomonas. Do not start topical steroids without specialist direction.

9 · Endophthalmitis

Severe pain, reduced vision, red eye and hypopyon after intraocular surgery or injection (or endogenous infection) suggest endophthalmitis. Immediate ophthalmology for vitreous sampling and intravitreal antibiotics; do not delay for routine outpatient review.

10 · Other sudden vision loss

Central retinal artery occlusion causes sudden painless monocular vision loss and is a retinal stroke: immediate emergency stroke pathway and ophthalmology evaluation, with giant cell arteritis assessment when indicated. Giant cell arteritis with visual symptoms requires urgent systemic glucocorticoids per emergency protocol and specialist review; do not wait for biopsy. Central retinal vein occlusion typically causes painless visual impairment with retinal haemorrhages and needs prompt ophthalmic assessment.

11 · Red eye distinctions

Conjunctivitis usually has discharge with preserved acuity and little photophobia; uveitis causes photophobia, ciliary injection and a small/irregular pupil; scleritis causes deep severe pain; keratitis has corneal infiltrate/opacity. Any pain, photophobia or visual loss merits urgent assessment rather than assuming simple conjunctivitis.

Rapid eye emergency algorithms

Painful red eye + halos + vomiting

Visual acuity → suspect acute angle closure → emergency ophthalmology → pressure-lowering treatment per protocol → definitive laser iridotomy.

Flashes + floaters + curtain

Urgent dilated retinal examination → retinal tear/detachment assessment → emergency retinal specialist treatment, especially if macula-on.

Chemical splash

Irrigate immediately (before history) → remove lenses/particles when safe → check pH and continue irrigation → urgent ophthalmology.

Possible penetrating injury

Rigid shield, no pressure or tonometry → NBM, analgesia/antiemetic, tetanus/antibiotics as advised → emergency ophthalmic surgery.

Post-traumatic proptosis + reduced vision

Suspect orbital compartment syndrome → immediate specialist escalation and decompression when indicated; CT must not delay treatment.

Contact lenses + pain + corneal opacity

Suspect microbial keratitis → same-day ophthalmology; avoid empiric steroid drops.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Painful red eye and vomiting

A 67-year-old develops severe unilateral eye pain, halos, headache and nausea; the cornea is hazy and pupil mid-dilated. Likely diagnosis: acute angle-closure glaucoma. Priority: emergency ophthalmology and immediate IOP-lowering treatment per specialist protocol.

Case 2 — Curtain across vision

A 54-year-old reports flashes and many new floaters followed by a dark shadow spreading from the side. Likely diagnosis: retinal detachment. Priority: emergency dilated retinal examination and retinal surgeon review, particularly before macula involvement.

Case 3 — Alkali splash

A cleaner splashes drain cleaner into an eye and has severe pain. First action: immediate copious irrigation with water or saline, without waiting for visual acuity testing; monitor pH and obtain emergency ophthalmology care.

Case 4 — High-velocity metal injury

A metalworker develops pain and reduced vision after hammering metal; the pupil is irregular. Concern: open globe/intraocular foreign body. Priority: rigid shield, no pressure or tonometry, NBM and immediate ophthalmology.

PLAB 2 OSCE — sudden flashes and a curtain

Station: A 58-year-old presents with new flashes, floaters and a shadow across one eye.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, check symptoms and visual acuity in each eye.
  2. Ask onset/progression, one eye or both, pain, trauma, myopia, previous cataract surgery, diabetes and prior retinal problems.
  3. Screen for red flags: expanding curtain, central vision loss, many new floaters, eye injury.
  4. Explain concern about retinal tear or detachment in plain language; do not promise benign vitreous changes.
  5. Arrange urgent same-day emergency ophthalmology/retinal assessment and dilated examination; explain possible laser or surgery.
  6. Discuss practical safety: do not drive with impaired vision; arrange escort and follow local ophthalmic advice.
  7. Safety-net: any worsening shadow, new loss of central vision or rapidly increasing floaters requires immediate emergency review.

Exam pitfall: normal central visual acuity does not exclude a peripheral macula-on retinal detachment.

12 high-yield facts

  1. Record visual acuity in each eye separately in eye complaints.
  2. Acute angle closure: painful red eye, halos, vomiting, hazy cornea, mid-dilated pupil.
  3. Angle closure requires emergency pressure reduction and ophthalmology.
  4. Flashes, floaters and a curtain indicate possible retinal detachment.
  5. Macula-on retinal detachment is particularly time-critical.
  6. Chemical eye injury: irrigate immediately before other assessment.
  7. Continue chemical irrigation with repeated pH checks until stable neutral pH.
  8. Open globe: rigid shield, no pressure, no tonometry, NBM, urgent surgery.
  9. Orbital compartment syndrome may require decompression without waiting for CT.
  10. Contact lens wearer with painful red eye: exclude microbial keratitis urgently.
  11. Postoperative painful red eye with reduced vision: consider endophthalmitis.
  12. Sudden painless monocular visual loss: consider retinal artery occlusion/stroke and giant cell arteritis.
