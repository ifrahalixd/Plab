Preterm Labour, PPROM & Premature Birth

Urgent assessment: Preterm contractions, suspected ruptured membranes, vaginal bleeding, fever or reduced fetal movements need prompt maternity assessment.

Definitions and priorities

Preterm birth occurs before 37+0 weeks. Preterm labour involves regular uterine contractions with cervical change before 37 weeks. PPROM means preterm prelabour rupture of membranes before 37 weeks, before labour begins.

PLAB application: First assess maternal stability, gestation, fetal wellbeing, infection and bleeding.

Risk factors

Previous spontaneous preterm birth, cervical surgery or short cervix, multiple pregnancy, uterine abnormalities, infection and smoking are relevant risks; many patients have no identifiable risk.

PLAB application: Take a sensitive history and avoid implying blame.

Presenting symptoms

Regular tightenings, pelvic pressure, backache, increased discharge, fluid leakage and vaginal bleeding can indicate preterm labour or PPROM.

PLAB application: Ask about fetal movements, fever, offensive fluid and pain.

Initial assessment

Assess ABCDE when unwell, vital signs, temperature, pain, abdominal tenderness, contractions, vaginal loss and fetal movements. Arrange obstetric review and appropriate fetal assessment.

PLAB application: Sepsis, haemorrhage and fetal compromise require urgent escalation.

Confirming rupture of membranes

Use history and sterile speculum examination for pooling of amniotic fluid. If no fluid is seen, NICE recommends considering IGFBP-1 or PAMG-1 testing; interpret alongside clinical findings.

PLAB application: Avoid routine digital vaginal examination when PPROM is suspected, unless labour assessment requires it.

Diagnosing preterm labour

For suspected preterm labour with intact membranes, use gestation-specific NICE pathways. At 30 weeks or more, transvaginal cervical length is preferred where available; fetal fibronectin can be considered if cervical length testing is unavailable or unacceptable.

PLAB application: Do not combine cervical length and fetal fibronectin to diagnose preterm labour.

Cervical length thresholds

At 30 weeks or more with intact membranes, cervical length over 15 mm makes birth within 48 hours less likely; 15 mm or less supports a diagnosis of preterm labour in NICE guidance.

PLAB application: Interpret results within the complete clinical picture.

Fetal fibronectin

At 30 weeks or more when cervical length is unavailable or unacceptable, a fetal fibronectin result of 50 ng/mL or less makes birth within 48 hours less likely; above 50 ng/mL supports preterm labour.

PLAB application: Follow test sampling instructions and local pathways.

Antenatal corticosteroids

Corticosteroids accelerate fetal lung maturation when preterm birth is likely. NICE recommends offering them at 24+0 to 33+6 weeks and considering at 34+0 to 35+6 weeks in relevant preterm birth/PPROM scenarios.

PLAB application: Timing and eligibility require obstetric assessment; do not delay emergency delivery.

Magnesium sulfate neuroprotection

IV magnesium sulfate can reduce risk of cerebral palsy when very preterm birth is imminent. NICE recommends offering at 24+0 to 29+6 weeks and considering at 30+0 to 33+6 weeks if birth is expected within 24 hours.

PLAB application: Monitor respiratory rate, reflexes, urine output and signs of toxicity under protocol.

Tocolysis

Nifedipine may be used to delay birth briefly for corticosteroids or in-utero transfer when membranes are intact and criteria are met. NICE recommends offering nifedipine at 26+0 to 33+6 weeks and considering at 24+0 to 25+6 weeks.

PLAB application: Do not use tocolysis when maternal or fetal indications favour delivery; follow specialist guidance.

PPROM antibiotic prophylaxis

In PPROM, NICE recommends oral erythromycin for up to 10 days or until established labour, whichever is sooner, unless contraindicated; use a suitable alternative if erythromycin cannot be used.

PLAB application: Do not prescribe co-amoxiclav as prophylaxis for PPROM because of neonatal necrotising enterocolitis risk.

Infection and chorioamnionitis

Fever, maternal/fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness and offensive discharge raise concern for intra-amniotic infection. No single sign or test is definitive.

PLAB application: Urgent obstetric review, sepsis assessment, appropriate antibiotics and delivery planning are needed.

GBS and intrapartum care

Preterm labour and PPROM affect group B streptococcal risk assessment and intrapartum antibiotic decisions. In established preterm labour, NICE recommends intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis for GBS according to guidance.

PLAB application: Distinguish PPROM latency antibiotics from intrapartum GBS prophylaxis.

Neonatal and family considerations

Prematurity increases risks of respiratory distress, hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, infection, feeding difficulties, intraventricular haemorrhage and long-term developmental problems. Neonatal team input and in-utero transfer may be appropriate.

PLAB application: Explain uncertainties sensitively and involve parents in decisions.

Discharge and safety-netting

When preterm labour is unlikely and assessment is reassuring, give clear return advice for contractions, fluid loss, bleeding, fever, reduced fetal movements or feeling unwell; arrange appropriate follow-up.

PLAB application: Document findings, discussions and a specific escalation plan.

PLAB 2 — Suspected PPROM consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm gestational age, ask about gush or trickle of fluid, colour/odour, contractions, bleeding, fever and fetal movements. Explain sterile speculum examination, maternal observations, fetal assessment and possible tests. Discuss neonatal and obstetric review, corticosteroids, antibiotics and magnesium sulfate only where indicated. Check understanding, provide empathetic safety-netting and document the plan.

Deep Dive

Distinguishing labour from PPROM

Preterm labour is contractions with cervical change; PPROM is rupture of membranes before labour. They can coexist, and management depends on gestational age, infection, fetal wellbeing and likelihood of birth.

Gestation-specific decisions

Corticosteroids, magnesium sulfate, tocolysis and neonatal transfer have different gestational thresholds. Use NICE NG25 and local maternity protocols rather than applying a single treatment to all patients.

Avoiding unnecessary examinations

Digital vaginal examination in PPROM may increase infection risk and is generally avoided unless labour assessment makes it necessary. Sterile speculum examination and appropriate biomarker testing are preferred for membrane assessment.

Antibiotic distinction

Latency antibiotics in PPROM aim to reduce infectious morbidity and prolong pregnancy; intrapartum GBS prophylaxis is a separate decision. Co-amoxiclav is not recommended for PPROM prophylaxis.

Delivery timing

PPROM without infection or compromise may be managed expectantly with close surveillance. Suspected chorioamnionitis, abruption, severe maternal illness or fetal compromise may necessitate delivery; obstetric teams individualise timing.

Counselling and shared decisions

Discuss uncertainty, likelihood of neonatal admission, possible transfer and the balance between prolonging pregnancy and infection risk. Include the patient’s preferences and offer interpreters when needed.
