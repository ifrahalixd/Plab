Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Emergency: Unstable haematemesis is life-threatening. Resuscitate and escalate immediately.

Recognition and definition

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) arises proximal to the ligament of Treitz. Presentations include haematemesis, coffee-ground vomiting and melaena.

PLAB application: Severe bleeding can present with syncope, shock or fresh rectal blood if brisk.

Immediate assessment: ABCDE

Assess airway protection, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure; call senior help early. Monitor pulse, BP, mental status, oxygen saturation and urine output.

PLAB application: Protect the airway if ongoing massive haematemesis or reduced consciousness; involve anaesthetics early.

Resuscitation

Insert two large-bore IV cannulas when appropriate; send bloods and crossmatch. Give IV fluids judiciously while arranging blood products for major bleeding using local major haemorrhage protocols.

PLAB application: Do not delay resuscitation for endoscopy or a risk score.

Focused history

Ask about haematemesis, melaena, dizziness, pain, previous ulcer or bleed, liver disease, alcohol, anticoagulants, antiplatelets and NSAIDs.

PLAB application: Ask about recent retching, weight loss and co-morbidities.

Physical examination

Look for shock, pallor, abdominal tenderness and signs of chronic liver disease or portal hypertension.

PLAB application: Normal blood pressure does not rule out major blood loss, particularly early.

Investigations

Request FBC, U&Es, liver tests, coagulation profile, group and save/crossmatch, and venous blood gas/lactate if unwell. Repeat Hb as clinically indicated.

PLAB application: Initial haemoglobin may be deceptively normal in acute haemorrhage.

Risk stratification

Use the Glasgow-Blatchford score at first assessment. After endoscopy, use the full Rockall score where indicated by NICE.

PLAB application: Very low-risk patients may be considered for early discharge only after appropriate assessment and local criteria.

Timing of endoscopy

For unstable severe UGIB, offer endoscopy immediately after resuscitation. For other admitted patients, offer endoscopy within 24 hours of admission.

PLAB application: Endoscopy identifies the source and allows haemostasis.

Peptic ulcer bleeding

Common causes include H. pylori-associated and NSAID-related gastric or duodenal ulcers. Endoscopic treatment is guided by bleeding stigmata.

PLAB application: Endoscopic haemostasis may involve clips, thermal therapy or other modalities; adrenaline alone is not definitive treatment.

PPI in non-variceal bleeding

NICE CG141 advises against acid suppression before endoscopy for suspected non-variceal UGIB. After endoscopy, give PPI when stigmata of recent haemorrhage are present.

PLAB application: Follow current local protocols for PPI regimen and route.

Variceal bleeding

Suspect oesophageal or gastric varices in portal hypertension or cirrhosis. Start appropriate vasoactive therapy and prophylactic antibiotics at presentation, following local protocols.

PLAB application: Urgent endoscopy with band ligation for oesophageal varices; specialist escalation is essential.

Anticoagulants and antiplatelets

Review warfarin, DOACs and antiplatelet agents urgently. Reversal decisions depend on severity, drug, timing and thrombosis risk.

PLAB application: Follow current guidance and involve haematology/cardiology when needed; do not stop essential antithrombotics casually.

Transfusion decisions

Consider clinical instability, active bleeding, haemoglobin and comorbidities. A restrictive red-cell transfusion approach is often appropriate in stable patients, with individualisation.

PLAB application: Massive haemorrhage requires protocol-driven blood product support rather than waiting for a fixed Hb threshold.

Rebleeding and escalation

New haematemesis, haemodynamic deterioration or falling Hb after treatment may indicate rebleeding. Seek urgent endoscopic reassessment and consider interventional radiology or surgery if needed.

PLAB application: Repeated instability requires immediate senior-led escalation.

Aftercare and prevention

Identify and treat H. pylori, review NSAIDs and gastroprotection, assess iron deficiency and arrange follow-up as appropriate.

PLAB application: For variceal bleeding, secondary prevention is specialist-led.

PLAB 2 communication

Explain that bleeding may arise from an ulcer, inflamed lining or enlarged veins, and that resuscitation and endoscopy may be necessary.

PLAB application: Communicate sensitively, explain transfusion/endoscopy risks and benefits, check understanding and safety-net.

PLAB 2: explaining UGIB and urgent endoscopy

  1. Introduce yourself, check identity and assess stability.
  2. Explain why bleeding from the stomach or oesophagus can be dangerous.
  3. Discuss blood tests, IV treatment, transfusion if needed and endoscopy.
  4. Ask about NSAIDs, anticoagulants and liver disease.
  5. Explain consent, uncertainties, monitoring and signs of rebleeding.

Deep Dive

Haemodynamic compensation

Tachycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction may temporarily preserve blood pressure despite major haemorrhage.

Why haemoglobin can lag

Acute loss of whole blood may not immediately change concentration until redistribution or fluid resuscitation occurs.

Portal hypertension

Increased portal venous pressure creates collateral veins in the oesophagus and stomach that may rupture.

Endoscopic risk stigmata

Active bleeding and visible vessels identify ulcers with higher rebleeding risk and influence endoscopic treatment and PPI use.

Why anticoagulants matter

Antithrombotic drugs can worsen bleeding; reversal balances immediate haemostasis with thrombosis risk.

Rebleeding management

Persistent or recurrent bleeding may require repeat endoscopy, transcatheter embolisation or surgery.
