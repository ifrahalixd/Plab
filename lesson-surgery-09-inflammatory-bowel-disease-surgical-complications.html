Inflammatory bowel disease: surgical recognition and management

Emergency priorities: Suspected acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC), toxic megacolon, perforation, obstruction or intra-abdominal sepsis requires admission, ABCDE assessment, IV access, urgent gastroenterology and colorectal surgical involvement. Do not delay escalation for a routine outpatient work-up.

1 · Ulcerative colitis versus Crohn disease

FeatureUlcerative colitis (UC)Crohn disease (CD)
DistributionColon and rectum; continuous mucosal inflammation beginning at rectumAnywhere mouth to anus; discontinuous skip lesions; terminal ileum commonly affected
DepthPredominantly mucosalTransmural

ComplicationsASUC, toxic megacolon, colorectal dysplasia/cancerStrictures, obstruction, fistulas, abscesses, perianal sepsis
Surgical cure?Colectomy removes colonic UC, but pouch/stoma complications and extraintestinal disease can remainNot curative: recurrence after resection is common

2 · Assessment and investigations

Assess stool frequency and blood, weight loss, fever, pain, hydration, medication use (including steroids and biologics), recent antibiotics, travel, pregnancy possibility and extraintestinal symptoms. Check observations, abdominal tenderness/distension and perianal findings when appropriate with consent. Tests: FBC, CRP, U&Es, LFTs, albumin, stool cultures and Clostridioides difficile testing in a flare; faecal calprotectin helps distinguish inflammatory from functional symptoms in appropriate non-emergency presentations. Endoscopy with biopsies establishes diagnosis; use cross-sectional small-bowel imaging for Crohn disease. In severe colitis, use limited flexible sigmoidoscopy if appropriate rather than full colonoscopy when perforation risk is high.

3 · Acute severe ulcerative colitis (ASUC)

Truelove and Witts criteria: ≥6 bloody stools/day plus at least one of temperature >37.8°C, pulse >90/min, haemoglobin <105 g/L or ESR >30 mm/h (CRP may support severity assessment). Admit; assess sepsis and fluid/electrolyte deficits, send stool tests, give IV corticosteroids under specialist care, prescribe VTE prophylaxis unless contraindicated, monitor stool chart/CRP/observations and involve colorectal surgery early. Reassess response around day 3; consider rescue infliximab or ciclosporin under specialists if steroid-refractory, or urgent colectomy when clinically indicated.

4 · Toxic megacolon and perforation

Toxic megacolon is severe colitis with non-obstructive colonic dilatation and systemic toxicity; patients may have fever, tachycardia, shock, abdominal distension and tenderness. Urgent imaging and joint gastroenterology–surgical care are needed. Manage with resuscitation, bowel rest where indicated, treatment of infection and inflammation, electrolyte correction and close monitoring. Perforation, peritonitis, uncontrolled haemorrhage or deterioration despite treatment warrants urgent surgery. Avoid antidiarrhoeals/opioids that can worsen colonic dilatation in severe colitis.

5 · Crohn disease surgical complications

  • Fibrostenotic stricture/obstruction: colicky pain, vomiting and distension; resuscitate, image (often CT in emergency), assess for ischaemia/perforation; endoscopic dilatation or surgery for selected strictures.
  • Abscess: fever, localised pain, raised inflammatory markers; antibiotics plus image-guided drainage where feasible; surgery for uncontrolled sepsis or other indications.
  • Fistula: enteroenteric, enterovesical (pneumaturia/recurrent UTI), enterocutaneous; multidisciplinary management with control of sepsis and nutritional optimisation.
  • Perianal disease: abscess requires prompt drainage; complex fistula management often includes examination under anaesthesia, seton and biologic therapy coordinated by specialists.

6 · Surgery and longer-term follow-up

UC indications include refractory severe disease, perforation, toxic megacolon, severe bleeding and dysplasia/cancer. Emergency operation is often subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy; restorative proctocolectomy with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis may be considered later in suitable patients. Crohn surgery treats complications while preserving bowel length because recurrence and short-bowel syndrome are concerns. Review nutrition, anaemia, vaccination and immunosuppression risks, and colonoscopic cancer surveillance according to disease extent, duration and risk factors.

PLAB traps: Bloody diarrhoea plus tachycardia may be ASUC; always test for C. difficile during a severe flare; VTE risk is increased even with bloody diarrhoea; colectomy is not a cure for Crohn disease; a perianal abscess needs drainage, not antibiotics alone.

Decision pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Bloody diarrhoea

Assess ABCDE and hydration → stool frequency, blood and systemic signs → blood tests + stool culture/C. difficile → if ASUC criteria: admit, IV steroids, VTE assessment/prophylaxis, gastroenterology and early surgical review → day-3 response assessment → rescue therapy or surgery.

Pathway B — Toxic megacolon

Severe colitis + distension/systemic toxicity → resuscitate, urgent abdominal imaging, senior GI/colorectal review → treat underlying inflammation/infection, monitor closely → perforation, shock or failure to improve: emergency colectomy assessment.

Pathway C — Crohn abscess or obstruction

Fever/focal pain or vomiting/distension → ABCDE + labs + urgent cross-sectional imaging → abscess: antibiotics and drainage/source control; obstruction: fluids, decompression when indicated, assess for strangulation → definitive endoscopic/surgical plan.

Slide 1 · Pattern

UC = continuous colonic mucosal disease; Crohn = skip lesions and transmural disease.

Slide 2 · Time-critical red flags

ASUC, toxic megacolon, perforation, obstruction and sepsis.

Slide 3 · Surgical principle

Colectomy can eliminate colonic UC; Crohn resection is not curative.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Acute severe UC

A 28-year-old with UC passes eight bloody stools/day, pulse 112 and temperature 38.1°C. Decision: Meets ASUC criteria; admit, resuscitate, bloods/stool cultures including C. difficile, IV steroids under specialist care, VTE prophylaxis assessment and early colorectal review.

Case 2 — Toxic megacolon

A 39-year-old with colitis develops a distended tender abdomen, fever and hypotension. Imaging shows marked colonic dilatation. Decision: Suspect toxic megacolon; urgent resuscitation, specialist multidisciplinary management, and immediate surgical assessment for deterioration or perforation.

Case 3 — Crohn intra-abdominal abscess

A 24-year-old with ileal Crohn has fever and right iliac fossa pain; CT shows a 5 cm collection. Decision: IV antibiotics, urgent GI/colorectal and interventional radiology input for image-guided drainage and sepsis source control.

Case 4 — Crohn stricture

A 47-year-old with previous Crohn resections develops colicky pain, vomiting and distension. Decision: Assess for bowel obstruction, fluids, electrolytes and urgent CT; early surgical review, bowel-preserving treatment where feasible.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute bloody diarrhoea station

Station: You are an FY2 in ED. A 30-year-old with known ulcerative colitis reports worsening bloody diarrhoea and weakness. Take a focused history and explain immediate management.

Examiner checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent; identify acute illness.
  2. Assess ABCDE, observations, hydration, abdominal pain and peritonism; escalate early.
  3. Quantify bloody stool frequency, onset, urgency, nocturnal symptoms and fever.
  4. Ask about oral intake, vomiting, dizziness, weight loss and previous admissions/surgery.
  5. Review steroids, mesalazine, biologics, antibiotics, adherence and recent infection.
  6. Explain FBC, U&Es, CRP, albumin, stool cultures and C. difficile testing.
  7. Recognise ASUC; explain admission, IV fluids as needed, IV corticosteroids, thromboprophylaxis assessment and specialist review.
  8. Explain monitoring, possible rescue treatment and surgical options if deterioration; safety-net and SBAR handover.

Patient-centred wording: “Your symptoms suggest a severe flare of bowel inflammation. We need to admit you, replace any lost fluids, check for infection and start treatment while our bowel specialists assess you.”

Rapid revision & UK references

  1. UC: continuous mucosal colitis beginning at the rectum; Crohn: skip lesions, transmural inflammation.
  2. ASUC: ≥6 bloody stools daily plus ≥1 systemic severity feature.
  3. ASUC needs admission, stool infection testing, IV corticosteroids and early surgical input.
  4. Evaluate steroid response by around day 3; rescue therapy or colectomy may be required.
  5. Toxic megacolon: colonic dilatation + systemic toxicity; watch for perforation and shock.
  6. Crohn causes strictures, fistulas, abscesses and perianal disease.
  7. Drain perianal abscesses promptly; control intra-abdominal sepsis.
  8. Colectomy treats colonic UC; Crohn can recur after surgery.
