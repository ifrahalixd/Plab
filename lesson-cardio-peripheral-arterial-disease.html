Learning objectives

Peripheral vascular disease and limb-threatening emergencies

Recognise chronic PAD and acute limb ischaemia, interpret ABPI, identify threatened limbs and select safe UK referral and treatment pathways.

Emergency rule: A suddenly painful, cold, numb or weak limb is a vascular emergency. Call the vascular team immediately; do not delay for routine testing. Do not use this educational page as a clinical protocol.

Browser text-to-speech, if supported.

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)

Atherosclerotic narrowing reduces blood flow to the legs. Typical intermittent claudication is reproducible calf, thigh or buttock discomfort on walking, relieved by rest. PAD is also a marker of increased cardiovascular risk.

Assessment and management: Assess smoking, diabetes, hypertension, lipid profile and cardiovascular history. Examine pulses, skin, temperature, ulcers and capillary refill. Arrange ABPI and vascular assessment as appropriate.

Ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI)

ABPI compares ankle systolic pressure with brachial systolic pressure. An ABPI below 0.90 supports PAD; normal or high readings do not always exclude disease, particularly in diabetes where calcified arteries can be incompressible.

Assessment and management: Use Doppler measurements correctly; consider exercise ABPI or toe pressure testing when clinical suspicion persists. Avoid interpreting ABPI in isolation.

Intermittent claudication

Exertional muscle pain occurs because oxygen supply cannot meet demand. Symptoms usually settle within minutes of stopping exercise. Neurogenic claudication from spinal stenosis may improve on flexion rather than simply stopping.

Assessment and management: Offer supervised exercise programmes, smoking cessation, cardiovascular risk reduction and antiplatelet/statin therapy where indicated. Revascularisation may be considered for persistent lifestyle-limiting symptoms after appropriate treatment.

Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI)

Advanced PAD may cause persistent ischaemic rest pain, non-healing ulcers or gangrene. Patients may report night pain relieved by hanging the leg down.

Assessment and management: Urgent vascular referral is essential. Assess infection and tissue loss; provide analgesia, wound care and coordinated revascularisation planning.

Acute limb ischaemia (ALI)

A sudden reduction in arterial perfusion threatens limb viability. The six Ps are pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis and poikilothermia (cold limb). Not every sign is present, and sensory or motor deficits suggest advanced ischaemia.

Assessment and management: Immediate emergency vascular referral. Assess ABCDE, document neurovascular status, provide analgesia and start IV unfractionated heparin when appropriate under emergency specialist guidance unless contraindicated. Do not delay referral for imaging.

Causes of ALI

Arterial embolism (often from atrial fibrillation), thrombosis of pre-existing PAD, graft occlusion and trauma are common mechanisms. An embolic limb may have sudden symptoms without prior claudication.

Assessment and management: Check ECG for AF and assess both limbs, embolic sources and prior vascular procedures. Vascular imaging and intervention are specialist-led.

Acute limb viability

A viable limb has preserved sensation and movement; threatened limbs show sensory loss and then weakness. Profound anaesthesia and paralysis can indicate irreversible injury.

Assessment and management: Serial neurovascular examinations are vital. Emergency revascularisation may involve embolectomy, catheter-directed therapy or surgery, depending on anatomy and viability.

Investigations

Duplex ultrasound assesses arterial flow. CT angiography can define anatomy for intervention, but must not delay treatment of a critically threatened limb. ABPI helps chronic PAD assessment, not the sole test for an acute emergency.

Assessment and management: Bloods may include FBC, renal function, electrolytes, coagulation, glucose and lactate; ECG and crossmatch as indicated.

Medical prevention in PAD

PAD often coexists with coronary and cerebrovascular atherosclerosis. Long-term care addresses smoking, lipids, blood pressure, diabetes and physical activity.

Assessment and management: Follow NICE PAD guidance and local protocols for antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statins, supervised exercise and vascular referral. Review bleeding risk and medicine contraindications.

Diabetic foot and PAD

Diabetes can cause neuropathy and incompressible arteries, masking pain and making ABPI less reliable. Ulceration plus infection or ischaemia raises amputation risk.

Assessment and management: Urgently assess threatened feet, arrange multidisciplinary diabetic foot/vascular input and consider toe pressures or other perfusion tests.

Compartment syndrome after reperfusion

Restored blood flow can cause swelling and rising compartment pressure. Severe pain, pain on passive stretch and neurological deficits are concerning.

Assessment and management: Urgent reassessment and possible fasciotomy by the vascular team may be required.

PLAB 2 vascular examination

Ask about walking distance, rest pain, ulcers, smoking, diabetes, AF and prior interventions. Inspect both legs; palpate femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses; assess temperature, capillary refill and neurological function.

Assessment and management: For an acutely cold, painful or weak limb, stop the routine examination and escalate immediately.

PLAB 2: focused OSCE approach

  1. Ask about claudication distance, rest pain, ulcers, smoking, diabetes, atrial fibrillation and previous vascular surgery.
  2. Inspect both limbs for colour, ulcers, hair loss, wasting and gangrene.
  3. Palpate femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses; compare temperature and capillary refill.
  4. Assess sensation and movement, particularly in suspected acute ischaemia.
  5. Explain ABPI or vascular imaging and urgent referral when indicated; give clear safety-net advice.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Why claudication occurs

During exercise, skeletal muscle oxygen demand rises but stenosed arteries cannot increase flow sufficiently. Rest reduces demand and relieves ischaemic pain.

ABPI pitfalls

Calcification in diabetes and chronic kidney disease may make ankle vessels incompressible, producing a misleadingly high ABPI. Toe pressure or vascular imaging may help.

ALI vs CLTI

ALI is a sudden perfusion reduction, typically within 14 days, whereas CLTI is chronic rest pain, tissue loss or gangrene due to PAD. Both may threaten a limb but differ in timing and management.

Embolus vs thrombosis

Emboli can cause abrupt severe symptoms in previously asymptomatic limbs; thrombosis often occurs on a background of chronic PAD, though clinical patterns overlap.

Why weakness matters

Motor dysfunction suggests severe nerve and muscle ischaemia. Delayed revascularisation increases the risk of irreversible tissue damage.

Reperfusion injury

Re-establishing flow can release metabolites and cause oedema, hyperkalaemia and compartment syndrome, requiring close monitoring.

Cardiovascular prevention

PAD is not just a leg condition; smoking cessation, lipid lowering and antithrombotic decisions reduce systemic vascular risk.
