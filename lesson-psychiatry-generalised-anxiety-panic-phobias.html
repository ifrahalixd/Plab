Generalised Anxiety Disorder, Panic Disorder & Phobias

PLAB priority: Differentiate GAD, panic disorder and phobias; assess medical mimics, suicide risk and appropriate NICE stepped care.

01. Recognising anxiety disorders

Anxiety becomes a disorder when persistent fear, worry or avoidance causes significant distress or functional impairment. Assess duration, triggers, physical symptoms, avoidance and impact on work, sleep and relationships.

PLAB application: Do not label new chest pain, palpitations or breathlessness as anxiety without assessing physical causes.

02. Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)

GAD involves excessive, difficult-to-control worry across multiple domains, often with restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension and disturbed sleep. Symptoms are typically present for at least six months in diagnostic frameworks.

PLAB application: Explore the breadth of worry and functional impairment, not just whether the patient feels nervous.

03. Panic attacks

A panic attack is an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort, typically peaking within minutes, with palpitations, sweating, trembling, dyspnoea, choking sensations, chest discomfort, dizziness, tingling or fear of dying.

PLAB application: A panic attack is a symptom, not automatically panic disorder.

04. Panic disorder

Panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus persistent concern about further attacks or maladaptive behaviour such as avoidance. Anticipatory anxiety may become disabling.

PLAB application: Ask about unprovoked attacks and what the patient does to prevent another.

05. Agoraphobia

Agoraphobia is fear or avoidance of situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable, such as public transport, queues, crowds or being outside alone. It can occur with or without panic disorder.

PLAB application: Assess whether the person can leave home, travel and attend appointments.

06. Specific phobias

Specific phobia involves disproportionate fear of a particular object or situation, such as heights, blood, needles, animals or flying, leading to immediate anxiety and avoidance.

PLAB application: Graded exposure-based CBT is central; routine medication is usually not the main treatment.

07. Social anxiety disorder

Social anxiety centres on fear of negative evaluation or embarrassment in social or performance situations, often with avoidance. It differs from general shyness through distress and impairment.

PLAB application: Ask about eating, speaking or meeting people in public; offer disorder-specific CBT.

08. Differential diagnosis and physical illness

Consider arrhythmia, acute coronary syndrome, asthma, pulmonary embolism, hyperthyroidism, hypoglycaemia, anaemia, stimulant or caffeine use, substance withdrawal and medication effects when indicated by presentation.

PLAB application: Use focused history, observations and examination; urgent symptoms require appropriate emergency assessment.

09. Clinical assessment and screening

Take a biopsychosocial history: onset, triggers, panic frequency, worry domains, sleep, functioning, medical history, medications, alcohol and drugs, trauma, supports and previous treatments. GAD-7 can help assess symptom burden but is not diagnostic alone.

PLAB application: Ask about depression, self-harm and suicidal thoughts, especially with severe impairment or comorbidity.

10. GAD stepped care: NICE CG113

For GAD, begin with education, active monitoring and low-intensity interventions such as individual non-facilitated or guided self-help and psychoeducational groups. If marked impairment or inadequate response, offer high-intensity CBT or applied relaxation, or drug treatment according to preference.

PLAB application: Use shared decision-making; avoid presenting medication as compulsory.

11. Drug treatment for GAD

When medication is chosen, NICE CG113 recommends an SSRI, considering sertraline first (off-label for GAD in the UK). If ineffective, consider another SSRI or SNRI; pregabalin may be considered if SSRIs/SNRIs are not tolerated, with dependence and pregnancy-related cautions.

PLAB application: Discuss delayed benefit, initial adverse effects, withdrawal and follow-up; check current BNF and safety advice.

12. Panic disorder treatment

NICE recommends CBT and, where appropriate, antidepressant medication for longer-term management of panic disorder. Antidepressants with evidence include SSRIs and some other agents; choose based on licensed indication, risks and patient preference.

PLAB application: Explain that short-term panic symptoms can feel catastrophic but require safety-netting and physical assessment when appropriate.

13. Benzodiazepines and prescribing safety

NICE advises against benzodiazepines for long-term GAD management except short-term use during crises, and against their use for panic disorder treatment. They carry sedation, dependence, falls and driving risks.

PLAB application: Do not use benzodiazepines as routine maintenance for anxiety or panic disorder.

14. Psychological treatment and exposure

CBT targets catastrophic interpretations, worry cycles and safety behaviours. Exposure is planned and graded, helping the person learn that feared situations are manageable without relying on avoidance.

PLAB application: Explain exposure collaboratively, without forcing distressing situations or minimising symptoms.

15. Urgent assessment and safeguarding

Escalate urgently for suicidal intent, severe self-neglect, psychosis, intoxication or withdrawal, serious physical symptoms or inability to maintain safety. Consider safeguarding and domestic abuse sensitively.

PLAB application: A patient with chest pain, syncope or marked breathlessness needs clinical assessment before assuming panic.

16. PLAB 2 consultation structure

Introduce yourself, establish consent, use open questions, clarify worry versus discrete attacks and triggers, assess impact and risk, rule out red flags, explore ICE, explain the formulation, agree evidence-based options and safety-net.

PLAB application: Use validating language: symptoms are real, common and treatable; avoid saying “it is all in your head”.

Deep Dive

Why panic feels like a heart attack

Sympathetic activation produces tachycardia, sweating and tremor; hyperventilation may cause tingling and dizziness. Symptoms can overlap with serious disease, so clinical assessment remains essential.

Worry and avoidance cycles

Avoidance reduces anxiety in the short term but can maintain fear by preventing corrective learning. CBT addresses catastrophic predictions and safety behaviours.

GAD versus panic disorder

GAD is sustained multi-domain worry, while panic disorder centres on recurrent unexpected surges of intense fear and their consequences. Both can coexist.

Social anxiety versus agoraphobia

Social anxiety is driven by negative evaluation; agoraphobia is driven by fear of difficult escape or lack of help. The same location may be avoided for different reasons.

SSRI initiation and follow-up

Anxiety may transiently increase on starting an SSRI. Discuss adverse effects, delayed improvement, withdrawal risk, interactions and early review, especially for younger adults and those at suicide risk.

Communicating with a frightened patient

Use calm, concrete language; ask permission to explain the fight-or-flight response, validate symptoms and agree practical coping and follow-up plans without premature reassurance.
