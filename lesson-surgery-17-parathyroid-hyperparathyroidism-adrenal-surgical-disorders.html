Parathyroid Disease, Hyperparathyroidism & Adrenal Surgical Disorders

Surgery · Lesson 17 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Parathyroid and adrenal surgery: clinical approach

Do not miss: severe symptomatic hypercalcaemia, adrenal crisis, phaeochromocytoma crisis or hypertensive emergency. Start ABCDE, assess haemodynamic stability and seek senior endocrine/surgical advice immediately.

1 · Calcium and PTH physiology

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) raises serum calcium through bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption and activation of vitamin D; it increases renal phosphate excretion. Interpret adjusted calcium with PTH, renal function, phosphate and vitamin D. In hypercalcaemia, PTH should normally be suppressed: a high or inappropriately normal PTH suggests PTH-dependent hypercalcaemia.

2 · Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT)

Usually a solitary parathyroid adenoma; less often multigland hyperplasia or carcinoma. Often asymptomatic; classic features include renal stones, polyuria, constipation, abdominal discomfort, low mood, cognitive changes, osteoporosis and fragility fractures. Investigations: repeat albumin-adjusted calcium, PTH, renal function, phosphate, vitamin D, bone-density assessment and renal imaging when indicated. Consider familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH), typically low urinary calcium; use calcium/creatinine clearance ratio under specialist interpretation. Localization imaging is for operative planning, not diagnosis (ultrasound, sestamibi or 4D CT where appropriate).

NICE NG132 recommends referral to a surgeon with expertise in parathyroid surgery for appropriate confirmed PHPT, including symptoms, end-organ disease or calcium thresholds; surgical eligibility is individualized. Parathyroidectomy is definitive for suitable patients. Monitor calcium, renal function and bone health if managed non-operatively.

CKD-related mineral bone disorder requires nephrology input; do not treat all elevated PTH with surgery.

4 · Hypercalcaemic emergency

Symptoms: dehydration, vomiting, confusion, weakness, arrhythmias; severe hypercalcaemia can impair renal function. Assess ABCDE, ECG, adjusted calcium, U&Es and fluid status. Give IV isotonic saline when appropriate with careful monitoring, particularly in heart or renal failure; stop exacerbating drugs where appropriate. Consider IV bisphosphonate, calcitonin or other specialist-directed therapy based on cause and severity. Treat the underlying cause. Avoid indiscriminate diuretics.

5 · Post-parathyroidectomy complications

Monitor for neck haematoma (airway emergency), recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and hypocalcaemia. Hungry bone syndrome can cause prolonged hypocalcaemia after successful surgery in patients with marked preoperative bone turnover; requires calcium and often vitamin D replacement with monitoring.

6 · Adrenal incidentaloma and functional tumours

An adrenal mass requires assessment for malignancy risk and hormone excess, usually by specialist endocrine pathways. Consider autonomous cortisol secretion, phaeochromocytoma and primary aldosteronism when indicated. Imaging features, size, growth and hormone results guide surveillance versus adrenalectomy. Do not biopsy an adrenal mass without specialist assessment and excluding phaeochromocytoma.

7 · Phaeochromocytoma

Catecholamine-secreting tumour: episodic headache, sweating, palpitations, pallor and hypertension. Investigate with plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines under appropriate conditions; then localize with imaging. Surgical treatment requires specialist alpha blockade before beta blockade if beta blockade is needed, plus volume preparation. Starting a beta blocker first can precipitate hypertensive crisis. Consider hereditary syndromes including MEN2, VHL, NF1 and SDHx.

8 · Conn syndrome and Cushing syndrome

Primary aldosteronism: resistant hypertension ± hypokalaemia, suppressed renin and high aldosterone-to-renin ratio. Confirm and lateralize under endocrine guidance; unilateral disease may be treated by adrenalectomy, bilateral disease medically with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. Cushing syndrome: proximal weakness, easy bruising, purple striae, central weight gain, diabetes and hypertension; specialist biochemical screening and source localization precede surgery.

9 · Adrenal insufficiency and adrenal crisis

Features: hypotension, vomiting, abdominal pain, weakness, hyponatraemia, sometimes hyperkalaemia (primary adrenal failure). In suspected adrenal crisis give immediate parenteral hydrocortisone and IV saline per emergency protocol; do not wait for confirmatory tests. Patients with bilateral adrenalectomy require lifelong glucocorticoid replacement and sick-day education.

High-yield decision pathways

A · Hypercalcaemia

Confirm adjusted calcium → assess symptoms/ECG/renal function → check PTH → PTH high or inappropriately normal: PHPT/FHH/tertiary causes → PTH suppressed: malignancy, vitamin D and other causes. Severe symptomatic disease: resuscitate and obtain urgent senior review.

B · Confirmed PHPT

Repeat calcium/PTH and evaluate vitamin D, renal and bone health → exclude FHH where relevant → decide on surgery with specialist → localization imaging only after decision to operate → monitor postoperative calcium.

C · Adrenal incidentaloma

Review imaging characteristics and symptoms → endocrine biochemical assessment for cortisol excess, phaeochromocytoma and aldosteronism when indicated → endocrine MDT → surveillance or surgery. Never biopsy before excluding phaeochromocytoma.

D · Phaeochromocytoma

Suggestive symptoms → metanephrines → imaging and genetic evaluation as indicated → specialist alpha blockade and volume preparation → beta blockade only after adequate alpha blockade if needed → adrenalectomy.

E · Suspected adrenal crisis

Hypotension/shock ± vomiting and electrolyte abnormalities → immediate hydrocortisone, IV fluids and urgent admission → investigate cause without delaying treatment.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Kidney stones and hypercalcaemia

A 58-year-old has recurrent stones, calcium 2.88 mmol/L and raised PTH. Diagnosis: likely PHPT. Repeat biochemistry, assess kidney and bone complications, consider FHH and refer to an experienced parathyroid surgeon.

Case 2 — Confusion with severe hypercalcaemia

A dehydrated 72-year-old with malignancy is confused with markedly elevated calcium and suppressed PTH. Approach: ABCDE, ECG, careful IV isotonic fluids and urgent specialist-led calcium-lowering therapy; malignancy-related hypercalcaemia is likely.

Case 3 — Episodic hypertension

A 39-year-old has headaches, sweating, palpitations and episodic hypertension with an adrenal mass. Approach: suspect phaeochromocytoma; order appropriate metanephrines and refer. Never start beta blockade before alpha blockade in preparation for surgery.

Case 4 — Shock after missed steroids

A patient with previous bilateral adrenalectomy has vomiting, hypotension and cannot retain oral medication. Approach: suspected adrenal crisis: immediate parenteral hydrocortisone, IV saline and emergency transfer; do not wait for cortisol testing.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Explaining primary hyperparathyroidism

Station: A 55-year-old with kidney stones is told blood calcium and parathyroid hormone are raised and asks whether surgery is necessary.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and establish concerns and understanding.
  2. Explain the parathyroid glands regulate blood calcium and one may be overactive.
  3. Ask about stones, fractures, constipation, mood, thirst, urinary frequency and kidney disease.
  4. Check medications, supplements, family history and previous blood results.
  5. Explain repeat blood tests and assessment of kidneys and bone strength.
  6. Discuss that surgery can cure PHPT in suitable patients, with specialist assessment of indications and risks.
  7. Explain imaging may help locate an overactive gland after surgery is planned; it does not establish diagnosis alone.
  8. Outline surgery risks: bleeding, voice change, low calcium, and potential need for supplements.
  9. Explain monitoring if surgery is not chosen or indicated and discuss shared decision-making.
  10. Safety-net confusion, severe vomiting, dehydration or marked weakness for urgent assessment.

Patient-friendly phrasing: “The parathyroid glands control calcium. If one is too active, calcium can rise and cause kidney stones or weaker bones. We will check how it affects you and whether removing the overactive gland would help.”

Rapid revision

  1. High calcium with raised or inappropriately normal PTH suggests primary hyperparathyroidism.
  2. Hypercalcaemia of malignancy usually suppresses PTH.
  3. Primary hyperparathyroidism is commonly due to a single adenoma.
  4. Consider FHH when urinary calcium is unusually low.
  5. Parathyroid localization imaging is for surgical planning, not diagnosis.
  6. Secondary hyperparathyroidism commonly occurs in CKD or vitamin D deficiency.
  7. Hungry bone syndrome can cause prolonged postoperative hypocalcaemia.
  8. Severe symptomatic hypercalcaemia needs urgent resuscitation and specialist treatment.
  9. Phaeochromocytoma causes episodic headache, sweating, palpitations and hypertension.
  10. Alpha blockade precedes beta blockade in phaeochromocytoma management.
  11. Primary aldosteronism causes resistant hypertension, sometimes hypokalaemia.
  12. Adrenal crisis requires immediate hydrocortisone and fluids without waiting for tests.
