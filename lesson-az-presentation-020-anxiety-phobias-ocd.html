Anxiety, phobias and obsessive–compulsive disorder: a practical clinical approach

Anxiety can be a normal adaptive response or a clinically significant condition when persistent fear, worry or avoidance causes distress or impairs everyday functioning. GAD is pervasive and difficult-to-control worry; panic disorder involves recurrent unexpected panic attacks and worry or behavioural change related to them; phobias concern specific objects/situations; social anxiety is fear of negative evaluation; OCD consists of intrusive obsessions and/or compulsions. Diagnose patterns, not an isolated symptom.

Article audio
Emergency exclusions before attributing symptoms to anxiety: acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, hypoglycaemia, intoxication/withdrawal, thyrotoxicosis, serious respiratory disease or other acute medical illness when suggested by the presentation. Assess suicide/self-harm risk, severe self-neglect, psychosis and safeguarding concerns. A panic attack is a clinical diagnosis only after appropriate assessment.

2. History: structured psychiatric assessment

  1. Opening: ask what the person fears, how long symptoms have been present, frequency, severity and interference with work, study and relationships.
  2. Triggers: spontaneous attacks versus specific situations; ask avoidance and safety behaviours.
  3. Associated symptoms: palpitations, trembling, breathlessness, sweating, gastrointestinal discomfort, muscle tension, irritability, insomnia and concentration difficulty.
  4. OCD sensitively: ask about contamination, checking, harm, symmetry, unwanted sexual or religious thoughts, reassurance seeking and mental rituals. Distinguish ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts from intent; assess actual risk in context.
  5. Comorbidity: depression, trauma, PTSD, substance use, eating disorders, mania, psychosis and neurodevelopmental conditions.
  6. Physical mimics: chest pain, syncope, weight loss, fever, stimulant use, caffeine, medications and withdrawal.
  7. Risk: suicidal thinking, plans, means, past attempts, self-harm, neglect, risk to others and safeguarding. Ask directly and compassionately.
  8. Context: pregnancy/postpartum, domestic abuse, social stress, occupation, culture, support and prior responses to treatment.

3. Examination and mental-state assessment

Observations and focused physical examination depend on symptoms. For an acute first episode, assess ABCDE and cardiopulmonary or neurological findings where appropriate. Mental-state examination covers appearance, behaviour, speech, mood and affect, thought form/content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. Note avoidance, psychomotor agitation, compulsive checking and functional impairment. Tools such as GAD-7 and PHQ-9 support assessment and monitoring but do not replace diagnosis or risk assessment. For OCD record time spent, resistance, distress and life disruption; specialist scales can help.

OCD distinction: an intrusive thought of harming someone can be frightening and unwanted. Assess intent, planning, psychotic symptoms, protective behaviours, distress and context; do not assume an intrusive thought equals imminent violent intent.

4. Investigations: clinical reasoning rather than blanket testing

There is no blood test for GAD, panic disorder, phobia or OCD. Use ECG, glucose, FBC, TSH, electrolytes, pregnancy testing or toxicology selectively when clinical findings indicate a mimic. For sudden chest pain, syncope or significant dyspnoea, follow the relevant emergency pathway; never rely on a reassuring anxiety history alone. Review drug and alcohol use, including withdrawal and prescribed stimulants.

5. Evidence-based management — match intervention to disorder

Generalised anxiety disorder (NICE CG113)

Explain diagnosis and preferences; offer education and active monitoring for mild symptoms. For persistent impairment, use stepped care including guided CBT-based self-help or psychoeducational groups. At higher-intensity steps offer CBT/applied relaxation or an SSRI, according to preference and suitability. Review early side effects, initial activation, suicidal ideation and adherence. NICE considers sertraline as a cost-effective first consideration for GAD but it was unlicensed for that indication when recommended; check the current BNF, product licence and local prescribing policy. Benzodiazepines are generally not offered for GAD other than short-term crisis use.

Panic disorder

Provide explanation of the panic cycle and behavioural avoidance. CBT is a core treatment; an appropriately licensed antidepressant may be offered when indicated. Benzodiazepines are not recommended for long-term panic disorder treatment. Manage comorbid depression and assess medical causes where clinically needed.

Social anxiety, specific phobia and agoraphobia

NICE CG159 recommends individual disorder-specific CBT for adults with social anxiety, with appropriate medication options when CBT is declined or unavailable. Specific phobias are usually managed using graded exposure-based CBT. For agoraphobia, address avoidance and use tailored CBT (often alongside panic treatment). Do not promise rapid recovery or force exposure without consent.

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (NICE CG31)

Offer CBT incorporating exposure and response prevention (ERP) tailored to severity and impairment. SSRIs are effective pharmacological options; moderate/severe or persistent symptoms may need a combination of CBT/ERP and SSRI. A specialist may consider clomipramine when appropriate after alternatives; review interactions, ECG/cardiac risks and adverse effects. Children and young people require age-appropriate specialist pathways and monitoring.

Shared safety and follow-up

Agree goals, an accessible crisis plan, medication safety counselling, follow-up and monitoring for deterioration. Discuss work/school adjustments, sleep, caffeine/stimulant reduction and support networks. Avoid presenting simple relaxation as a substitute for evidence-based treatment where symptoms are severe.

PLAB prescribing: an SSRI is not an immediate treatment for a medical emergency, may initially increase anxiety, and requires counselling about delayed benefit, discontinuation symptoms, interactions and suicidal thoughts particularly in younger people. Individualise choice and consult current BNF/NICE and local guidelines.

6. Five worked PLAB cases

Case A — Constant worry

A 32-year-old has months of worry about money, family and health, poor sleep, restlessness and impaired work functioning. No single trigger. Consider GAD. Assess depression, safety and physical contributors, then use stepped-care CBT-based approaches or medication by preference.

Case B — Sudden palpitations

A 45-year-old presents with first-ever chest pressure, sweating and palpitations. Do not prematurely diagnose panic. Assess observations, ECG and acute cardiopulmonary causes as indicated. Once serious causes are reasonably excluded, explore panic symptoms and follow-up.

Case C — Fear of public scrutiny

A 19-year-old avoids seminars because of overwhelming fear of humiliation and has stopped attending university. Social anxiety disorder is possible; consider targeted individual CBT and assess depression and functioning.

Case D — Contamination rituals

A 24-year-old spends four hours washing and cleaning to neutralise distressing contamination thoughts and knows the fear is excessive. OCD: explore distress, insight and impairment; offer CBT with ERP, consider SSRI according to severity and preference.

Case E — Intrusive harm thoughts

A parent reports unwanted images of harming their baby, feels horrified, avoids knives and seeks reassurance. Take a careful perinatal mental-health history: distinguish ego-dystonic obsessions from psychosis or actual intent, assess suicide/infant safety and arrange suitable specialist care.

Deep Dive · neurobiology / clinical psychology / pharmacology

How anxiety and compulsions become self-reinforcing

1. Threat and arousal systems

The amygdala and connected salience networks detect potential threats, while medial prefrontal circuits and hippocampal context regulate appraisal and extinction learning. Sympathetic activation can cause tachycardia, sweating and tremor. Autonomic symptoms are not specific to psychiatric disease.

2. Panic and catastrophic misinterpretation

Benign bodily sensations can be interpreted as danger, provoking further autonomic arousal. Hyperventilation can lower carbon dioxide and contribute to tingling/lightheadedness. Cognitive restructuring and interoceptive exposure can target this cycle, but physical red flags still require appropriate evaluation.

3. Avoidance learning and phobias

Avoidance reduces distress immediately (negative reinforcement) but prevents corrective learning. Supported, consent-based graded exposure allows fear extinction or inhibitory learning without unnecessary reliance on safety behaviours.

4. OCD cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuits

Networks involving orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate and striatum participate in error/uncertainty processing and repetitive action. Obsessions evoke distress; compulsions momentarily relieve it, maintaining the loop. ERP involves facing feared cues while refraining from rituals, with specialist tailoring.

5. Why SSRIs can help

SSRIs inhibit serotonin reuptake, but clinical benefit usually takes weeks and depends on complex network adaptation. Adverse effects include nausea, sleep change, activation and sexual dysfunction; some medicines carry interactions and QT or bleeding considerations. OCD sometimes needs an adequate longer trial than uncomplicated depression, directed by specialist guidance.

6. High-yield diagnostic traps

  1. Chest pain is not automatically panic: consider ACS, arrhythmia and PE when clinically plausible.
  2. Intrusive image ≠ intent: assess ego-dystonic character, actual wishes, planning and psychotic features.
  3. Screen scales ≠ diagnosis: combine validated measures with clinical interview.
  4. Do not give routine long-term benzodiazepines: dependence, sedation and withdrawal are important risks.
  5. Different disorders need different CBT: ERP is key for OCD; social-anxiety CBT and graded phobia exposure are tailored.

Exam framing

Recognise the phenotype; exclude indicated medical mimics and imminent risk; name a NICE-consistent disorder-specific treatment; provide follow-up and safety-net advice. See NICE CG113, CG159 and CG31 for complete criteria and prescribing decisions.
