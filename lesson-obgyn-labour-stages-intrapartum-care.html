Labour, Stages of Labour & Intrapartum Care

Emergency warning: Maternal collapse, severe bleeding, seizures or sustained fetal bradycardia require immediate obstetric emergency response.

What is labour?

Labour is the process of regular uterine contractions producing progressive cervical effacement and dilatation, followed by birth of the baby and placenta. Distinguish spontaneous onset from induction and augmentation.

PLAB application: A contraction pattern alone does not prove established labour; assess cervical change and the whole clinical picture.

Latent first stage

The latent first stage involves painful contractions and cervical changes, including effacement and dilatation up to 4 cm. It can be prolonged, especially in a first pregnancy. Offer reassurance, hydration, comfort measures and individualised assessment.

PLAB application: Do not label slow latent labour as established-labour delay.

Established first stage

NICE describes established first stage as regular painful contractions with progressive cervical dilatation from 4 cm. Assess maternal observations, contractions, fetal wellbeing, vaginal loss and progress.

PLAB application: Document findings and explain any examination before obtaining consent.

Second stage

The second stage begins at full cervical dilatation and ends with birth. Passive and active phases may occur. Observe pushing, descent, maternal condition and fetal wellbeing; consider position changes and support.

PLAB application: Escalate delay or fetal compromise; thresholds depend on parity and epidural status.

Third stage

The third stage begins after birth and ends with placental delivery. Active management includes a prophylactic uterotonic, cord clamping/cutting and controlled cord traction after signs of placental separation by a trained clinician. Physiological management may be chosen after discussion.

PLAB application: Know the benefits of active management in reducing postpartum haemorrhage.

Initial intrapartum assessment

Confirm gestation, parity, pregnancy risks, presentation, fetal movements, membrane status, bleeding, contractions and preferences. Check temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory status and fetal heart. Review antenatal notes and infection risks.

PLAB application: Significant bleeding, seizures, shock or abnormal fetal heart need immediate escalation.

Abdominal and vaginal examination

Abdominal palpation assesses lie, presentation, engagement and contraction pattern. With consent and appropriate indications, vaginal examination assesses dilatation, effacement, station, membranes and presenting part. Offer privacy and a chaperone as appropriate.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary vaginal examinations, particularly after rupture of membranes.

Monitoring fetal wellbeing

For low-risk established labour, intermittent auscultation is generally offered using recommended intervals and duration. Continuous cardiotocography (CTG) is used when risk factors or concerns arise; reassess the indication and interpret the full clinical context.

PLAB application: An abnormal CTG is not a diagnosis; correct reversible factors and call for senior review.

Maternal monitoring and documentation

Record pulse, blood pressure, temperature and other observations at appropriate intervals; document contractions, progress, fetal monitoring and maternal preferences. Use local labour care records and escalation pathways.

PLAB application: Recognise maternal tachycardia, fever or hypotension as possible deterioration.

Pain relief and supportive care

Offer continuous support, movement, positioning, water immersion when suitable, breathing techniques, nitrous oxide/oxygen (Entonox), opioids and epidural analgesia after shared decision-making. Explain benefits and adverse effects.

PLAB application: An epidural may affect monitoring and mobility but is not automatically a reason for caesarean delivery.

Membranes, amniotic fluid and infection

Note whether membranes are intact or ruptured, time of rupture and liquor colour. Meconium-stained liquor prompts risk assessment; fever, uterine tenderness and fetal tachycardia may indicate intra-amniotic infection.

PLAB application: Assess possible infection promptly and follow local antibiotic and neonatal plans.

Slow progress and augmentation

Assess contractions, maternal/fetal wellbeing, position and cervical change before diagnosing delay. Options may include support, amniotomy and oxytocin under protocol, with discussion of risks and continuous monitoring when indicated.

PLAB application: Never prescribe oxytocin simply because labour seems slow without reassessment.

Assisted vaginal birth

Ventouse or forceps may be considered for delay in the second stage or fetal compromise when prerequisites are met: full dilatation, known position, engaged head, adequate analgesia, consent and an experienced operator.

PLAB application: If vaginal birth is unsafe or unlikely, consider urgent caesarean delivery.

Perineal care and birth

Support controlled birth of the head, assess for shoulder dystocia and inspect for tears after delivery. Discuss episiotomy only when clinically indicated, provide analgesia and arrange appropriate repair.

PLAB application: Third- or fourth-degree tears require specialist assessment and repair.

Immediate newborn and maternal care

Dry and warm the newborn, assess breathing and tone, encourage skin-to-skin contact and feeding when safe, and follow newborn resuscitation guidance if needed. Assess maternal blood loss, uterine tone, placenta completeness and vital signs.

PLAB application: Quantify blood loss and recognise postpartum haemorrhage early.

Emergency recognition and communication

Emergencies include shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, uterine rupture, fetal bradycardia, major haemorrhage and maternal collapse. Call the obstetric emergency team, start ABCDE and use structured SBAR handover.

PLAB application: Explain actions clearly, document times and seek senior obstetric and anaesthetic support.

PLAB 2 — Labour admission consultation

Introduce yourself and confirm the patient’s identity, gestation and concerns. Ask about contraction onset/frequency, waters breaking, colour of liquor, bleeding, fetal movements, parity, complications and birth preferences. Check maternal observations and fetal heart, explain examination and obtain consent. Discuss pain relief and the care plan, involve a senior clinician for risk factors, and safety-net clearly.

Deep Dive

Understanding labour progress

Cervical dilatation is one part of labour progress. Parity, contractions, fetal position, descent, maternal wellbeing and patient preferences matter. Avoid rigid decisions based on a single examination.

Intermittent auscultation versus CTG

Intermittent auscultation is usually offered in low-risk labour. CTG provides a continuous tracing but can increase interventions and must be interpreted alongside the clinical picture. Follow current NICE guidance for timing and escalation.

Pain management and shared decisions

Non-drug approaches can be combined with pharmacological analgesia. Discuss efficacy, mobility, nausea, sedation, fetal/newborn effects and epidural-related monitoring, without coercion.

When labour does not progress

Confirm whether the patient is in latent or established labour, review the frequency and strength of contractions, fetal position and wellbeing. Amniotomy and oxytocin require individual assessment, discussion and monitoring.

Operative vaginal birth and caesarean

Assisted birth is not appropriate unless prerequisites are met. Discuss risks, benefits and alternatives, and ensure a safe backup plan if the attempt is unsuccessful.

Postpartum haemorrhage and neonatal transition

After birth, rapidly identify uterine atony, retained placenta, genital tract trauma or coagulation disorders when bleeding is excessive. At the same time, maintain newborn warmth and recognise infants needing resuscitation.
