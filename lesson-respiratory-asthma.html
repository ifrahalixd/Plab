Recognise chronic PAD and acute limb ischaemia, interpret ABPI, identify threatened limbs and select safe UK referral and treatment pathways.

Emergency rule: A suddenly painful, cold, numb or weak limb is a vascular emergency. Call the vascular team immediately; do not delay for routine testing. Do not use this educational page as a clinical protocol.

Asthma: definition and mechanisms

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable respiratory symptoms and variable expiratory airflow limitation. Airway hyperresponsiveness, bronchoconstriction, mucus and inflammation contribute.

Clinical application: Typical symptoms are wheeze, cough, chest tightness and breathlessness that vary over time and intensity, often at night or early morning.

Recognising patterns and triggers

Exercise, viral infections, allergens, smoke, pollution and occupational exposures may provoke symptoms. Atopy and family history support the diagnosis but are not diagnostic.

Clinical application: Ask about day/night variability, triggers, work-related changes, prior admissions, steroid courses and reliever use.

Diagnosis in adults

Do not diagnose asthma solely from symptoms. NICE/BTS/SIGN NG245 recommends a structured history and objective testing using an age-appropriate pathway.

Clinical application: Tests may include blood eosinophils, FeNO, bronchodilator reversibility, peak-flow variability or bronchial challenge depending on availability and results. Normal tests between episodes do not always exclude asthma.

Spirometry and reversibility

Spirometry assesses airflow obstruction and response to a bronchodilator. FEV1, FVC and their ratio should be interpreted against appropriate reference ranges.

Clinical application: Follow the current NG245 diagnostic algorithm and local quality-assured testing; bronchodilator reversibility thresholds differ by age and test framework.

Peak expiratory flow

Serial peak-flow readings can demonstrate variability and are useful for monitoring or action plans. Technique, effort and personal best matter.

Clinical application: Record morning and evening readings as directed, and interpret alongside symptoms and objective investigations.

Long-term treatment in people aged 12 and over

Current UK joint guidance emphasises anti-inflammatory treatment and avoiding SABA-only management. For newly diagnosed asthma in people aged 12 and over, as-needed low-dose ICS/formoterol (AIR) is a recommended initial approach where suitable.

Clinical application: If symptoms are uncontrolled, step up to low-dose maintenance-and-reliever therapy (MART) and reassess adherence, technique, triggers and diagnosis. Prescribing depends on licensed products and the local formulary.

Children and age-specific pathways

Management for children aged 5–11 and under 5 follows separate pathways and inhaler/device considerations. Do not apply the adult AIR/MART pathway to every child.

Clinical application: Use age-specific NG245 recommendations and review spacer technique, family understanding and growth monitoring when indicated.

Inhaler technique and adherence

Poor inhaler technique and inconsistent inhaled corticosteroid use are common causes of poor control. Spacers can improve delivery from pressurised metered-dose inhalers.

Clinical application: Demonstrate and ask for a teach-back demonstration at every review; check device suitability, adherence and side effects.

Asthma control and follow-up

Review daytime symptoms, night waking, activity limitation, exacerbations, reliever use, lung function when relevant and patient priorities.

Clinical application: Provide a personalised written asthma action plan and arrange structured follow-up, particularly after attacks or treatment changes.

Acute asthma exacerbation

An attack is worsening airway obstruction with increased symptoms and potentially hypoxaemia. Assess ABCDE, ability to speak, respiratory rate, pulse, oxygen saturation and peak flow if feasible without delaying treatment.

Clinical application: Treat promptly using local acute asthma protocol, inhaled bronchodilator therapy, oxygen titrated appropriately, systemic corticosteroids when indicated and frequent reassessment.

Life-threatening asthma

Red flags include silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, exhaustion, altered consciousness, hypotension, severe hypoxaemia or arrhythmia. A normal or rising PaCO2 in a very unwell patient can indicate fatigue.

Clinical application: Call emergency/critical-care help immediately; do not be reassured by reduced wheeze. Follow emergency protocols and consider adjuncts under senior guidance.

Occupational asthma and differential diagnosis

Adult-onset asthma may relate to workplace exposures. Alternative diagnoses include COPD, vocal cord dysfunction, heart failure, bronchiectasis, reflux and dysfunctional breathing.

Clinical application: Ask whether symptoms improve on weekends or holidays; refer for specialist occupational evaluation without unnecessary delay.

Pregnancy and asthma

Good asthma control is important during pregnancy. Stopping preventer medication can increase risk of exacerbations.

Clinical application: Continue appropriate prescribed asthma treatment with maternity/respiratory advice and follow pregnancy-specific guidance.

PLAB 2: counselling and safety net

Explain asthma as a variable inflammatory airway disease; distinguish reliever and anti-inflammatory medicines, demonstrate inhaler use and discuss triggers.

Clinical application: Agree a written action plan and explain when to seek urgent help for severe breathlessness, inability to speak, drowsiness or poor response to reliever treatment.

PLAB 2: focused OSCE approach

  1. Introduce yourself and establish the patient's concerns and current symptom control.
  2. Ask about wheeze, night waking, reliever use, attacks, triggers and work exposures.
  3. Explain objective tests and the rationale for anti-inflammatory treatment.
  4. Demonstrate inhaler and spacer technique and use teach-back.
  5. Agree a written action plan, follow-up and emergency safety-net.

Advanced asthma reasoning

Deep Dive

Airway inflammation

Type 2 inflammation often involves eosinophils and cytokine signalling. Other inflammatory phenotypes exist; asthma is heterogeneous.

Bronchoconstriction

Airway smooth muscle contraction, mucosal swelling and mucus reduce airflow, especially during expiration.

Why ICS matters

Inhaled corticosteroids reduce airway inflammation and future exacerbation risk. Reliever bronchodilation alone does not address underlying inflammation.

Why ICS/formoterol can be a reliever

Formoterol has a rapid onset, and combining it with ICS gives anti-inflammatory treatment whenever symptoms prompt use. Only appropriate licensed combinations are suitable for AIR or MART.

Severe asthma physiology

Air trapping increases work of breathing; respiratory muscle fatigue can cause CO2 retention and respiratory failure.

Biologics

Selected severe asthma phenotypes may benefit from specialist biologic therapy after optimisation and phenotyping.

Diagnostic uncertainty

Normal tests between episodes do not necessarily exclude asthma. Follow the age-specific NG245 algorithm and seek specialist input where needed.
