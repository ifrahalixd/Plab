Myasthenia Gravis & Neuromuscular Junction Disorders

PLAB priority: In suspected MG, new breathlessness, weak cough or difficulty swallowing is an emergency: assess ABCDE and escalate immediately.

01. Neuromuscular junction basics

Motor nerve impulses release acetylcholine (ACh), which binds postsynaptic receptors and initiates muscle contraction. Neuromuscular junction disorders impair transmission rather than damaging sensory nerves.

PLAB application: Fatigable weakness with preserved sensation should prompt a neuromuscular junction differential.

02. Myasthenia gravis (MG): definition

MG is an autoimmune disorder causing fluctuating, fatigable skeletal muscle weakness. Antibodies most often target the ACh receptor; some patients have MuSK or other antibodies, and some are seronegative.

PLAB application: Symptoms worsen with use and often improve after rest.

03. Typical presenting features

Ptosis and binocular diplopia are common. Bulbar symptoms include nasal speech, dysarthria, chewing fatigue and dysphagia; neck and proximal limb weakness may occur. Pupils are normally spared.

PLAB application: Ask about evening deterioration, chewing towards the end of meals and difficulty holding the head up.

04. Ocular versus generalised MG

Ocular MG affects eye muscles; generalised MG affects additional skeletal muscles including bulbar, limb or respiratory muscles. Ocular disease can generalise, especially early in its course.

PLAB application: Do not assume isolated ptosis is harmless; seek neurological assessment.

05. Focused clinical examination

Assess variable ptosis, sustained upgaze fatigue, ocular movements and diplopia; listen for dysarthria, assess palate, cough and swallowing safety, neck flexion and proximal power. Reflexes and sensation are usually normal.

PLAB application: Stop testing and escalate if breathlessness, weak cough or swallowing difficulty is present.

06. Red flags and myasthenic crisis

Myasthenic crisis is life-threatening respiratory and/or bulbar weakness requiring urgent airway assessment and often ventilatory support. Warning signs include dyspnoea, orthopnoea, weak cough, rapidly worsening dysphagia or inability to handle secretions.

PLAB application: Emergency admission and critical-care/neurology input; normal pulse oximetry does not exclude impending ventilatory failure.

07. Initial investigations

Request AChR antibodies and, if negative with ongoing suspicion, MuSK antibodies or further specialist testing. Repetitive nerve stimulation and single-fibre EMG may support diagnosis. Antibody-negative results do not rule out MG.

PLAB application: A clear clinical history and neurological referral are central.

08. Thymus and imaging

Thymic hyperplasia and thymoma are associated with MG. Chest CT or MRI is commonly used after diagnosis to assess for thymoma; thymectomy decisions are specialist-led and depend on disease phenotype.

PLAB application: Think thymoma when MG is diagnosed, but avoid implying every patient requires surgery.

09. Symptomatic treatment

Pyridostigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, can improve neuromuscular transmission. Adverse effects include abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, sweating and increased secretions. Dose adjustment requires clinical review.

PLAB application: Medication is supportive; persistent generalised disease often needs immunotherapy.

10. Long-term disease control

Neurologists may use corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunosuppressants, selected biologics or thymectomy in appropriate patients. Initial steroid treatment can transiently worsen weakness in some patients and requires specialist planning.

PLAB application: Explain monitoring, vaccination considerations and individualised treatment.

11. Treating acute deterioration

Assess ABCDE, swallowing, respiratory effort and serial respiratory mechanics where available. Treat precipitants such as infection. IV immunoglobulin or plasma exchange may be used for severe exacerbation/crisis alongside supportive and critical care.

PLAB application: Do not delay escalation while awaiting blood tests or antibody results.

12. Medication and anaesthetic precautions

Certain medications may worsen MG, including some antibiotics, magnesium and neuromuscular blocking agents. Risk varies by drug and patient; check current specialist guidance before prescribing or stopping medicines.

PLAB application: Flag MG during admission, prescribing, procedures and anaesthesia.

13. Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS)

LEMS is usually associated with antibodies to presynaptic P/Q-type voltage-gated calcium channels. It causes proximal leg weakness, depressed reflexes and autonomic symptoms such as dry mouth; power/reflexes may briefly improve after exercise. It may be paraneoplastic, especially with small-cell lung cancer.

PLAB application: Contrast LEMS with MG: reflexes reduced and autonomic features common in LEMS.

14. Botulism and other junction disorders

Botulism causes descending flaccid paralysis with cranial nerve dysfunction and often autonomic symptoms after exposure to botulinum toxin. It is an emergency requiring urgent public-health and specialist management, with antitoxin when indicated. Congenital myasthenic syndromes are inherited, not autoimmune.

PLAB application: Consider botulism with acute descending weakness, pupillary abnormalities or relevant food/wound exposure.

15. Differential diagnosis

Distinguish MG from thyroid eye disease, cranial nerve palsy, motor neurone disease, inflammatory myopathy, GBS and brainstem pathology. Sensory loss, fixed deficits, abnormal pupils or marked reflex changes suggest alternatives.

PLAB application: Sudden diplopia with other focal neurological signs requires urgent stroke assessment.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain MG as a treatable disorder of nerve-to-muscle signalling, often fluctuating with fatigue. Discuss medication adherence, planned specialist review and triggers. Advise emergency care for breathing difficulty, choking or rapidly worsening speech/swallowing.

PLAB application: Demonstrate clear communication, avoid promising cure and provide a specific emergency plan.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why weakness fluctuates

The neuromuscular junction has a safety margin: repeated activity can deplete effective transmission when ACh receptor function is impaired. Rest may temporarily restore performance.

AChR versus MuSK MG

AChR antibody MG commonly includes ocular and generalised weakness; MuSK-associated MG can have prominent bulbar, facial and neck involvement. Testing and treatment choices are specialist-led.

Respiratory physiology in crisis

Weak respiratory muscles reduce ventilation and cough. Hypercapnia, secretion retention and aspiration can develop even when early pulse oximetry appears reassuring; serial clinical and physiological assessments matter.

How neurophysiology helps

Repetitive nerve stimulation may show a decremental response in MG. Single-fibre EMG can detect increased jitter, but results require interpretation in clinical context.

Presynaptic versus postsynaptic dysfunction

MG impairs postsynaptic ACh receptor signalling; LEMS reduces presynaptic ACh release through calcium-channel autoimmunity. The difference helps explain LEMS hyporeflexia and facilitation after exercise.

Why medications matter

Some drugs can interfere with neuromuscular transmission. Antibiotic and anaesthetic choices should be checked with current MG-specific guidance rather than applying indiscriminate blanket bans.
