Urinary Incontinence, Urogynaecology & Pelvic Floor Disorders

Red flags: new urinary retention with saddle anaesthesia or leg weakness, visible haematuria, severe pelvic pain or suspected urinary fistula require urgent or specialist assessment.

Clinical overview

Urinary incontinence is involuntary leakage of urine. Distinguish stress, urgency, mixed, overflow and functional incontinence. Symptoms can affect sleep, confidence, intimacy and daily activities.

PLAB application: Start with patient goals and exclude infection, retention, haematuria and neurological red flags.

Stress urinary incontinence

Leakage with exertion, coughing, laughing or sneezing results from impaired urethral closure or pelvic support. Risk factors include pregnancy, vaginal birth, obesity, chronic cough and ageing.

PLAB application: Cough-associated leakage without urgency suggests stress incontinence.

Urgency urinary incontinence and OAB

Urgency incontinence is leakage associated with a sudden compelling desire to void. Overactive bladder (OAB) involves urgency, usually frequency and nocturia, with or without leakage, in the absence of infection or other evident pathology.

PLAB application: Ask about urgency, frequency, nocturia and bladder irritants.

Mixed incontinence

Mixed incontinence combines stress leakage and urgency leakage. Determine the predominant symptom because initial treatment should target what is most troublesome.

PLAB application: Do not assume every patient needs medication or surgery.

Overflow and chronic retention

Incomplete bladder emptying may cause dribbling, poor stream, hesitancy and recurrent infection. Causes include obstruction, prolapse, neurological disease and medications. Post-void residual assessment is important when voiding dysfunction is suspected.

PLAB application: Painful acute urinary retention requires urgent assessment and bladder drainage.

Focused history

Ask onset, triggers, frequency, fluid and caffeine intake, nocturia, dysuria, haematuria, recurrent UTIs, bowel symptoms, constipation, obstetric and surgical history, medications, neurological symptoms, prolapse symptoms and sexual impact.

PLAB application: Ask about pregnancy possibility and impact on quality of life.

Examination and consent

Perform appropriate abdominal and pelvic examination with consent and chaperone; assess pelvic masses, atrophy, prolapse and pelvic floor contraction. A focused neurological examination is appropriate if neurological symptoms are present.

PLAB application: Avoid unnecessary invasive examination and preserve dignity.

First-line tests

Use urine dipstick as appropriate for blood, glucose, protein, leukocytes and nitrites; send culture when indicated. A bladder diary for at least 3 days is useful. Assess residual urine when voiding dysfunction or recurrent UTI is suspected.

PLAB application: Routine cystoscopy or urodynamics is not needed for uncomplicated initial assessment.

Bladder diary

Record fluid intake, voiding times and volumes, urgency, leakage episodes and pad use for at least 3 days covering usual activities.

PLAB application: A diary helps classify symptoms and measure response to treatment.

Pelvic floor muscle training

For stress or mixed incontinence, offer a supervised pelvic floor muscle training programme for at least 3 months, including at least 8 contractions performed 3 times daily where appropriate. Confirm ability to contract correctly.

PLAB application: Pelvic floor training is first-line for stress incontinence.

Bladder training and lifestyle

Offer bladder training lasting at least 6 weeks for urgency or mixed incontinence. Address excess caffeine, fluid intake extremes, constipation and weight management where appropriate.

PLAB application: Behavioural measures come before drug escalation for many patients.

Medicines for overactive bladder

If conservative measures are insufficient, discuss antimuscarinic medicines or a beta-3 agonist depending on contraindications, interactions, adverse effects and current NICE guidance. Antimuscarinics can cause dry mouth, constipation and cognitive adverse effects; monitor response.

PLAB application: Check anticholinergic burden, glaucoma considerations, retention risk and blood pressure where relevant.

Stress incontinence procedures

After adequate conservative treatment, specialist options may include colposuspension, autologous fascial sling or urethral bulking. Surgical mesh options require careful discussion of current restrictions, risks and specialist pathways.

PLAB application: Do not present surgery as automatic first-line treatment.

Pelvic organ prolapse

Prolapse of the anterior, posterior or apical vaginal compartment may cause a vaginal bulge, pressure, urinary or bowel symptoms. Options include observation, supervised pelvic floor training, vaginal pessary and selected surgery.

PLAB application: A bulge is the most characteristic symptom; offer shared decision-making.

Red flags and referrals

Visible haematuria, recurrent unexplained UTI, significant voiding dysfunction, suspected fistula, pelvic mass, severe prolapse, neurological deficit or pain require further evaluation or referral as appropriate. New saddle anaesthesia or bladder dysfunction with back pain raises concern for cauda equina syndrome.

PLAB application: New urinary retention with saddle numbness is an emergency.

PLAB 2 counselling

Use sensitive, non-judgemental language; ask permission before intimate questions. Explain the likely type, diary and urine testing, conservative options, review plan and red flags. Discuss pads as support rather than definitive treatment.

PLAB application: Summarise, check understanding and agree a realistic follow-up plan.

PLAB 2 — Urinary leakage consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, obtain consent, explore the type and impact of leakage, assess red flags and relevant history, explain a bladder diary and urine testing, and offer first-line pelvic floor or bladder training as appropriate. Agree review and safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Mechanisms

Stress incontinence reflects inadequate urethral closure during raised abdominal pressure; urgency incontinence involves bladder storage symptoms and may relate to detrusor overactivity, though symptoms do not prove a urodynamic diagnosis.

NICE conservative care

NICE NG123 supports supervised pelvic floor muscle training for at least 3 months for stress/mixed incontinence, and bladder training for at least 6 weeks for urgency/mixed incontinence. Use individualised goals and follow-up.

Drug decisions

Antimuscarinic medicines can cause dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision and cognitive adverse effects; assess retention and overall anticholinergic burden. Beta-3 agonists have different cautions and prescribing criteria. Check current NICE and BNF.

Prolapse choices

Treatment depends on symptoms and patient preference, not anatomy alone. Vaginal pessaries need fitting and follow-up; surgical options require discussion of recurrence, urinary symptoms and complications.

Referral and emergencies

New bladder dysfunction with saddle sensory change, bilateral leg symptoms or severe back pain can indicate cauda equina syndrome. Haematuria, recurrent infection, pain and suspected fistula require tailored investigation.

PLAB 2 framework

Acknowledge embarrassment, assess how symptoms affect life, explain likely mechanism in plain language, discuss non-surgical options first, confirm patient priorities and give explicit safety-net advice.
