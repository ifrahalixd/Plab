Alcohol Use Disorder, Withdrawal & Substance Misuse

PLAB priority: Recognise severe alcohol withdrawal, treat suspected Wernicke encephalopathy promptly, prioritise ventilation in opioid overdose and practise non-judgemental risk assessment.

01. Recognising harmful alcohol use

Explore quantity in UK units, frequency, binges, morning drinking, loss of control, cravings, withdrawal and consequences. One UK unit equals 8 g or 10 mL pure alcohol. AUDIT-C can screen; full AUDIT helps assess severity.

PLAB application: Ask permission, avoid judgement and clarify the last drink.

02. Dependence and withdrawal risk

Dependence may involve tolerance, impaired control, prioritising alcohol and physiological withdrawal. Abrupt cessation after heavy sustained use can be dangerous. Prior withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens increase risk.

PLAB application: Never advise a highly dependent person to stop suddenly without an assessment and a safe withdrawal plan.

03. Alcohol withdrawal presentation

Symptoms often start 6–24 hours after reduction: tremor, sweating, anxiety, tachycardia, nausea, insomnia and agitation. Seizures often occur within 6–48 hours; delirium tremens commonly develops later, around 48–72 hours or beyond. Timing varies.

PLAB application: Check observations, glucose, hydration, mental state and alternative causes of agitation.

04. Severe withdrawal and delirium tremens

Delirium tremens features fluctuating confusion, disorientation, severe autonomic hyperactivity, agitation and sometimes hallucinations. It is life-threatening. Withdrawal seizures, severe autonomic instability or delirium require urgent hospital care.

PLAB application: Use ABCDE, emergency escalation and monitored benzodiazepine-based treatment according to protocol.

05. Withdrawal assessment

Ask about previous complicated withdrawal, daily intake, comorbidity, pregnancy, other sedatives and social support. CIWA-Ar can help monitor symptoms in appropriate settings but must not replace clinical judgement, particularly in delirious patients.

PLAB application: Consider admission if high-risk, medically unwell, unsafe home setting or unable to engage with monitoring.

06. Assisted alcohol withdrawal

For alcohol withdrawal, chlordiazepoxide or diazepam are commonly used in structured regimens with monitoring; consider reduced doses or alternatives in significant liver disease per specialist/local protocol. Avoid unsupervised prescribing.

PLAB application: Withdrawal treatment is not the same as long-term relapse prevention.

07. Thiamine and Wernicke encephalopathy

Chronic alcohol misuse and malnutrition predispose to thiamine deficiency. Wernicke encephalopathy may present with confusion, ataxia and eye signs, but the classic triad is often incomplete. Suspected Wernicke requires urgent parenteral thiamine.

PLAB application: Give thiamine promptly; do not delay emergency glucose for hypoglycaemia.

08. Korsakoff syndrome

Untreated thiamine deficiency may lead to persistent amnesia and confabulation. Prevention through early recognition and adequate parenteral thiamine in at-risk presentations is essential.

PLAB application: Ask about nutritional status and memory difficulties.

09. Physical complications

Alcohol can cause liver disease, pancreatitis, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, neuropathy, gastritis, hypertension, falls and cancers. Check appropriate blood tests and consider liver fibrosis assessment when indicated.

PLAB application: A normal liver enzyme result does not exclude harmful alcohol use.

10. Psychiatric and social assessment

Assess depression, anxiety, suicidality, domestic abuse, child safeguarding, housing, employment and driving. Alcohol intoxication can increase impulsivity and self-harm risk. Reassess mental state once sober when safe.

PLAB application: Use a non-stigmatising formulation and ask about immediate safety.

11. Long-term alcohol treatment

After stabilisation, offer psychosocial treatment, relapse prevention and specialist support. Acamprosate or oral naltrexone may be considered with psychological intervention after successful withdrawal; disulfiram is an alternative in selected patients with appropriate supervision.

PLAB application: Review contraindications, liver function, opioid use and patient goals before medication.

12. Opioid intoxication

Typical findings include reduced consciousness, slow breathing and pinpoint pupils, though pupils may vary. Airway and ventilation are priorities; give naloxone and monitor for recurrence because naloxone may wear off before the opioid.

PLAB application: Call emergency help and provide ventilation/CPR as indicated.

13. Opioid withdrawal and dependence treatment

Withdrawal causes yawning, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, sweating, mydriasis, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps and piloerection. It is distressing; assess dehydration and comorbidity. Methadone or buprenorphine treatment is specialist-supervised.

PLAB application: Distinguish opioid withdrawal from overdose: pupils and breathing differ.

14. Benzodiazepines and other sedatives

Benzodiazepine withdrawal can cause anxiety, insomnia, tremor, perceptual disturbance and seizures. Abrupt stopping after dependence may be dangerous; supervised gradual reduction is usually required. Mixed alcohol/sedative use increases overdose risk.

PLAB application: Ask specifically about prescribed, borrowed and illicit sedatives.

15. Stimulants, cannabis and other substances

Cocaine/amphetamines can cause agitation, tachycardia, chest pain, psychosis and hyperthermia; assess urgently for cardiac or neurological complications. Cannabis can impair cognition and may trigger anxiety or psychotic symptoms. Ask about synthetic substances and polysubstance use.

PLAB application: In stimulant-associated chest pain, perform urgent medical assessment rather than assuming panic.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and safety-netting

Open with confidentiality and its safeguarding limits, use motivational interviewing, ask what matters to the patient, agree a realistic plan and arrange follow-up. Discuss emergency symptoms, overdose prevention and local specialist referral.

PLAB application: Explain that seizures, confusion, severe shaking, breathing difficulty or reduced consciousness need urgent emergency help.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

GABA and glutamate adaptation

Alcohol enhances inhibitory GABAergic effects and suppresses excitatory glutamatergic signalling. Chronic exposure drives compensatory changes; abrupt withdrawal unmasks CNS hyperexcitability and can cause seizures.

Why withdrawal is dangerous

Autonomic activation, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance and seizures can coexist with infection or head injury. Confusion must not automatically be attributed to alcohol.

Thiamine and cerebral energy metabolism

Thiamine is a cofactor for neuronal glucose metabolism. Deficiency can injure vulnerable brain regions, causing acute Wernicke encephalopathy and chronic memory impairment.

Naloxone pharmacology

Naloxone competitively reverses opioid effects, especially respiratory depression. Its action may be shorter than the causative opioid, so reassessment and observation are essential.

Harm reduction and motivation

Non-judgemental, collaborative discussions can support engagement even when abstinence is not an immediate goal. Address overdose prevention, infection risks, safe housing and safeguarding.

PLAB differential reasoning

Tremor and agitation may also result from sepsis, thyrotoxicosis, hypoglycaemia or stimulant intoxication. Sedation and confusion may reflect overdose, stroke, head injury or metabolic illness.
