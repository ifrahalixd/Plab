Children, Adolescents & Parental Responsibility

Core principles follow GMC Decision making and consent and Good medical practice. The age thresholds and legal rules below primarily concern England and Wales; Scotland and Northern Ireland have distinct legislation. Apply current local guidance in practice.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish consent by a 16–17-year-old, Gillick competence under 16, and parental responsibility.
  • Assess decision-specific understanding, voluntariness, and safeguarding.
  • Respond appropriately to parental disagreement and refusal of essential treatment.
  • Communicate privately and respectfully with young patients while involving families appropriately.

1 · Who can consent?

Age 16–17: In England and Wales, a young person is presumed able to consent to their own treatment under section 8 of the Family Law Reform Act 1969; assess capacity where concerns arise. Their refusal of life-saving treatment may be overridden by a court in some circumstances, unlike an adult with capacity.

Under 16: Assess Gillick competence: can the child understand the nature, purpose, benefits, risks, alternatives and consequences of the specific decision? No fixed age makes every child competent or incompetent. A Gillick-competent child can consent to the relevant treatment.

Without competence: An individual with parental responsibility can usually consent to treatment in the child's best interests, within the limits of that authority. For serious disputes or exceptional interventions, seek senior/legal advice and possibly court authorisation.

2 · Parental responsibility

Parental responsibility is legal authority, not simply biological relationship. Mothers usually have it; fathers and others may have it depending on circumstances and legal arrangements. Do not assume a grandparent, step-parent, or accompanying adult has authority. Check when necessary.

3 · Refusal and disagreement

Explore reasons and involve the child. If parents refuse urgently needed life-saving care, escalate immediately to seniors and legal/safeguarding teams; urgent treatment may be justified to prevent serious harm while legal steps are taken. If time allows, seek court guidance rather than simply overriding disagreement. A competent child’s refusal, especially of life-saving care, needs urgent specialist legal advice.

4 · Confidentiality and safeguarding

Young people have confidentiality rights. Explain limits clearly; consider risk of abuse, exploitation, coercion or serious harm. Share only relevant information when justified, and document reasoning. Encourage parental support where safe but do not promise automatic disclosure.

5 · Common PLAB traps

  • Gillick competence is decision-specific; it is not the same as Fraser guidelines (specific to contraceptive advice).
  • Consent by a competent young person is not automatically invalid because a parent disagrees.
  • A 16-year-old's ability to consent does not mean refusal always has the same legal effect as an adult's refusal.
  • Emergency care and court involvement depend on urgency and the facts.
  • Never assume a parent can demand confidential information without considering the young person's rights.

Clinical decision pathways

A · Child needs treatment

  1. Assess urgency: stabilise immediately if critically ill.
  2. Explain treatment in age-appropriate language and seek the child's views.
  3. If 16–17, assess ability to consent; if under 16, assess Gillick competence.
  4. If competent, seek their informed consent. If not, identify a person with parental responsibility and consider best interests.
  5. For refusal or serious disagreement, involve senior clinician and legal advice; document carefully.

B · Confidential adolescent consultation

  1. Offer private conversation and explain confidentiality and its limits.
  2. Assess understanding, voluntariness and relevant safeguarding concerns.
  3. Discuss choices and encourage trusted adult involvement if safe.
  4. Share information only with consent or another justified basis; record rationale.

Do not delay emergency stabilisation while seeking a court order. Do not use emergency exceptions for non-urgent convenience.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 · A mature 14-year-old

A 14-year-old understands the proposed treatment, risks and alternatives and wants it; her parent objects.

Best approach: assess and document Gillick competence for this decision, seek voluntary informed consent, address family concerns sensitively, and seek senior advice if the dispute is serious.

Case 2 · Refusal of transfusion

A 16-year-old with major haemorrhage refuses a potentially life-saving transfusion.

Best approach: urgent resuscitation, assess capacity and reasons, involve senior team and emergency legal advice. Do not assume the refusal has the same legal finality as an adult's.

Case 3 · Parent refuses antibiotics

A 5-year-old with suspected bacterial meningitis needs immediate treatment; a parent refuses.

Best approach: act to protect the child from imminent serious harm, escalate urgently and obtain legal/safeguarding support; do not postpone emergency care.

Case 4 · Privacy request

A 15-year-old asks that details of a consultation not be shared with her parents.

Best approach: assess competence and safety, respect confidentiality where appropriate, encourage supportive involvement and explain when disclosure might be necessary.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Adolescent consent and parental disagreement

Station: A 15-year-old requests treatment; her mother insists she is too young to decide.

Candidate checklist

  1. Introduce yourself; confirm identities and ask to speak with the young person privately.
  2. Build rapport; explain confidentiality and safeguarding limits.
  3. Explore the young person's understanding of illness, treatment, risks, benefits and alternatives.
  4. Check whether the decision is voluntary and whether there is coercion or abuse.
  5. Assess Gillick competence for this particular treatment; avoid relying on age alone.
  6. Seek the young person's permission to involve the parent where appropriate.
  7. Explain options without confrontation; involve senior team if disagreement persists.
  8. Summarise plan, safety-net and document capacity/competence assessment.

Suggested wording: “I want to hear what you understand about the treatment and what matters to you. I will keep our conversation private unless I am worried someone is at serious risk of harm.”

Examiner pitfalls

Do not immediately disclose to the parent, dismiss the young person's wishes, or state that all under-16s require parental consent.
