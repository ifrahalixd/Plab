Hypoglycaemia & Diabetic Emergencies

Emergency: Seizure, unconsciousness or unsafe swallowing with suspected hypoglycaemia requires immediate help and parenteral treatment.

Definition and clinical urgency

Hypoglycaemia is commonly defined in diabetes care as blood glucose below 4 mmol/L. Severe hypoglycaemia means the person needs help from another person, irrespective of the measured glucose.

PLAB application: Treat immediately; do not wait for laboratory confirmation when symptoms and bedside findings fit.

Causes in diabetes

Insulin and sulfonylureas are important drug causes. Missed meals, vomiting, exercise, alcohol, kidney impairment and dosing errors increase risk.

PLAB application: Ask about the timing of insulin, food, alcohol and recent medication changes.

Autonomic symptoms

Sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger and anxiety result from the sympathoadrenal response.

PLAB application: Recognise early warning signs and check capillary glucose.

Neuroglycopenic symptoms

Confusion, behavioural change, blurred vision, difficulty speaking, seizures and coma reflect insufficient glucose reaching the brain.

PLAB application: Hypoglycaemia can mimic stroke; check glucose in every patient with altered consciousness.

Immediate ABCDE assessment

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Check capillary glucose, consciousness, swallowing safety and vital signs.

PLAB application: Call for emergency assistance in seizures, coma, airway compromise or persistent altered consciousness.

Conscious and able to swallow

Give approximately 15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate, such as glucose tablets or a suitable sugary drink; recheck glucose after 10–15 minutes and repeat if still below 4 mmol/L.

PLAB application: Once recovered, offer longer-acting carbohydrate if the next meal is not imminent; follow local protocol.

Unable to swallow safely

Do not give food or drink by mouth. In a healthcare setting, trained staff administer IV glucose according to local protocol; glucagon may be used when IV access is unavailable or outside hospital.

PLAB application: Position safely, summon help and monitor for recovery; glucagon may be less effective after prolonged fasting or alcohol-related glycogen depletion.

After initial recovery

Repeat glucose monitoring, provide a meal or longer-acting carbohydrate as appropriate, review cause and ensure symptoms have resolved.

PLAB application: Do not discharge a patient with ongoing confusion or recurrent low glucose.

Sulfonylurea-related hypoglycaemia

Sulfonylureas can cause prolonged or recurrent hypoglycaemia, particularly in renal impairment or overdose. Hospital observation and specialist management may be required.

PLAB application: Rebound hypoglycaemia is possible after initial glucose correction.

Hypoglycaemia unawareness

Repeated episodes can blunt warning symptoms, making severe hypoglycaemia more likely.

PLAB application: Review glycaemic targets and insulin regimen with the diabetes team; consider CGM where appropriate.

Nocturnal hypoglycaemia

Night sweats, disturbed sleep, morning headache or unexplained waking fatigue can suggest overnight episodes.

PLAB application: Discuss glucose trends, evening insulin, exercise and alcohol; avoid assuming symptoms are diagnostic.

Exercise and sick-day prevention

Exercise increases glucose use; illness can reduce intake while insulin needs change. People using insulin need individualised adjustment and monitoring plans.

PLAB application: Never advise a person with type 1 diabetes to stop basal insulin during illness.

Driving and safety

Hypoglycaemia can impair driving. People treated with insulin or medicines that cause hypos should follow current DVLA guidance, including glucose testing and reporting rules where applicable.

PLAB application: After severe episodes, discuss driving restrictions and the need for specialist advice.

Other diabetic emergencies

DKA causes ketosis and acidosis, HHS causes severe hyperosmolality and dehydration, and both require urgent hospital assessment.

PLAB application: In any unwell person with diabetes, check glucose, ketones when indicated, hydration and mental status.

Non-diabetic hypoglycaemia

Consider critical illness, liver or renal disease, adrenal insufficiency, alcohol, medication exposure and, rarely, endogenous hyperinsulinism.

PLAB application: Document Whipple’s triad when investigating spontaneous hypoglycaemia: symptoms, low plasma glucose and resolution after correction.

Discharge education and follow-up

Teach the 15–20 g rescue approach, carrying glucose, family training, glucagon access when appropriate, glucose monitoring and a clear plan for recurrent episodes.

PLAB application: Safety-net recurrent episodes, seizures, inability to eat, persistent vomiting or altered consciousness.

PLAB 2: Hypoglycaemia emergency and counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, assess ABCDE, check glucose and call for help when indicated.
  2. Determine whether the patient can swallow safely; explain rescue treatment in plain language.
  3. Ask about medication doses, missed meals, exercise, alcohol and recurrent episodes.
  4. Demonstrate the fast-acting carbohydrate plan and repeat testing; discuss rescue glucagon for appropriate patients.
  5. Explain prevention, driving considerations, follow-up and emergency red flags.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why the brain is affected

Glucose is the brain’s principal immediate fuel; falling supply produces confusion, seizures and reduced consciousness.

Counter-regulatory hormones

Glucagon and adrenaline normally increase glucose availability; repeated episodes can weaken warning responses.

Insulin and exercise

Muscle glucose uptake increases with activity; exogenous insulin may remain active even when intake is reduced.

Why sulfonylurea hypos recur

These drugs can continue stimulating insulin release after rescue glucose has been administered.

Why oral sugar is unsafe in coma

Reduced airway protection creates an aspiration hazard; use parenteral rescue.

Hypoglycaemia versus DKA/HHS

Hypoglycaemia requires immediate glucose replacement; DKA/HHS are hyperglycaemic emergencies with different fluid, insulin and monitoring strategies.
