Eating Disorders: Anorexia Nervosa, Bulimia Nervosa & Binge-Eating Disorder

PLAB priority: Diagnose eating disorders without relying on BMI alone; recognise medical instability, purging complications and refeeding risk.

01. Overview and diagnostic approach

Eating disorders are serious psychiatric illnesses with psychological and physical consequences. Assess restrictive intake, binge episodes, compensatory behaviours, weight trajectory and medical instability; body weight alone does not establish severity.

PLAB application: Ask sensitively about secrecy, shame, impairment and the patient’s own concerns.

02. Anorexia nervosa

Persistent restriction of energy intake with significantly low weight, intense fear of weight gain or behaviours preventing gain, and disturbance in body image or recognition of seriousness. Some patients deny feeling afraid but persistently restrict.

PLAB application: Rapid weight loss or medical compromise warrants urgent assessment regardless of BMI.

03. Bulimia nervosa

Recurrent binge eating with loss of control followed by inappropriate compensatory behaviours such as self-induced vomiting, laxative misuse, fasting or excessive exercise. Weight may be within the expected range.

PLAB application: Do not exclude bulimia because BMI is normal.

04. Binge-eating disorder

Recurrent distressing binge episodes with loss of control, typically without regular compensatory behaviours. Associated with shame and impairment; weight varies.

PLAB application: Distinguish from bulimia by the absence of recurrent compensatory behaviour.

05. Other specified feeding or eating disorders

Clinically significant eating disorders may not meet all criteria for anorexia, bulimia or binge-eating disorder. Atypical anorexia can occur at normal or high BMI after substantial weight loss. ARFID involves restriction without weight/shape concerns.

PLAB application: Treat medical risk seriously even if diagnostic criteria are not fully met.

06. History taking

Ask about a typical day of eating, dietary rules, restriction, binge frequency, vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, exercise, weight changes, menstrual history where relevant, dizziness, syncope, palpitations and bowel symptoms.

PLAB application: Use neutral non-judgemental language; ask about self-harm and suicide.

07. Examination and vital signs

Measure pulse, blood pressure including postural change when appropriate, temperature, weight trajectory, hydration and signs of malnutrition. Look for parotid swelling, dental erosion, calluses on knuckles and peripheral oedema.

PLAB application: Bradycardia, hypotension, hypothermia or collapse are warning signs.

08. Investigations

Depending on presentation obtain ECG, FBC, electrolytes, renal function, magnesium, phosphate, calcium, glucose and liver tests. Consider pregnancy testing when appropriate and other tests based on differential.

PLAB application: Hypokalaemia and QTc prolongation raise arrhythmia risk.

09. Medical emergencies

Severe malnutrition, acute food refusal, electrolyte disturbance, arrhythmia, haemodynamic instability, dehydration or rapid deterioration require urgent acute medical assessment and specialist input.

PLAB application: Do not rely on a single BMI threshold to determine admission.

10. Refeeding syndrome

Reintroducing nutrition in a severely malnourished patient can provoke shifts in phosphate, potassium and magnesium, causing cardiac, neurological and respiratory complications. Plan nutrition and monitoring with specialist teams.

PLAB application: Check and monitor phosphate, magnesium, potassium and fluid status in high-risk patients.

11. Treatment of anorexia nervosa

Use specialist eating-disorder services and coordinated medical/nutritional/psychological care. NICE recommends eating-disorder-focused CBT (CBT-ED), MANTRA or specialist supportive clinical management in adults; family-based approaches for young people.

PLAB application: Medication is not recommended as the sole treatment for anorexia nervosa.

12. Treatment of bulimia nervosa

Guided self-help focused on bulimia is an initial option for adults; if ineffective, consider individual CBT-ED. Fluoxetine may be used in some settings but psychological treatment remains central.

PLAB application: Address purging-related electrolyte and dental complications.

13. Treatment of binge-eating disorder

Offer eating-disorder-focused guided self-help, then group or individual CBT-ED if needed. Focus on regular eating and binge triggers; avoid recommending dieting during active treatment because it may worsen binge eating.

PLAB application: Do not frame weight loss as the immediate therapeutic goal.

14. Children, adolescents and families

Young people may become medically unstable at higher weights. Involve parents/carers appropriately, assess growth and development, and refer promptly to age-appropriate eating-disorder services.

PLAB application: Use safeguarding assessment and shared decision-making tailored to capacity.

15. Risk, comorbidity and communication

Assess depression, anxiety, OCD, trauma, substance use, suicidality and self-harm. Discuss confidentiality, collaboratively explain physical risk, and avoid blaming language or arguments about appearance.

PLAB application: A compassionate PLAB 2 consultation includes explicit risk assessment and safety-netting.

16. Referral and follow-up

Refer suspected eating disorders early to specialist services; urgent same-day medical assessment when unstable. Agree monitoring of physical health, treatment engagement and relapse warning signs.

PLAB application: NICE NG69 and local emergency pathways guide escalation; do not delay referral while waiting for investigations.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Restriction and physiological adaptation

Energy deprivation lowers resting metabolism and may cause bradycardia, hypothermia, amenorrhoea, bone loss and cognitive changes. Complications are not reliably predicted by BMI alone.

Binge–purge cycle

Restriction and emotional distress can precede binge episodes; purging temporarily reduces anxiety but reinforces the cycle and causes dental, gastrointestinal and electrolyte harm.

Refeeding physiology

Insulin release during nutritional restoration drives phosphate, magnesium and potassium into cells. High-risk patients require planned feeding and close biochemical monitoring.

Electrolytes and the heart

Hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia and malnutrition increase arrhythmia risk. ECG and urgent correction may be needed according to clinical severity.

Psychological interventions

CBT-ED targets rigid dietary rules and maintaining beliefs; guided self-help uses structured evidence-based material. Young people may benefit from family-based treatment.

Diagnostic traps and communication

Do not equate eating disorders with low BMI or a particular gender. Avoid assumptions and blame; assess risk and functional impact even when appearance seems well.
