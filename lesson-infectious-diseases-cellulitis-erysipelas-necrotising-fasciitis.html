Cellulitis, Erysipelas & Necrotising Fasciitis

PLAB priority: Recognise necrotising fasciitis early, escalate immediately, and differentiate cellulitis from its mimics.

01. Approach to skin and soft-tissue infection

Determine onset, spread, pain, fever, systemic illness, immune status, recent injury, surgery, bite or water exposure. Mark the border when useful and assess for sepsis.

PLAB application: Look for rapidly progressive infection and disproportionate pain.

02. Cellulitis presentation

Cellulitis is bacterial infection of dermis and subcutaneous tissue, typically unilateral with erythema, warmth, tenderness and swelling; fever may occur. Streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus are common pathogens.

PLAB application: Bilateral red legs are often not cellulitis; consider venous eczema or oedema.

03. Erysipelas

Erysipelas is a more superficial infection, classically with a raised, sharply demarcated erythematous border, often caused by beta-haemolytic streptococci.

PLAB application: Distinguish the sharply defined edge from more diffuse cellulitis.

04. Necrotising fasciitis

A rapidly progressive deep soft-tissue infection with severe pain out of proportion, swelling, systemic toxicity, skin discoloration, bullae or anaesthesia; early skin changes may be subtle.

PLAB application: Urgent surgical assessment is essential; do not wait for imaging or scores.

05. Sepsis and shock

Assess ABCDE, vital signs, perfusion, mental status, urine output and NEWS2 where appropriate. Hypotension, confusion, tachypnoea or rapidly spreading infection require immediate escalation.

PLAB application: Start emergency sepsis care alongside surgical consultation when indicated.

06. Risk factors

Skin breaks, eczema, tinea pedis, lymphoedema, venous disease, diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression and prior episodes increase risk.

PLAB application: Treat underlying skin disease and oedema to reduce recurrence.

07. Important differentials

Deep vein thrombosis, venous eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, contact dermatitis, gout, septic arthritis, superficial thrombophlebitis and allergic reactions can mimic cellulitis.

PLAB application: Painful restricted joint movement suggests septic arthritis rather than uncomplicated cellulitis.

08. Examination

Document distribution, borders, tenderness, swelling, entry site, pulses, neurovascular status and regional lymph nodes. Check for crepitus, bullae, necrosis and pain beyond visible erythema.

PLAB application: Crepitus may be absent in necrotising fasciitis.

09. Investigations

Typical mild cellulitis is diagnosed clinically. Blood tests and cultures are considered for severe infection, sepsis or unusual exposures. Swab broken skin only when clinically indicated; intact skin swabs are unhelpful.

PLAB application: Normal inflammatory markers do not safely rule out early necrotising infection.

10. Antibiotics for cellulitis

Follow NICE NG141 and local antimicrobial policy. Flucloxacillin is a usual first-choice oral antibiotic for uncomplicated adult cellulitis when appropriate; alternatives depend on allergy, site and exposure.

PLAB application: Check penicillin allergy, severity, pregnancy and renal function.

11. When to admit or give IV therapy

Severe systemic illness, rapidly progressive infection, inability to tolerate oral medication, significant immunosuppression or concern for deep infection require urgent hospital review.

PLAB application: Reassess within 2–3 days or sooner if deterioration; erythema may initially persist.

12. Necrotising fasciitis emergency treatment

Immediate senior surgical involvement and urgent debridement are life-saving. Give broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and resuscitation per local protocol; cultures and imaging must not delay surgery.

PLAB application: Surgery is definitive source control; antibiotics alone are inadequate.

13. Special sites and exposures

Periorbital or orbital infection, infection near the nose/eyes, hand infections, bites, seawater or freshwater injuries and postoperative infections may require tailored evaluation and antibiotics.

PLAB application: Painful eye movements, proptosis or visual loss suggest orbital cellulitis: emergency referral.

14. Complications

Abscess, bacteraemia, sepsis, tissue necrosis, lymphatic injury and recurrent cellulitis can occur. An abscess often requires drainage rather than antibiotics alone.

PLAB application: Seek a fluctuant collection and arrange drainage where indicated.

15. Recurrence prevention

Manage oedema and lymphoedema, treat interdigital fungal infection, moisturise dry skin, optimise diabetes care and review recurrent episodes; prophylactic antibiotics are specialist-directed.

PLAB application: Compression may help chronic oedema once acute issues are assessed.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain antibiotics, expected gradual improvement, limb elevation if appropriate and skin care. Seek urgent help for severe escalating pain, rapidly spreading redness, fever, confusion, blisters or dark skin.

PLAB application: Clearly explain why necrotising infection is a time-critical emergency.

Deep Dive

Anatomical depth

Erysipelas affects upper dermis and superficial lymphatics; cellulitis affects deeper dermis/subcutaneous tissue; necrotising infection spreads along fascia.

Why pain may precede skin signs

Deep fascial inflammation and tissue ischaemia can cause intense pain before extensive superficial discoloration appears.

Why surgery is essential

Necrotic poorly perfused tissue limits antibiotic delivery. Early debridement removes infected tissue and reduces toxin burden.

Cellulitis mimics

Venous eczema and chronic oedema are often bilateral, recurrent and less acutely tender than bacterial cellulitis.

Antibiotic stewardship

Choose antibiotics by NICE NG141, local resistance, allergy and exposure history; broaden urgently for suspected necrotising infection.

Skin barrier and recurrence

Toe-web maceration, eczema, wounds and lymphatic dysfunction permit recurrent bacterial entry; managing these reduces future attacks.
