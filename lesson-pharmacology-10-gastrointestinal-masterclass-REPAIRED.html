GI Pharmacology · Mechanisms and Clinical Deep Dive

Parietal cell signalling

Histamine H2 receptors couple to Gs, raising cAMP; acetylcholine M3 and gastrin CCK-B receptors use Gq and intracellular calcium. These converge on the H+/K+-ATPase. PPIs block the final common pathway; H2 blockers inhibit one upstream signal.

PPI pharmacodynamics

PPIs are prodrugs activated in the acidic secretory canaliculi of parietal cells. Their short plasma half-life contrasts with prolonged acid suppression because they irreversibly inhibit active pumps; new pump synthesis restores secretion. Timing before a meal maximises access to active pumps.

Dopamine and serotonin antiemetic pathways

D2 signalling in the chemoreceptor trigger zone contributes to emesis. Metoclopramide and prochlorperazine inhibit D2 pathways, producing nausea relief but also extrapyramidal effects. 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferents and central pathways mediate chemotherapy- and postoperative-related emesis; ondansetron antagonises them.

Motility and water balance

Macrogol osmotically retains luminal water; senna stimulates enteric motor activity. Loperamide activates peripheral mu-opioid receptors, slowing transit and enhancing fluid absorption. Reduced transit can worsen inflammatory or invasive infectious disease.

IBD inflammatory targets

Mesalazine has topical anti-inflammatory actions in intestinal mucosa. Glucocorticoids bind intracellular receptors to alter inflammatory gene transcription. Thiopurines interfere with purine metabolism and lymphocyte proliferation; anti-TNF biologics neutralise tumour necrosis factor; integrin blockers reduce leukocyte gut trafficking.

Monitoring immunosuppression

Thiopurine toxicity can involve bone marrow suppression, hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis. Check baseline testing, full blood count and liver enzymes according to specialist protocols; counsel on fever, sore throat, bruising and infection.

Dose and patient factors

Renal function can influence mesalazine safety and famotidine dosing. Hepatic disease affects drug metabolism and fluid/electrolyte handling. For all GI drugs, review age, pregnancy, frailty, concurrent medications and indication-specific risks.

Integrated clinical reasoning

The best prescription follows diagnosis and red-flag screening. For example, nausea from bowel obstruction requires assessment and definitive management; a prokinetic antiemetic may worsen the situation. In ulcerative colitis, worsening bloody diarrhoea requires escalation rather than loperamide.

Gastrointestinal Pharmacology · Clinical Masterclass

PLAB-focused clinical pharmacology: mechanisms, safe prescribing, differential diagnosis, patient counselling, monitoring and UK guideline pathways. Educational material; confirm current BNF/NICE and local formularies before clinical use.

60-second clinical summary

• PPIs inhibit the gastric H+/K+-ATPase and are first-line acid suppression for many acid-related disorders; use the lowest effective dose and review indications.

• Antiemetic selection depends on cause, QT risk, extrapyramidal risk, bowel obstruction and pregnancy.

• Laxatives are selected by stool consistency, cause and patient factors; avoid treating an undiagnosed acute abdomen as simple constipation.

• Oral rehydration is the foundation for most acute diarrhoeal illnesses; avoid antimotility drugs in bloody diarrhoea or suspected severe inflammatory/infective colitis.

• IBD drugs differ for induction and maintenance: 5-ASA is primarily used for ulcerative colitis, whereas corticosteroids induce remission but are not maintenance therapy.

1. Gastric acid physiology and acid suppression

Mechanism

Parietal-cell H+/K+-ATPase is the final step of acid secretion. Histamine H2, gastrin and acetylcholine increase acid secretion through different signalling pathways.

Proton-pump inhibitors

Omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole and esomeprazole irreversibly inhibit active proton pumps. They are usually taken before food; onset of full acid suppression may take several doses. Uses include GORD, peptic ulcer disease, ulcer prophylaxis in selected high-risk NSAID users and H. pylori eradication regimens.

Adverse effects and interactions

Common effects include headache and gastrointestinal upset. Long-term associations include hypomagnesaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, enteric infection and fractures; association does not always establish causation. Reassess ongoing need. Omeprazole/esomeprazole can inhibit CYP2C19, reducing clopidogrel activation; consider an alternative PPI where appropriate and check BNF.

H2 blockers and antacids

Famotidine blocks H2 receptors; renal adjustment may be needed. Antacids and alginates offer short-term symptom relief but may interfere with absorption of other medicines; separate dosing as indicated.

Alarm features

Dysphagia, persistent vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss and concerning age/risk features require assessment for serious disease, not indefinite empirical PPI treatment.

2. Dyspepsia, peptic ulcer and H. pylori

Assessment

Distinguish functional dyspepsia, GORD, peptic ulcer, medication injury and malignancy risk. Ask about NSAIDs, aspirin, alcohol, smoking and previous ulcer complications.

H. pylori test-and-treat

Use appropriate stool antigen or urea breath testing according to NICE; PPIs may need a two-week washout before testing and antibiotics a four-week washout to reduce false negatives. Eradication regimens depend on local resistance, prior antibiotics, allergy and current BNF/NICE recommendations.

NSAID-associated ulcer

Stop the NSAID where possible; prescribe a PPI and review bleeding risk. If NSAID continuation is unavoidable, assess gastroprotection and alternatives.

Bleeding ulcer

Haematemesis, melaena, shock or syncope require emergency resuscitation, blood tests, risk assessment and urgent endoscopic management. Do not delay escalation to prescribe an outpatient PPI.

3. Nausea and vomiting: choose an antiemetic

First identify the cause

Consider gastroenteritis, pregnancy, medicines, migraine, vestibular disease, obstruction, raised intracranial pressure, metabolic disturbance and postoperative causes. Correct dehydration and electrolytes.

Metoclopramide

D2 antagonism and prokinetic action; may cause acute dystonia, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia and other extrapyramidal effects. Restrict duration per current safety advice; avoid in bowel obstruction and Parkinson disease.

Prochlorperazine

Dopamine antagonist used for nausea and vertigo; causes sedation, postural hypotension and extrapyramidal effects.

Cyclizine

H1 antihistamine with antimuscarinic effects; may cause sedation, dry mouth and urinary retention. Consider glaucoma, older age and anticholinergic burden.

Ondansetron

5-HT3 antagonist useful for postoperative and chemotherapy-associated nausea; can prolong QT, especially with electrolyte disturbances and other QT-prolonging drugs. Constipation is common.

Pregnancy

Assess for hyperemesis, dehydration and ketonuria where relevant; follow pregnancy-specific guidance, considering doxylamine/pyridoxine and other suitable antiemetics according to local availability and recommendations. Severe illness may require IV fluids and thiamine before dextrose if malnourished.

4. Constipation and laxatives

Clinical assessment

Check onset, stool frequency/consistency, pain, bleeding, weight loss, medication causes (opioids, iron, anticholinergics), mobility and hydration. Severe distension, vomiting, peritonism or absent flatus suggests obstruction and requires urgent evaluation.

Bulk-forming

Ispaghula increases stool bulk; requires adequate fluids and is inappropriate when obstruction is suspected.

Osmotic

Macrogol retains water in the bowel; lactulose is an alternative but can cause bloating. Monitor hydration and electrolytes in vulnerable patients.

Stimulant

Senna and bisacodyl stimulate motility; can cause cramps. Useful with opioid-related constipation, often alongside an osmotic agent.

Softening and rectal agents

Docusate, glycerol suppositories and enemas have selected roles. Assess faecal impaction before escalating; follow local bowel protocols.

Opioid-induced constipation

Proactively prescribe a bowel regimen when initiating ongoing opioids; review opioid dose, hydration, movement and response. Peripherally acting mu-opioid antagonists may be considered for refractory cases under guidance.

5. Diarrhoea, dehydration and antimotility therapy

Initial priorities

Assess dehydration, sepsis, blood in stool, fever, recent antibiotics, travel, immunosuppression and outbreak exposure. Oral rehydration solution replaces water and electrolytes; severe dehydration may require IV fluids.

Loperamide

Peripheral mu-opioid agonist reduces gut motility. Short-term symptom control in selected adults with non-bloody diarrhoea; avoid in suspected dysentery, C. difficile colitis, acute severe ulcerative colitis and ileus. Excessive doses can cause serious arrhythmias.

C. difficile

Recent antibiotic exposure plus diarrhoea warrants consideration of C. difficile and infection-control precautions. Stop unnecessary causative antibiotics and follow current local antimicrobial guidance; avoid routine antimotility therapy.

Chronic diarrhoea

Consider IBD, coeliac disease, bile acid diarrhoea, malabsorption, medication effects and colorectal pathology. Treat the cause rather than repeatedly masking symptoms.

6. Inflammatory bowel disease: ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease

Core distinction

Ulcerative colitis affects colonic mucosa continuously from the rectum; Crohn disease can involve any gastrointestinal segment and is often transmural. Treatment depends on phenotype, extent, severity and complications.

5-aminosalicylates

Mesalazine is a cornerstone for mild–moderate ulcerative colitis; oral and rectal routes are selected by disease extent. It is not a reliable primary induction strategy for Crohn disease. Monitor renal function and assess intolerance.

Corticosteroids

Prednisolone, intravenous hydrocortisone and selected budesonide formulations induce remission in flares but should not be used for long-term maintenance. Monitor infection, glucose, bone health, mood and adrenal suppression.

Thiopurines

Azathioprine and mercaptopurine can maintain remission in selected patients but act slowly and require baseline TPMT/NUDT15 assessment where appropriate, blood-count and liver monitoring. Risks include myelosuppression, pancreatitis and infection.

Methotrexate

Used in selected Crohn disease regimens under specialist care; given weekly, never daily, with monitoring and pregnancy precautions.

Biologics and small molecules

Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab), vedolizumab, ustekinumab and other advanced therapies are specialist-selected. Screen for latent infections and update vaccination plans before immunosuppression.

Acute severe colitis

Frequent bloody stool plus systemic toxicity requires admission, blood tests, stool studies, VTE prophylaxis where appropriate, IV corticosteroids and multidisciplinary review. Toxic megacolon and perforation are emergencies.

7. Liver disease and pancreatic conditions

Hepatic encephalopathy

Lactulose reduces intestinal ammonia absorption and is titrated to an appropriate stool target according to specialist advice; rifaximin may be used for recurrent episodes. Address triggers such as infection, bleeding and constipation.

Ascites

Spironolactone, sometimes with furosemide, may be used under cirrhosis-specific protocols. Monitor renal function, potassium, sodium, blood pressure and weight.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement

Pancreatin with meals and snacks is used for pancreatic exocrine insufficiency; adjust to nutritional response and formulation guidance. Treat underlying disease and nutritional deficiencies.

8. Prescribing safety and patient-centred review

Renal and hepatic function

Check kidney function, hepatic status and drug-specific adjustments. Avoid reflexive dose changes without knowing the medicine, indication and relevant BNF advice.

Interactions

Review QT-prolonging antiemetics, PPI–clopidogrel combinations, antacid absorption interactions, immunosuppressant interactions and steroid-related infection risks.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Use indication-specific UK pregnancy resources and BNF. Avoid assuming a drug is safe or unsafe solely because it belongs to a class.

Counselling

Explain purpose, expected response, administration, duration, adverse effects, red flags and when to seek review. For IBD, discuss adherence, monitoring and infection precautions.

Worked PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — PPI and clopidogrel

Presentation: A 68-year-old taking clopidogrel after a coronary stent develops reflux symptoms and asks for omeprazole.

Clinical reasoning: Check for alarm features, review antiplatelet indication and gastroprotection needs, assess interacting medicines, and consider a PPI with less CYP2C19 inhibition where clinically appropriate. Do not stop clopidogrel independently.

Case 2 — vomiting and QT risk

Presentation: A 54-year-old with gastroenteritis, hypokalaemia and a prolonged QT interval requests ondansetron.

Clinical reasoning: Correct electrolytes and dehydration first; avoid or carefully assess QT-prolonging medicines and seek an alternative suited to the cause and risk profile.

Case 3 — severe constipation

Presentation: An 80-year-old on morphine has abdominal distension, vomiting and no flatus.

Clinical reasoning: Suspect bowel obstruction rather than simple opioid constipation. Assess urgently, keep nil by mouth as clinically indicated, arrange imaging/surgical review and do not simply prescribe a bulk laxative.

Case 4 — severe ulcerative colitis

Presentation: A 27-year-old with known ulcerative colitis has six or more bloody stools daily, fever and tachycardia.

Clinical reasoning: Arrange emergency hospital assessment for acute severe ulcerative colitis, infection exclusion, fluids, VTE assessment, IV corticosteroids and early gastroenterology/surgical involvement.

PLAB 2 counselling station

A patient with reflux has used a PPI for two years. Confirm indication, ask about alarm symptoms and concomitant NSAIDs, explain acid suppression, discuss lowest effective dose and planned review, provide lifestyle advice, describe when to seek urgent assessment, and agree a shared follow-up plan. Avoid abruptly discontinuing essential ulcer prophylaxis without reviewing risk.

Exam traps and take-home points

• Steroids induce IBD remission; they are not maintenance drugs.

• Mesalazine is important in ulcerative colitis, not routine Crohn disease induction.

• Metoclopramide can cause dystonia and is inappropriate in mechanical bowel obstruction.

• Loperamide is inappropriate in bloody diarrhoea or suspected severe colitis.

• A suspected obstruction is a red flag before laxative prescribing.

• Check QT and potassium before selecting QT-prolonging antiemetics.

• Assess red flags before prolonged empirical treatment of dyspepsia.

    NICE / BNF · GI prescribing pathways

    Educational decision aids. Check current NICE/BNF, local pathways and specialist advice.

    Dyspepsia / GORD

    Screen alarm features → review NSAIDs and medicines → consider H. pylori testing or PPI trial as indicated → review response and step down if appropriate.

    Acute GI bleeding

    Assess ABCDE and haemodynamic stability → resuscitate, crossmatch and risk stratify → urgent endoscopy pathway → manage antithrombotics with specialist input.

    IBD flare

    Assess severity and systemic toxicity → exclude infection → mild UC: site-appropriate 5-ASA; severe disease: admit, IV steroids and early specialist review → maintenance plan.

    Diarrhoea / constipation

    Assess dehydration and red flags → treat cause and replace fluid/electrolytes → choose symptomatic drug only when safe → review response and escalation triggers.
