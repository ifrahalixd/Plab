Child Safeguarding, Non-Accidental Injury, Neglect & Child Protection

Safety first: Treat urgent injuries, assess immediate danger, involve senior safeguarding professionals and do not send a child into an unsafe situation.

Safeguarding principles

Safeguarding means protecting children from abuse and neglect, preventing impairment of health or development and ensuring safe care. Every clinician has a duty to recognise concerns, document accurately and share information appropriately. The child’s welfare is paramount.

PLAB application: Do not wait for proof of abuse before seeking safeguarding advice.

Types of maltreatment

Physical abuse includes inflicted injury; emotional abuse includes persistent harmful interactions; sexual abuse includes contact and non-contact exploitation; neglect is persistent failure to meet basic needs. Categories overlap, and exploitation or domestic abuse may coexist.

PLAB application: A child can experience more than one form of abuse.

Recognising concerning injuries

Bruises in non-mobile infants, patterned marks, multiple injuries at different healing stages, burns with clear immersion lines and injuries inconsistent with development or history warrant careful assessment. Accidental injuries also occur: evaluate the whole context.

PLAB application: Any bruise in a non-mobile baby needs prompt safeguarding assessment.

History and discrepancies

Ask who witnessed events, precise chronology, mechanism, delay in presentation, developmental abilities and prior injuries. Obtain the caregiver’s explanation without accusation. If age-appropriate, speak with the child in a safe, sensitive manner and use their exact words.

PLAB application: Do not repeatedly interrogate a child or ask leading questions.

Physical examination

Prioritise acute medical needs, obtain consent as appropriate and perform a respectful head-to-toe examination. Record injury size, site, shape, colour and associated findings on a body map; avoid estimating bruise age from colour. Consider differential diagnoses.

PLAB application: Normal examination does not exclude abuse.

Abusive head trauma

Infants may present with seizures, apnoea, vomiting, altered consciousness or unexplained neurological findings, sometimes without external injury. Stabilise ABCDE and seek urgent paediatric, radiological and safeguarding expertise.

PLAB application: Do not infer mechanism solely from one imaging finding.

Fractures and skeletal injuries

Rib fractures, certain metaphyseal injuries or fractures in non-mobile infants may raise concern, but no single fracture is diagnostic of abuse. Investigations such as skeletal survey follow specialist protocols and age-specific indications.

PLAB application: Do not order or interpret a skeletal survey in isolation.

Burns and scalds

Assess burn severity and provide analgesia and appropriate burn care. Consider mechanism, distribution, clear demarcation, symmetry, developmental ability and delay in seeking care. Refer suspicious patterns to safeguarding services.

PLAB application: Treat the burn immediately while safeguarding assessment proceeds.

Neglect and failure to thrive

Potential signs include persistent poor hygiene, inadequate nutrition, untreated illness, missed essential healthcare or unsafe supervision. Poverty alone is not neglect; consider access barriers, disability and family circumstances while evaluating impact and risk.

PLAB application: Use a holistic, non-stigmatising assessment.

Sexual abuse concerns

Presentations can include disclosure, genital symptoms, behavioural change or no physical signs. Ensure safety, avoid leading questions and seek urgent specialist advice about forensic timing, sexual health, infection testing and support.

PLAB application: A normal genital examination does not rule out sexual abuse.

Domestic abuse and exploitation

Exposure to domestic abuse can seriously harm children. Be alert to coercive control, trafficking, grooming, online exploitation and contextual safeguarding risks. Speak privately when safe and follow local procedures for referral.

PLAB application: Never confront an alleged perpetrator in a way that increases danger.

Differential diagnoses

Consider bleeding disorders, connective tissue disorders, accidental trauma, birthmarks, cultural practices and medical causes of poor growth. Investigating alternatives is essential but should not delay protecting a child at immediate risk.

PLAB application: Avoid both premature accusations and premature dismissal.

Documentation and information sharing

Write contemporaneous factual notes, exact quotations, examination findings and who was present. Record discussions, referrals, safety plans and reasons for decisions. Share relevant information proportionately for child protection; confidentiality is not absolute.

PLAB application: Document facts separately from professional interpretations.

Escalation and referral in the UK

Discuss concerns promptly with the named or designated safeguarding professional and paediatric team. Refer to local authority children’s social care when indicated; contact police/emergency services for immediate danger. Follow local multi-agency procedures.

PLAB application: Do not discharge a child at risk without an agreed safety plan.

Consent, capacity and communication

Explain concerns honestly without blame where safe. Children’s participation depends on age and understanding; consider Gillick competence and parental responsibility. Consent is not always required to share information needed to protect a child.

PLAB application: Avoid promising absolute secrecy to a child making a disclosure.

PLAB 2 safeguarding station

Use empathy and neutral language, check immediate safety, listen without leading, address urgent medical issues, seek senior safeguarding advice, explain information sharing and arrange safe follow-up. Use SBAR for referral and clear contemporaneous documentation.

PLAB application: Say “I am concerned about the child’s safety” rather than alleging guilt.

PLAB 2 — Explaining a safeguarding concern

“I am concerned that these findings may not be fully explained by what we know so far. My priority is your child’s health and safety. I need to discuss this with our senior paediatric and safeguarding team so we can assess what support and protection are needed.” Document the conversation, assess safety and arrange an appropriate referral.

Beyond the basics

Deep Dive — Child Protection

Why developmental stage matters

An injury mechanism must be plausible for the child’s motor abilities. Bruising in a baby who cannot roll or crawl differs from shin bruises in a running toddler. Developmental assessment is supportive evidence, not a substitute for comprehensive assessment.

The TEN-4-FACESp principle

This clinical decision aid identifies bruising patterns that warrant further evaluation in young children; it is not a diagnosis of abuse and should not replace local UK safeguarding guidance.

Disclosure and trauma-informed practice

Listen calmly, use open prompts such as “Tell me what happened,” avoid repeated questioning, do not promise secrecy and explain that some information must be shared to keep the child safe.

Investigations are specialist-led

Consider medical assessment, targeted blood tests, imaging, ophthalmology or forensic assessment as indicated by age and presentation. Investigations should follow paediatric safeguarding pathways with careful interpretation.

Threshold for sharing information

A reasonable concern for safety can justify proportionate information sharing with appropriate agencies even without consent. Record what was shared, with whom, why and any reasons for not informing caregivers.

Safeguarding is multidisciplinary

Named safeguarding clinicians, paediatricians, social workers, police and other professionals may be involved. The role of the examining clinician is to recognise, treat, document and escalate—not independently determine criminal responsibility.
