Burns, Wounds & Soft Tissue Emergencies: Burn Assessment, Wound Care, Cellulitis & Necrotising Fasciitis

Emergency red flags: Airway injury after enclosed-space fire, rapidly spreading painful infection, major burns, compromised limb perfusion and systemic deterioration require immediate escalation.

First approach to burns

Use ABCDE, assess scene safety, remove the patient from the heat source, and look for associated trauma. A burn may distract from airway injury or shock. Early senior help is essential for extensive burns.

PLAB application: Treat threats to life before calculating burn area.

Burn first aid

Cool a thermal burn under cool running tap water for 20 minutes, ideally as soon as possible and within 3 hours of injury. Remove jewellery and non-adherent clothing; do not pull away material stuck to skin. Keep the rest of the patient warm to avoid hypothermia. Cover loosely with cling film laid lengthwise, not wrapped circumferentially.

PLAB application: Avoid ice, butter and topical home remedies.

Burn depth

Superficial epidermal burns are red, painful and dry. Superficial partial-thickness burns are often blistered, moist and very painful. Deep partial-thickness burns may be pale, drier and less sensate. Full-thickness burns are leathery, dry and may be insensate; mixed-depth burns are common.

PLAB application: Loss of pain does not imply a minor burn.

Burn surface area

Estimate total body surface area (TBSA) using the Lund–Browder chart, especially in children; the adult rule of nines is a rapid approximation. The patient’s palm including fingers is roughly 1% TBSA. Exclude simple epidermal erythema from TBSA estimates used for resuscitation.

PLAB application: Record both depth and percentage TBSA.

Referral to a burns service

Seek specialist burns advice for significant partial-thickness burns, any full-thickness burn, burns to face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum or major joints, circumferential burns, chemical/electrical burns, inhalation injury and vulnerable patients. Apply local UK burn network criteria and clinical judgement.

PLAB application: Small burns in high-risk locations may need specialist care.

Inhalation injury and carbon monoxide

Suspect inhalation injury after an enclosed-space fire, facial burns, soot, hoarseness, stridor or respiratory distress. Give high-concentration oxygen when indicated and arrange early airway expertise; oedema may progress. Standard pulse oximetry cannot reliably exclude carbon monoxide exposure; consider co-oximetry.

PLAB application: Do not wait for stridor to request airway support.

Burn shock and fluids

Large burns cause capillary leak and fluid loss. Obtain IV access, monitor observations and urine output, and follow a specialist burn-resuscitation protocol for major burns. Formulae are starting estimates rather than fixed prescriptions; titrate to clinical response and avoid both under- and over-resuscitation.

PLAB application: Do not use a formula instead of repeated clinical reassessment.

Chemical and electrical burns

Protect staff with PPE and prevent ongoing exposure. For chemical burns, remove contaminated clothing and irrigate copiously with water unless a specific substance requires a different protocol; dry powders may need brushing off first. Electrical injury can cause arrhythmia, deep muscle damage and rhabdomyolysis despite small skin wounds.

PLAB application: Electrical injury warrants assessment beyond visible burn size.

Wound assessment

Record mechanism, timing, contamination, depth, size, neurovascular status, tendon function and foreign-body risk. Examine and document before local anaesthesia when possible. Consider safeguarding where the injury history or pattern is concerning.

PLAB application: Check movement, sensation and perfusion distal to the wound.

Wound irrigation and closure

Control bleeding with direct pressure, provide analgesia and irrigate contaminated wounds thoroughly with appropriate solution. Explore when safe, identify tendon or nerve injury, and decide between primary closure, delayed closure or specialist repair according to infection risk and anatomy. Avoid routine antibiotics for uncomplicated clean lacerations.

PLAB application: A hand wound may conceal tendon or nerve injury.

Tetanus and bite wounds

Check tetanus immunisation status and assess whether a wound is tetanus-prone or high-risk; follow current UKHSA guidance for vaccination and immunoglobulin. For human or animal bites, irrigate, assess deep structures and infection risk, consider prophylactic antibiotics using NICE NG184 and assess rabies risk when relevant.

PLAB application: Hand bites and human bites deserve careful specialist assessment.

Cellulitis and erysipelas

Cellulitis produces spreading warmth, erythema, tenderness and swelling; erysipelas tends to be more sharply demarcated. Check for systemic illness, entry sites and alternative diagnoses such as DVT or dermatitis. Use NICE NG141 for antimicrobial selection and review if rapidly worsening or not improving as expected.

PLAB application: Mark the edge of erythema and provide safety-netting.

Abscesses and infected wounds

A fluctuant abscess usually requires drainage; antibiotics alone may not resolve a collection. Consider ultrasound for uncertain deep collections. Assess for fever, immunosuppression, spreading infection and anatomical risk; obtain specialist help for complex sites or deep infections.

PLAB application: Source control is central to abscess treatment.

Necrotising fasciitis

Think of necrotising soft-tissue infection with rapidly progressive pain out of proportion, systemic toxicity, skin discolouration, bullae, crepitus or anaesthesia. Early skin findings may be subtle and crepitus may be absent. Resuscitate, give urgent broad-spectrum IV antibiotics according to local protocol and obtain immediate surgical review for exploration and debridement.

PLAB application: Do not delay surgical referral for imaging or laboratory scores.

Compartment syndrome and circumferential burns

Increasing pain, pain on passive stretch, paraesthesia or tense compartments after trauma or burns may signal compartment syndrome; distal pulses can remain present. Circumferential deep burns can compromise limb perfusion or chest expansion. Escalate urgently for specialist assessment of fasciotomy or escharotomy.

PLAB application: Normal pulses do not rule out compartment syndrome.

Discharge and PLAB 2 counselling

Explain expected healing, dressings, analgesia, follow-up and scar protection in plain language. Give clear red flags: spreading redness, fever, increasing pain, numbness, colour change, reduced movement or breathlessness. Discuss safeguarding sensitively, document findings, check understanding and arrange appropriate review.

PLAB application: Give explicit return precautions and a documented plan.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Burn or infected wound

Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent, start with ABCDE if unwell, ask about mechanism and timing, offer analgesia, check neurovascular function, examine with a chaperone if appropriate, explain first aid or infection treatment, escalate red flags, and safety-net with teach-back.

Deep Dive — Burns, Wounds & Soft Tissue Emergencies

Thermal burns: an integrated approach

ABCDE first; stop burning; cool with running water for 20 minutes while keeping the patient warm; cover with a clean non-adherent dressing; assess depth, TBSA, special areas, inhalation injury and referral criteria.

Fluid resuscitation nuance

Large burns require expert-guided fluid resuscitation with close reassessment of perfusion and urine output. Avoid using a single calculated volume without considering other injuries, age, comorbidities and ongoing losses.

Airway and inhalation hazards

Enclosed-space fire, facial burns, soot and hoarseness are warning signs. Early senior airway review matters because oedema may progress. Carbon monoxide poisoning may produce misleading pulse oximeter readings.

Necrotising infection versus cellulitis

Cellulitis usually develops over hours to days with local erythema and tenderness; necrotising infection can progress quickly with disproportionate pain and systemic toxicity. Imaging and blood tests cannot safely exclude necrotising fasciitis when clinical suspicion is high.

Wound closure decisions

Irrigation, exploration, distal function and contamination determine repair. Bite wounds, heavily contaminated wounds and injuries involving tendons, joints, nerves or vessels may require specialist care rather than simple suturing.

Communication and documentation

Document injury mechanism, timing, site, depth, TBSA, photographs according to policy, analgesia, tetanus status, distal neurovascular findings, treatments, escalation and discharge advice. Be alert to safeguarding without making accusations.
