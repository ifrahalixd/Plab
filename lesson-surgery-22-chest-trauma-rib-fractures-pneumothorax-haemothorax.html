Chest trauma: rapid recognition and safe treatment

PLAB emergency rule: Suspected tension pneumothorax with severe respiratory compromise or haemodynamic instability is a clinical diagnosis. Decompress immediately using an appropriately trained clinician and local major-trauma protocol; do not wait for chest X-ray.

1 · Primary survey: <C>ABCDE

Call trauma team early, control catastrophic bleeding, protect airway with cervical-spine precautions when indicated, administer oxygen according to clinical need, check respiratory rate, SpO₂, chest expansion, tracheal position, percussion, breath sounds, pulses, BP, mental state and temperature. Expose the chest while preventing hypothermia. Reassess after each intervention. Treat pain early without compromising ventilation.

2 · Immediately life-threatening thoracic injuries

Tension pneumothorax

One-way air leak raises intrapleural pressure and impairs venous return. Signs may include severe dyspnoea, unilateral absent breath sounds, hypoxia, tachycardia and hypotension. Tracheal deviation is late and unreliable. If unstable with convincing clinical features, immediate chest decompression (finger/open thoracostomy or needle technique as appropriate to expertise and local pathway), followed by definitive chest drain. In stable patients, imaging can help confirm diagnosis.

Open pneumothorax

Penetrating chest-wall defect communicating with pleura; cover promptly with an appropriate vented chest seal, monitor for tension physiology, arrange definitive surgical/chest-drain management. Avoid an airtight dressing that traps air without a vent or monitoring.

Massive haemothorax

Blood in pleural space causing shock and reduced air entry, often with dull percussion. Resuscitate using haemorrhage protocol, give blood products as indicated, urgent chest drain and thoracic/trauma surgical consultation. Persistent major output or haemodynamic instability may require operative haemorrhage control.

Cardiac tamponade

Especially after penetrating injury: obstructive shock, possible raised JVP and muffled heart sounds (Beck triad often incomplete). Bedside ultrasound may help; urgent cardiothoracic/trauma intervention. Do not allow imaging to delay life-saving treatment in unstable patients.

3 · Simple pneumothorax

Traumatic pneumothorax may cause pleuritic pain, breathlessness and reduced breath sounds. Assess stability and size using clinical findings and imaging. Management depends on symptoms, size, ventilation status, concomitant injury and local trauma guidance; observation may suit selected stable small cases, while drainage is commonly needed for symptomatic or larger traumatic pneumothoraces. Positive-pressure ventilation increases risk of deterioration and warrants senior planning.

4 · Rib fractures and flail chest

Rib fractures cause focal pain, tenderness, splinting and atelectasis. Multiple fractures, older age, frailty and lung disease increase pneumonia and respiratory-failure risk. Offer multimodal analgesia, consider regional blocks, respiratory physiotherapy/incentive breathing, early mobilisation, oxygen if indicated and admission when risk is high. Flail chest means a segment of chest wall moves independently due to multiple adjacent rib fractures; it is often associated with pulmonary contusion and can impair ventilation. Seek senior critical-care/trauma input.

5 · Pulmonary contusion

Bruising of lung parenchyma after blunt trauma may worsen over hours. Hypoxaemia and infiltrates may evolve despite initially reassuring imaging. Provide supportive oxygenation, adequate analgesia and careful fluids; severe cases may require ventilatory support. Do not routinely give prophylactic antibiotics or steroids for uncomplicated contusion.

6 · Haemothorax

Suspect with chest trauma, reduced breath sounds, dullness, hypoxia or shock. Chest X-ray, eFAST and CT in stable patients help define injury. Drain clinically significant haemothorax, monitor drain output, haemoglobin and haemodynamics, and discuss retained clot or ongoing bleeding with thoracic surgeons. Chest drains require trained insertion, secure connection and regular observation.

7 · Imaging and investigations

In unstable major trauma, use bedside assessment and eFAST as appropriate without delaying intervention. Portable chest X-ray can identify pneumothorax, haemothorax and fractures but a normal film does not exclude significant injury. CT chest with IV contrast is considered in stable major trauma according to mechanism and findings. Send FBC, blood gas/lactate, coagulation, U&E and group/crossmatch as clinically indicated. ECG and troponin may be indicated for suspected blunt cardiac injury.

8 · Specific complications and pitfalls

Consider tracheobronchial injury with persistent air leak, oesophageal injury after penetrating trauma, diaphragmatic rupture with abdominal viscera in thorax, and blunt aortic injury after high-energy deceleration. Hypoxia after chest trauma may reflect multiple concurrent injuries. Do not rely on tracheal deviation to diagnose tension pneumothorax. Do not insert chest drains without proper training and safeguards.

9 · Secondary survey and disposition

Inspect anterior and posterior chest, palpate ribs/sternum/clavicles, examine abdomen and spine, reassess pain and ventilation, review serial observations and imaging. Escalate older patients with multiple fractures, oxygen requirement, anticoagulant use or deterioration. Provide clear return precautions for worsening breathlessness, pain, fever or syncope after discharge.

Clinical decision frameworks

Unstable chest trauma

<C>ABCDE → call trauma team → identify tension pneumothorax, major haemothorax, tamponade or airway injury → immediate indicated intervention → resuscitate and reassess → urgent definitive trauma/thoracic care.

Tension pneumothorax

Severe compromise + unilateral reduced breath sounds ± hypotension → decompress immediately without waiting for imaging → definitive drain → reassess oxygenation, perfusion and chest expansion.

Haemothorax

Shock + unilateral dullness/reduced air entry → haemorrhage resuscitation, bedside imaging when feasible → drain if indicated → monitor blood loss → early thoracic surgical decision for ongoing major bleed.

Rib fracture bundle

Analgesia → deep breathing and cough support → respiratory physiotherapy/mobilisation → monitor SpO₂, RR and pain → admit high-risk patients.

Imaging strategy

Unstable = bedside decisions and interventions first. Stable = CXR/eFAST as indicated, CT chest with contrast for major trauma when appropriate.

Examination contrast

Pneumothorax: reduced air entry, often hyperresonance. Haemothorax: reduced air entry, dullness. Both may coexist; exam is imperfect.

Four clinical decision cases

Case 1 — Sudden shock after collision

A 24-year-old after a road collision is profoundly breathless, hypotensive and has almost absent right breath sounds. Decision: treat presumed tension pneumothorax immediately with trained chest decompression; do not wait for X-ray, then arrange definitive drain.

Case 2 — Elderly fall and rib pain

A 79-year-old has four rib fractures, severe pain and shallow breathing. Decision: high risk of atelectasis and pneumonia; optimise multimodal analgesia, respiratory support and monitoring, consider admission and regional analgesia.

Case 3 — Penetrating trauma and shock

A 31-year-old with a stab wound has low BP, unilateral dull percussion and reduced air entry. Decision: suspect significant haemothorax, activate haemorrhage protocol, urgent drainage and thoracic surgical involvement.

Case 4 — Delayed hypoxia

A 40-year-old with blunt chest injury has worsening oxygen saturation over 8 hours and patchy lung opacities. Decision: suspect pulmonary contusion; monitor closely, provide supportive oxygenation, analgesia and critical-care review as needed.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Breathlessness after chest injury

Station: A 28-year-old presents after a motorbike crash with worsening breathlessness and right-sided chest pain. BP 82/50, SpO₂ 86%, RR 34.

  1. Introduce yourself, rapidly recognise critical illness and call for trauma/anaesthetic help.
  2. Perform <C>ABCDE, apply oxygen as indicated, assess airway, chest expansion, breath sounds, pulse, BP and mental state.
  3. Identify possible tension pneumothorax from severe respiratory and circulatory compromise with unilateral signs.
  4. Explain to team: “This may be a tension pneumothorax. We need immediate decompression; imaging must not delay treatment.”
  5. Arrange trained clinician to perform emergency decompression and definitive chest drainage according to protocol; reassess continuously.
  6. Obtain IV access, monitor ECG/SpO₂/BP, assess for concurrent haemorrhage and other traumatic injuries.
  7. Once stabilised, arrange imaging, analgesia, full secondary survey and appropriate admission.
  8. Communicate clearly with patient and team; document time, findings, treatment and response.

Examiner focus: recognition of tension physiology, prioritisation of intervention over imaging, teamwork, reassessment and patient safety.

Rapid revision: 12 essentials

  1. Use <C>ABCDE and repeat after interventions.
  2. Tension pneumothorax with instability is a clinical emergency: decompress, do not wait for X-ray.
  3. Tracheal deviation is late and unreliable.
  4. Definitive management commonly includes a chest drain after emergency decompression.
  5. Open pneumothorax needs a vented seal and specialist management.
  6. Massive haemothorax requires blood-loss resuscitation and urgent surgical assessment.
  7. Rib fractures need effective analgesia to maintain ventilation.
  8. Older adults with multiple rib fractures have high complication risk.
  9. Flail chest may coexist with pulmonary contusion.
  10. Pulmonary contusion can deteriorate over hours.
  11. eFAST and CT assist diagnosis but must not delay lifesaving interventions.
  12. Consider tamponade, aortic injury and diaphragmatic rupture after severe trauma.
