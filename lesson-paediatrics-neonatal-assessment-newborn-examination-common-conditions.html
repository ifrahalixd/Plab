Neonatal Assessment, Newborn Examination & Common Neonatal Conditions

Emergency red flags: Apnoea, central cyanosis, shock, seizures, severe respiratory distress, bilious vomiting or suspected neonatal sepsis require urgent neonatal assessment.

The first minutes after birth

Dry, warm and assess breathing, heart rate and tone. Follow the Resuscitation Council UK Newborn Life Support algorithm when a baby is not breathing effectively. Do not delay support to calculate an Apgar score.

PLAB application: Call trained neonatal help early and prioritise effective ventilation when indicated.

Apgar scoring

Assess appearance, pulse, grimace, activity and respiration at 1 and 5 minutes (and later if needed). It describes condition and response to resuscitation, but is not a stand-alone diagnostic test for asphyxia.

PLAB application: Know the five components and avoid using Apgar to decide whether to start resuscitation.

Thermal care and routine transition

Prevent hypothermia with drying, appropriate covering and skin-to-skin contact for a stable infant. Assess feeding, colour, tone, temperature and respiratory effort. Support early breastfeeding and parent–infant bonding.

PLAB application: Persistent central cyanosis, apnoea or poor perfusion is abnormal.

Newborn and infant physical examination (NIPE)

UK NIPE screening is offered within 72 hours of birth and again at 6–8 weeks for specified components. Screen eyes, heart, hips and testes where applicable; complete a comprehensive head-to-toe examination and document findings.

PLAB application: Explain that screening cannot detect every congenital abnormality.

Head-to-toe newborn examination

Review pregnancy and birth history. Inspect fontanelles, scalp and face; check red reflex, palate, breathing, heart sounds, pulses, abdomen, umbilicus, spine, limbs, genitalia, anus and primitive responses. Check for asymmetry and birth injury.

PLAB application: Absent red reflex, absent femoral pulses and concerning cardiac signs need prompt referral.

Congenital heart disease clues

Central cyanosis, tachypnoea, sweating with feeds, poor weight gain, shock after ductal closure and weak femoral pulses suggest significant cardiac disease. Pulse oximetry may help detect critical congenital heart disease but does not replace clinical examination.

PLAB application: A shocked neonate with weak femoral pulses may have coarctation or another duct-dependent lesion.

Hip and testicular screening

Use Barlow and Ortolani manoeuvres appropriately in trained hands; assess risk factors such as breech presentation and family history. Check whether both testes are palpable in the scrotum and follow NIPE referral pathways.

PLAB application: A normal early examination does not entirely exclude developmental dysplasia of the hip.

Neonatal jaundice: recognising risk

Physiological jaundice is common after the first day. Jaundice within the first 24 hours is pathological until assessed. Risk factors include prematurity, haemolysis, bruising, feeding difficulties and prior affected siblings.

PLAB application: Urgent bilirubin measurement is needed for jaundice in the first 24 hours.

Neonatal jaundice: assessment and treatment

Measure bilirubin using age-in-hours and gestation-specific NICE treatment thresholds; do not rely on visual inspection alone. Assess feeding, hydration and haemolysis risk. Phototherapy and exchange transfusion depend on thresholds and clinical context.

PLAB application: Conjugated jaundice, pale stools or dark urine raises concern for cholestasis or biliary atresia.

Early-onset neonatal infection

Risk factors include maternal infection, prolonged rupture of membranes and prematurity. Signs can be subtle: temperature instability, poor feeding, lethargy, respiratory distress, apnoea or poor perfusion. Escalate rapidly for suspected sepsis.

PLAB application: Follow NICE NG195 for assessment, cultures and timely empiric antibiotics when indicated.

Neonatal respiratory distress

Look for tachypnoea, recession, grunting, nasal flaring and cyanosis. Causes include transient tachypnoea of the newborn, respiratory distress syndrome, pneumonia, meconium aspiration and pneumothorax.

PLAB application: A baby with severe work of breathing, apnoea or hypoxia needs emergency neonatal review.

Hypoglycaemia and feeding

At-risk newborns include infants of mothers with diabetes, preterm infants and those who are small or unwell. Signs include jitteriness, poor feeding, lethargy, apnoea and seizures. Follow local neonatal glucose and feeding pathways.

PLAB application: Do not delay emergency treatment of a symptomatic or severely hypoglycaemic baby.

Bilious vomiting and abdominal red flags

Green bilious vomiting is a possible intestinal obstruction, including malrotation with volvulus, and is a surgical emergency. Abdominal distension, bloody stools, absent meconium or severe lethargy also need urgent assessment.

PLAB application: Never reassure or discharge a neonate with true green bilious vomit without emergency assessment.

Neonatal screening programmes

In the UK, newborn blood spot screening is usually offered at around day 5; hearing screening and NIPE are also offered. Explain screening, consent and follow-up of abnormal or incomplete results. Programme panels can change.

PLAB application: Know the distinction between screening and a confirmed diagnosis.

Common benign newborn findings

Milia, erythema toxicum, transient breast enlargement and some birthmarks may be benign. Differentiate from vesicular lesions, petechiae in an unwell baby, infection, abnormal red reflex or progressive swelling.

PLAB application: Examine the whole baby and check wellbeing before reassuring.

Discharge, safeguarding and safety-netting

Review feeding, weight, urine/stool output, jaundice, temperature, parental concerns and follow-up arrangements. Discuss safe sleep, vitamin K, immunisations and when to seek urgent help. Listen carefully to caregiver concerns.

PLAB application: Urgent help for poor feeding with lethargy, fever or hypothermia, apnoea, blue colour or green vomit.

PLAB 2 — Newborn check and parent counselling

Introduce yourself and confirm the baby’s identity, age and parental consent. Ask about pregnancy, delivery, feeding, wet nappies, stool, jaundice and parental concerns. Explain the head-to-toe examination, including heart, eyes, hips and testes as applicable. Explain findings in plain language, arrange referrals and give clear safety-netting.

Deep Dive

NIPE and screening versus diagnosis

The NIPE programme screens for selected eye, heart, hip and testicular conditions. An abnormal screen requires diagnostic follow-up; a normal screen does not guarantee absence of disease.

Jaundice timing matters

Jaundice in the first 24 hours needs urgent bilirubin measurement and evaluation for pathological causes. Treatment is guided by NICE age-in-hours and gestational-age charts, not a single universal bilirubin cut-off.

Respiratory distress differential

Transient tachypnoea often improves with supportive care, but pneumonia, respiratory distress syndrome, congenital heart disease and pneumothorax may look similar initially. Escalate based on clinical severity.

Newborn infection

Neonatal sepsis may present without fever. Risk factors, clinical examination, observations and NG195 pathways determine cultures, monitoring and antimicrobial treatment.

Hypoglycaemia and neuroprotection

Glucose management depends on symptoms, risk and local thresholds. Prompt treatment and repeat measurements are important, particularly with seizures or altered responsiveness.

Communication and safeguarding

Explain each examination step, seek consent, provide a chaperone where appropriate, document abnormalities and respond to parental concerns without dismissing them.
