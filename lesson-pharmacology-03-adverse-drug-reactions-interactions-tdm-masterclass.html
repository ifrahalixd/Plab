Pharmacology 03 · Clinical deep dive

The core science that explains why a standard dose can become unsafe.

PHARMACOKINETICS

1 · Exposure, clearance and accumulation

At a given dosing rate, average steady-state concentration is broadly inversely related to clearance. Reduced renal or hepatic elimination can increase exposure without any change to the prescribed dose. Volume of distribution affects loading doses; clearance is central to maintenance dosing. Acute kidney injury can develop faster than serum creatinine reaches a new steady state.

Clinical link: lithium, digoxin and aminoglycosides require special attention during dehydration, renal decline and interacting treatment.

RECEPTOR PHARMACOLOGY

2 · Pharmacodynamic interactions

Two agents acting on overlapping pathways can produce more than an expected therapeutic effect. Opioids and benzodiazepines both depress central nervous system activity; their combined use increases sedation and respiratory risk. NSAIDs impair platelet function and gastrointestinal mucosal protection, amplifying bleeding risk with anticoagulants.

IMMUNOLOGY

3 · Immune-mediated drug reactions

Immediate reactions can involve mast-cell mediator release with urticaria, bronchospasm and shock. Delayed T-cell-mediated reactions may involve widespread skin and internal-organ injury. Timing and phenotype help guide risk, but clinical presentation and severity determine urgent management.

METABOLISM

4 · Enzyme induction and inhibition

Inhibition can increase substrate concentrations relatively quickly; induction involves altered enzyme expression and often develops or resolves over days. The direction and clinical significance depend on whether the medicine is active itself or is a prodrug. Avoid assuming all CYP substrates respond identically.

BIOCHEMISTRY

5 · Protein binding and free drug

Only unbound drug is generally available to cross membranes and bind targets. Low albumin may change interpretation of total concentrations for highly protein-bound drugs such as phenytoin. A seemingly acceptable total level does not exclude excessive free exposure in the right clinical context.

TOXICOLOGY

6 · Concentration versus clinical toxicity

Symptoms, chronic exposure, tissue distribution, co-ingestants and sample timing all matter. Lithium neurological toxicity can be clinically important even when a single serum value is not dramatically raised. Treat the patient and seek poison-centre or specialist advice rather than waiting for a number.

MONITORING

7 · What makes a level interpretable?

Document dose, formulation, last dose time, blood sample time, changes in renal function, missed doses, interactions, and laboratory method. Know whether the regimen uses peak, trough, post-dose or AUC-based interpretation. Ask whether the result will change management.

PLAB 2

8 · Document and explain risk

Record the exact reaction and degree of certainty, explain uncertainty without minimising risk, provide safety-netting, update records and communicate with primary care. Serious suspected ADRs should be considered for MHRA Yellow Card reporting.

Worked clinical reasoning

Scenario 1 · Toxicity after dehydration

Stable chronic medicine + diarrhoea + new tremor = reassess clearance and exposure. Check symptoms, kidney function, interactions and timed drug level where relevant.

Scenario 2 · Rash after new drug

Determine severity before debating causality. Airway symptoms, mucosal lesions or blistering mean urgent escalation.

Scenario 3 · Two sedatives

Additive pharmacodynamic depression can occur even if neither drug changes the other’s plasma concentration.

Scenario 4 · Unexpected drug level

Check sample timing, missed doses, albumin, renal function and interactions before changing a dose.

A medicine can be correctly prescribed yet cause harm, or become dangerous when illness, an interaction or organ dysfunction changes exposure. This masterclass teaches you to recognise adverse drug reactions (ADRs), distinguish predictable toxicity from immune-mediated reactions, assess interactions and interpret therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) in clinical context.

In 60 seconds: the essentials

  • An ADR is a harmful, unintended response to a medicine used appropriately; a medication error is a preventable failure in the medication process.
  • Type A reactions are usually dose-related and predictable; type B reactions are less predictable and often not dose-related.
  • Suspected anaphylaxis, severe skin reactions, major bleeding, arrhythmia or organ failure require immediate assessment and escalation.
  • Interactions may be pharmacokinetic (alter drug concentration) or pharmacodynamic (alter combined effect).
  • A drug level is meaningful only with the dose, formulation, timing, renal function, clinical status and target range.
  • Report suspected serious or novel ADRs through the UK MHRA Yellow Card scheme.

1. What is an adverse drug reaction?

An adverse drug reaction is a harmful and unintended response associated with a medicine used at normal doses. An adverse event is an untoward event occurring during treatment, whether or not the medicine caused it. A medication error is a preventable problem in prescribing, dispensing, administration or monitoring. These concepts overlap: an error may cause an adverse event, but not all ADRs result from an error.

Do not assume that a symptom developing after a new prescription was caused by it. Establish the timeline, consider competing diagnoses and examine whether the suspected reaction is biologically plausible. Conversely, a familiar medicine can cause new toxicity when dehydration, acute kidney injury, dose escalation or interacting treatment changes exposure.

Allergy, intolerance and toxicity

Allergy involves an immune mechanism or a convincing immune-type clinical syndrome. Immediate reactions may cause urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm and anaphylaxis. Delayed reactions range from mild rash to life-threatening Stevens–Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis (SJS/TEN), DRESS or other severe reactions. Intolerance describes unpleasant effects not necessarily caused by allergy, such as gastrointestinal upset. Toxicity describes excessive harmful pharmacological exposure, sometimes despite an unchanged prescribed dose.

Do not miss: mucosal involvement, skin pain or blistering, fever with widespread rash, facial swelling, eosinophilia, hepatitis, respiratory compromise or hypotension. These demand urgent assessment; do not rechallenge a suspected culprit casually.

4. Investigating causality systematically

Step 1 — assess severity and stabilise.

Use ABCDE. Do not delay emergency treatment for a drug level, incident form or causality score.

Step 2 — build a medicine timeline.

Record drug name, indication, dose, route, start/stop dates, recent dose changes, last administration, OTC/herbal medicines and newly introduced interacting drugs. Include previous exposure and reaction history.

Step 3 — check the phenotype and alternative diagnoses.

Describe the event precisely: rash morphology, bleeding site, ECG findings, blood counts, renal/liver tests or neurological signs. Infection, dehydration, organ failure and disease progression may mimic ADRs or increase susceptibility.

Step 4 — evaluate plausibility and response.

Does the timing fit? Is the reaction known? Is there a dose–response relationship? Did it improve after stopping (dechallenge)? Rechallenge can support causality but may be unethical or dangerous after severe reactions.

Step 5 — document, communicate and report.

Record the suspected medicine, reaction, severity, date and degree of certainty. Update allergy alerts where appropriate, tell the patient and relevant teams, and use the MHRA Yellow Card system for suspected serious or important ADRs.

Structured tools such as the Naranjo algorithm can support review but cannot prove causation. Clinical judgement and specialist assessment remain essential.

5. Managing a suspected ADR

First treat the patient, not the laboratory result. Withhold the suspected medicine when clinically appropriate and safe; assess whether abrupt cessation creates withdrawal or disease-related risk. Support airway, breathing and circulation, treat the specific syndrome, correct contributing factors, and decide whether an antidote or reversal agent is indicated using local protocols.

Arrange appropriate observation and serial investigations. For example, severe bleeding requires resuscitation and anticoagulant-specific reversal decisions; suspected anaphylaxis requires immediate emergency treatment and subsequent observation according to current guidance. A mild, non-serious effect may instead be managed by dose adjustment or substitution after a risk–benefit discussion.

Clinical reasoning: stopping every medicine indiscriminately can be harmful. Prioritise the suspected culprit and interacting medicines, assess essential therapies and involve a pharmacist or senior clinician.

6. Drug interactions: pharmacokinetic versus pharmacodynamic

Pharmacokinetic (PK): one medicine changes exposure to another

Absorption: antacids or mineral supplements may bind certain antibiotics, reducing absorption; separate administration when the current product guidance recommends it. Metabolism: CYP enzyme inhibitors can increase concentrations of susceptible substrates; inducers can lower concentrations and may continue to affect metabolism after stopping. Elimination: impaired renal clearance or altered tubular handling can increase exposure. Transporters such as P-glycoprotein also influence absorption and elimination.

Pharmacodynamic (PD): effects combine at receptors or physiological systems

Opioids plus benzodiazepines may cause additive sedation and respiratory depression. Anticoagulants plus NSAIDs increase bleeding risk through several mechanisms. Multiple QT-prolonging medicines can increase arrhythmia risk, particularly with hypokalaemia or bradycardia. ACE inhibitors or ARBs combined with potassium-sparing therapies can increase hyperkalaemia risk.

Not every member of a drug class has the same interaction. Check the exact drug pair, formulation, dose and current BNF or local interaction resource rather than memorising a blanket rule.

7. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)

TDM measures a medicine concentration to support safe and effective dosing when exposure is variable, toxicity matters and the concentration can inform management. It is particularly useful for selected narrow-therapeutic-index medicines. It never replaces clinical assessment. Interpretation requires the time of last dose, sample time, dosing schedule, steady state, renal function, symptoms and assay-specific reference range.

Steady state, troughs and timing

For medicines with approximately first-order elimination, steady state is often approached after about 4–5 half-lives on a stable regimen. A trough is generally drawn immediately before the next scheduled dose, but some drugs require a specifically timed sample rather than a generic trough. A random concentration without timing may be uninterpretable. In acute toxicity, obtain urgent advice and appropriate samples even if routine steady-state timing is not met.

Worked interpretation — lithium

A patient taking lithium develops vomiting, coarse tremor and unsteady gait during a dehydrating illness. Even if a measured level is pending or not strikingly elevated, symptoms demand urgent clinical assessment. Withhold lithium pending specialist advice, check U&Es, renal function, ECG and a correctly timed level where appropriate, and seek urgent toxicology/medical advice. Clinical toxicity can be serious and does not always correlate neatly with a single level.

8. Patients at greater risk

Older adults: frailty, polypharmacy, falls, delirium, low muscle mass and renal impairment can magnify effects. Acute illness: dehydration, sepsis and AKI can change clearance rapidly; a printed eGFR may be unreliable during AKI. Pregnancy: consider maternal disease, fetal risks, gestation and specialist prescribing information. Children: verify weight-based calculations, age-specific pharmacokinetics and maximum doses. Liver disease: metabolism and protein binding may change, but the effect is drug-specific.

9. Four worked clinical cases

Case A — lithium toxicity during gastroenteritis

A 67-year-old on lithium reports diarrhoea, vomiting, coarse tremor and difficulty walking. A new NSAID was started. Reasoning: dehydration and interacting medicines may reduce clearance; neurological symptoms are red flags. Action: ABCDE, withhold lithium, review other medicines, urgent renal function/electrolytes, ECG, level with documented timing, and urgent senior/toxicology input. Do not rely on the number alone.

Case B — bleeding after an antibiotic

A 74-year-old on warfarin develops epistaxis and bruising after an antibiotic course. Reasoning: possible increased anticoagulant effect; infection and dietary change can also contribute. Action: assess haemodynamic stability and bleeding severity, obtain INR and relevant blood tests, manage according to local anticoagulation/bleeding protocol, review interacting medicines and arrange follow-up.

Case C — widespread blistering rash

A 29-year-old develops fever, painful widespread rash and mouth ulcers after starting a new medicine. Reasoning: suspect a severe cutaneous adverse reaction, not a simple intolerance. Action: urgent emergency assessment, stop suspected culprit after clinical review, assess airway and fluid status, obtain specialist dermatology/critical care input and document avoidance.

Case D — an unexpectedly high phenytoin result

A patient with epilepsy and hypoalbuminaemia has nystagmus and ataxia; the total phenytoin level appears only mildly elevated. Reasoning: reduced protein binding may increase free active drug. Action: assess clinically, review dose and interactions, consider free concentration or specialist interpretation, and avoid reflex dose escalation based on total level alone.

10. PLAB 2 OSCE — explain a suspected ADR

Station: A patient has developed a significant rash after starting an antibiotic and asks whether the doctor made a mistake. You have eight minutes to explain the concern, assess immediate safety and agree next steps.

Opening (0–2 min)

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, check current symptoms and red flags (breathing difficulty, facial swelling, blistering, mucosal involvement, fever), and assess immediate safety. Escalate urgently if severe features are present.

Explanation (2–5 min)

“The timing means this medicine could be responsible for your rash, although we need to examine you and consider other causes. A reaction does not necessarily mean the prescription was incorrect. Our first priority is to make sure you are safe.” Avoid falsely asserting causality.

Plan and closure (5–8 min)

Explain the assessment, whether the medicine should be withheld, alternative treatment, documentation, allergy referral if indicated, reporting and safety-netting. Invite questions, check understanding and provide clear follow-up. If an actual error is established, follow duty-of-candour requirements and be open about known facts.
