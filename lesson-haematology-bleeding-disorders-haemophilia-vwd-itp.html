Bleeding Disorders: Haemophilia, von Willebrand Disease & ITP

Emergency: Major bleeding, suspected intracranial haemorrhage, or thrombocytopenia with haemolysis requires urgent assessment and specialist help.

01. Overview

Bleeding disorders arise from platelet number or function, von Willebrand factor (VWF), coagulation factor deficiency or acquired disease. Haemophilia A is factor VIII deficiency; haemophilia B is factor IX deficiency; von Willebrand disease (VWD) involves VWF quantity or function; immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) causes immune-mediated low platelets.

PLAB focus: Separate mucocutaneous bleeding from deep-tissue bleeding.

02. Bleeding pattern

Epistaxis, gum bleeding, heavy menstrual bleeding and petechiae suggest platelet or VWF disorders. Haemarthrosis and deep muscle haematomas suggest coagulation factor deficiencies. Severity and family history matter, but no family history does not exclude inherited disease.

PLAB focus: Ask about dental extraction, surgery, childbirth and spontaneous bleeding.

03. Immediate assessment

Use ABCDE for active major bleeding; assess haemodynamics, neurological status and bleeding site. Obtain FBC/platelets, blood film, PT/INR, APTT, fibrinogen, renal and liver tests, group and save/crossmatch when needed. Do not delay life-saving treatment for results.

PLAB focus: Head injury or neurological symptoms in haemophilia require emergency factor treatment and imaging.

04. Haemophilia A and B

Haemophilia A is factor VIII deficiency; B is factor IX deficiency. They are usually X-linked, affecting males more often, though females can be symptomatic. Severity reflects baseline factor activity: severe <1%, moderate 1–5%, mild >5% to <40%.

PLAB focus: A prolonged APTT with normal PT and platelet count can suggest haemophilia, but a normal APTT does not exclude mild disease.

05. Haemophilia diagnosis

Measure factor VIII and IX activity; mixing studies can help distinguish factor deficiency from inhibitors. Ask about exposure to replacement therapy, new treatment failure and inhibitor risk. Genetics and specialist family counselling may be appropriate.

PLAB focus: Factor VIII can be low in VWD, so interpret results together.

06. Haemophilia treatment

Haemophilia centres guide prophylaxis, recombinant factor replacement and newer non-factor therapies such as emicizumab for eligible haemophilia A patients. Desmopressin can help selected mild haemophilia A patients after documented response; it does not treat haemophilia B.

PLAB focus: Avoid intramuscular injections and NSAIDs when possible; plan procedures with haematology.

07. Haemophilia emergencies

Suspected intracranial, neck, gastrointestinal or major muscle bleeding needs immediate specialist-led haemostatic treatment, resuscitation and appropriate imaging. A swollen painful joint may represent haemarthrosis; do not delay factor therapy while awaiting investigations.

PLAB focus: In known severe haemophilia with suspected critical bleeding, give prescribed factor promptly.

08. Von Willebrand disease

VWF mediates platelet adhesion and stabilises factor VIII. Type 1 is partial quantitative deficiency; type 2 is qualitative dysfunction; type 3 is near-complete deficiency. Symptoms often include nosebleeds, heavy menstrual bleeding and prolonged bleeding after procedures.

PLAB focus: VWD affects all sexes; menstrual and postpartum bleeding histories are important.

09. VWD investigations

Initial tests include FBC, PT, APTT; these may be normal. Specialist tests measure VWF antigen, VWF platelet-binding activity and factor VIII. VWF rises during inflammation, stress and pregnancy, so repeat testing may be needed.

PLAB focus: Normal routine clotting tests do not rule out VWD.

10. VWD management

Management depends on subtype, bleeding site and response: tranexamic acid for mucosal bleeding, desmopressin in responsive suitable subtypes, or VWF-containing concentrates for severe bleeding or procedures. Desmopressin requires attention to fluid restriction and hyponatraemia risk.

PLAB focus: Do not assume desmopressin is suitable for every VWD subtype.

11. Immune thrombocytopenia

ITP is an acquired immune-mediated thrombocytopenia, often isolated with otherwise normal blood counts and film. Presentation ranges from incidental low platelets to petechiae, purpura and mucosal bleeding. Diagnosis is clinical after excluding alternatives.

PLAB focus: Check medication history, infections, pregnancy and blood film for schistocytes or blasts.

12. ITP assessment and treatment

Urgency depends on bleeding, platelet count, trajectory and comorbidity. Mild cases may be observed under specialist advice. Adults requiring treatment often receive corticosteroids; IV immunoglobulin may be used when a rapid platelet rise is needed. Severe bleeding needs emergency specialist therapy, sometimes platelet transfusion alongside immunomodulation.

PLAB focus: Do not treat a platelet number in isolation: bleeding severity determines urgency.

13. Important differentials

TTP/HUS may cause thrombocytopenia with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia and organ injury; DIC causes consumption with abnormal coagulation tests; marrow failure may cause other cytopenias; drug-induced thrombocytopenia and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia need targeted evaluation.

PLAB focus: Thrombocytopenia plus schistocytes or neurological/renal findings is an emergency, not routine ITP.

14. Laboratory interpretation

Platelet disorders often have normal PT/APTT. Isolated prolonged APTT may reflect haemophilia, anticoagulants or inhibitors. Prolonged PT and APTT with low fibrinogen can occur in DIC. Interpret results alongside medicines, liver disease and sample quality.

PLAB focus: Always check whether the patient takes warfarin, heparin or a DOAC.

15. Procedures, pregnancy and safety

People with inherited bleeding disorders need an individual plan for dental work, surgery, pregnancy and delivery. Offer specialist counselling, medical alert identification and advice on bleeding-risk medicines. Heavy menstrual bleeding needs assessment for anaemia and appropriate treatment.

PLAB focus: For invasive procedures, coordinate with a haemophilia/VWD specialist centre.

16. PLAB 2 consultation and escalation

Take an empathetic bleeding history: onset, site, severity, family history, medicines, menstrual history, prior procedures and quality-of-life effects. Explain the likely category, investigations and referral; safety-net for persistent heavy bleeding, severe headache, head injury, melaena or haemodynamic symptoms.

PLAB focus: Never reassure a patient with major bleeding solely because the initial platelet count or APTT is normal.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Primary versus secondary haemostasis

Platelets and VWF form the initial plug. Coagulation factors stabilise it with fibrin. The clinical bleeding pattern often indicates which system is affected.

Why haemophilia causes haemarthrosis

Factor VIII/IX deficiency impairs thrombin generation and fibrin formation. Deep tissues and joints are particularly vulnerable; repeated joint bleeding can cause chronic arthropathy.

Why VWD can prolong APTT

VWF protects factor VIII from clearance. Markedly reduced VWF can lower factor VIII activity, but APTT may remain normal in mild disease.

Why ITP is a diagnosis of exclusion

There is no single confirmatory routine test. Isolated thrombocytopenia with an otherwise reassuring film supports ITP after excluding medications, infection, marrow disease and microangiopathy.

When the blood film changes everything

Schistocytes with anaemia and low platelets suggest thrombotic microangiopathy, which requires urgent treatment. Blasts or multiple cytopenias suggest marrow pathology.

OSCE: counselling with safety-netting

Explain the diagnosis in plain language, avoid unnecessary trauma and aspirin/NSAIDs unless prescribed, coordinate procedures and discuss emergency signs such as severe headache, persistent bleeding or collapse.
