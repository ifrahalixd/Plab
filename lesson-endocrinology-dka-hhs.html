DKA & HHS

Emergency: Suspected DKA or HHS needs urgent hospital treatment and monitoring; follow local adult JBDS-based protocols.

DKA and HHS overview

DKA is a hyperglycaemic emergency involving ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis. HHS features profound hyperglycaemia, hyperosmolality and dehydration, generally with little significant ketosis.

PLAB application: Both require urgent hospital assessment, ABCDE and senior input.

Recognising DKA

Polyuria, polydipsia, vomiting, abdominal pain, dehydration, Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath and altered consciousness may occur.

PLAB application: Check capillary glucose and blood ketones promptly; euglycaemic DKA is possible, especially with SGLT2 inhibitors.

Recognising HHS

HHS often develops over days in older adults with type 2 diabetes: extreme thirst, weakness, dehydration, confusion or seizures.

PLAB application: Look for infection, stroke, myocardial infarction and medication triggers.

DKA diagnostic pattern

Typical adult UK JBDS criteria: diabetes or glucose above 11 mmol/L; blood ketones at least 3 mmol/L (or urine ketones >2+); venous bicarbonate below 15 mmol/L and/or pH below 7.3.

PLAB application: Do not rule out DKA because glucose is only mildly elevated.

HHS diagnostic pattern

Marked hyperglycaemia (often ≥30 mmol/L), calculated osmolality ≥320 mOsm/kg, severe dehydration and absence of significant acidosis/ketonaemia characterise HHS.

PLAB application: Mixed DKA/HHS presentations occur and need specialist-guided management.

Immediate assessment

ABCDE, observations, mental state, volume status, weight if available, venous access, ECG, bedside glucose and ketones.

PLAB application: Assess shock, sepsis, cardiac ischaemia and consciousness early.

Initial investigations

Venous blood gas, glucose, ketones, electrolytes including potassium, urea/creatinine, full blood count, infection workup when indicated and ECG.

PLAB application: Repeat bedside glucose and ketones, blood gases and electrolytes according to protocol.

DKA fluids

Start IV isotonic crystalloid using adult DKA protocol, adjusting for heart failure, renal impairment, age and haemodynamic status.

PLAB application: Avoid blindly giving large fluid volumes to frail or fluid-overloaded patients.

DKA insulin

Use fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (commonly 0.1 units/kg/hour) under local JBDS-based protocol after initial assessment; continue usual long-acting basal insulin if prescribed.

PLAB application: Insulin clears ketones; add IV glucose when blood glucose falls while continuing ketone-clearing insulin.

Potassium safety

Total body potassium is depleted despite initially normal or high serum potassium; insulin can rapidly lower blood potassium.

PLAB application: Check potassium before and throughout treatment; follow protocol replacement thresholds and urgently escalate severe hypokalaemia.

DKA monitoring and resolution

Monitor capillary glucose and ketones hourly, with regular venous blood gases, bicarbonate and electrolytes. Resolution requires ketone clearance and correction of acidosis.

PLAB application: Do not stop IV insulin merely because glucose normalises.

HHS treatment priorities

Careful IV fluid replacement is the priority, with gradual fall in osmolality and glucose. Insulin is often delayed unless significant ketonaemia or glucose stops falling adequately with fluids.

PLAB application: Avoid rapid osmotic shifts; monitor fluid balance, sodium, osmolality and neurological status.

HHS thrombosis and complications

HHS carries high thrombosis risk; provide VTE prophylaxis when indicated and not contraindicated. Watch for fluid overload, hypoglycaemia and neurological deterioration.

PLAB application: Seek senior and critical care advice for severe cases.

Precipitating factors

Missed insulin, infection, new diabetes, myocardial infarction, stroke, steroids, SGLT2 inhibitors, vomiting and dehydration are common triggers.

PLAB application: Treat the cause alongside metabolic resuscitation.

Transition to subcutaneous insulin

Once DKA resolves and patient is eating/drinking, transition to subcutaneous insulin with an appropriate overlap per local protocol and diabetes-team advice.

PLAB application: Never stop IV insulin without a safe subcutaneous insulin plan.

Discharge and prevention

Review injection technique, access to insulin, ketone monitoring, sick-day rules, medication review and diabetes specialist follow-up.

PLAB application: Explain when to seek emergency help: vomiting, persistent ketones, breathlessness or confusion.

PLAB 2: DKA/HHS emergency assessment and counselling

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, perform ABCDE and call for senior help.
  2. Ask about insulin, missed doses, infections, vomiting, medications and sick-day practices.
  3. Explain glucose, ketone and acid-base tests in accessible language.
  4. Describe IV fluids, insulin where appropriate, electrolyte monitoring and treating triggers.
  5. Before discharge discuss insulin continuity, ketone checks, sick-day rules and urgent red flags.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Why ketones cause acidosis

Insulin deficiency increases lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis, producing beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate.

Why DKA potassium is deceptive

Acidosis and insulin deficiency shift potassium out of cells, masking total body losses from osmotic diuresis.

Why glucose is not the endpoint

Insulin must suppress ketone production and correct acidosis even after glucose concentration falls.

Why HHS develops slowly

Residual insulin may limit ketogenesis while hyperglycaemia drives prolonged osmotic diuresis and severe dehydration.

Osmolality reasoning

Calculate and trend osmolality according to local HHS guidance; sodium may rise as glucose falls, so assess the overall trend.

Mixed DKA/HHS

Patients may have both significant ketonaemia/acidosis and marked hyperosmolality; involve experienced clinicians and use appropriate combined protocols.
