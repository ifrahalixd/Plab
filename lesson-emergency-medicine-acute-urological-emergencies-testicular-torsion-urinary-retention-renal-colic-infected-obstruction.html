Acute Urological Emergencies: Testicular Torsion, Urinary Retention, Renal Colic & Obstructed Infected Kidney

Emergency red flags: sudden testicular pain, fever with obstructed urinary tract, anuria, urinary retention with neurological deficits, haemodynamic instability, painful erection lasting over four hours.

Emergency urology: first assessment

Assess ABCDE, vital signs, pain, fever, vomiting, urine output and haemodynamic stability. Identify sepsis, anuria, a solitary kidney, severe uncontrolled pain and an acute scrotum. Ask about pregnancy where relevant and consider abdominal or vascular causes.

PLAB application: Analgesia and resuscitation should start alongside urgent specialist referral when indicated.

Acute scrotal pain: immediate priorities

Sudden unilateral testicular pain, swelling, nausea or vomiting is torsion until excluded. Examine both testes and abdomen with consent and chaperone, noting lie, tenderness and cremasteric reflex, but no single finding safely rules out torsion.

PLAB application: Do not delay surgical assessment for ultrasound when torsion is suspected.

Testicular torsion: mechanism and timing

Twisting of the spermatic cord compromises testicular blood supply. Salvage declines as ischaemia continues; urgent scrotal exploration and detorsion with bilateral fixation are typically required. Pain may be intermittent if torsion detorses.

PLAB application: An apparently improved episode does not exclude intermittent torsion.

Torsion versus epididymo-orchitis

Epididymitis often develops more gradually with urinary symptoms or STI risk, but symptoms overlap substantially with torsion. Urinalysis and STI tests can help after immediate torsion assessment; a positive urine test does not definitively exclude torsion.

PLAB application: Never use an apparent infection to dismiss a classic torsion presentation.

Acute urinary retention: presentation

Inability to void with painful suprapubic distension may result from prostatic enlargement, constipation, medications, urethral obstruction, postoperative factors or neurological disease. Examine abdomen and consider bladder scan; assess renal function and infection.

PLAB application: Check for cauda equina symptoms and neurological deficits.

Urinary retention: decompression

Prompt bladder drainage is usually needed, commonly via urethral catheter by a trained clinician. If catheterisation is difficult or contraindicated, seek senior/urology help; suspected urethral injury after pelvic trauma is a contraindication to blind urethral catheterisation.

PLAB application: Document drained volume, urine appearance, renal function and a follow-up plan.

Post-obstructive diuresis and renal injury

After relieving longstanding obstruction, significant urine output can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbance. Monitor urine output, fluid balance, blood pressure, renal function and electrolytes, especially with large retained volumes or AKI.

PLAB application: Do not automatically discharge high-risk patients after catheter placement.

Renal colic: typical features

A ureteric stone causes sudden severe loin-to-groin pain, restlessness, nausea and sometimes haematuria. Examine for peritonism, fever and alternative diagnoses such as abdominal aortic aneurysm or ectopic pregnancy.

PLAB application: Absence of haematuria does not rule out a stone.

Renal colic: analgesia and imaging

For suspected renal colic, NICE recommends an NSAID first-line when appropriate, IV paracetamol if NSAIDs are contraindicated or insufficient, then opioids if needed. Adults typically undergo urgent low-dose non-contrast CT within 24 hours; pregnant patients should have ultrasound instead.

PLAB application: Choose imaging by pregnancy status, age, renal risk and local pathway.

Obstructed infected kidney: urological sepsis

Fever or systemic illness with urinary obstruction is a time-critical emergency. A blocked infected collecting system can rapidly cause septic shock and renal damage. Begin sepsis management, obtain cultures where feasible without delaying antibiotics, and arrange urgent decompression.

PLAB application: Antibiotics alone are not definitive source control when an infected kidney is obstructed.

Emergency decompression options

Urologists and interventional radiologists may drain an infected obstructed kidney with ureteric stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy. Definitive stone removal is generally delayed until infection is controlled.

PLAB application: Early senior urology involvement is essential.

Pyelonephritis versus infected obstruction

Pyelonephritis commonly causes fever, loin pain and urinary symptoms; obstruction must be considered with stones, anuria, AKI, severe sepsis or poor response. Urine cultures guide antibiotics and imaging assesses obstruction when suspected.

PLAB application: A septic patient with flank pain needs prompt evaluation for an obstructed system.

Haematuria and clot retention

Visible haematuria with inability to void may reflect clot retention and requires urgent urological assessment. Evaluate haemodynamic stability, anticoagulants, infection, malignancy risk and renal function. Three-way catheter irrigation may be needed under appropriate supervision.

PLAB application: Persistent gross haematuria or clot retention should not be treated as uncomplicated cystitis.

Priapism and paraphimosis

An erection lasting more than four hours, particularly painful rigid ischaemic priapism, requires urgent urology care to prevent erectile dysfunction. Paraphimosis is a retracted foreskin trapped behind the glans causing swelling and potential ischaemia; urgent reduction is required.

PLAB application: Recognise both as time-sensitive emergencies.

Cauda equina and urological red flags

New urinary retention or altered bladder sensation with saddle sensory changes, bilateral sciatica, leg weakness or bowel dysfunction suggests cauda equina syndrome. Perform focused neurological assessment and arrange emergency spinal assessment and MRI per pathway.

PLAB application: A bladder scan cannot exclude cauda equina syndrome.

PLAB 2 communication and safety-netting

Explain suspected diagnosis and urgency in plain language; obtain consent and offer a chaperone for genital examination. Discuss analgesia, imaging, catheter care or surgical referral and document the plan. Safety-net fever, rigors, worsening pain, inability to pass urine, vomiting and reduced urine output.

PLAB application: Use SBAR and explicit escalation for torsion, septic obstruction and neurological red flags.

PLAB 2 — acute scrotal pain and renal colic

Introduce yourself; assess pain, onset, urinary symptoms, systemic illness and red flags. Obtain consent and offer a chaperone before examination. Explain that suspected torsion requires immediate surgical assessment; suspected infected obstruction requires urgent antibiotics and drainage. Reassess, document and give an SBAR handover.

Deep Dive — Urological Emergencies

Torsion and the ischaemic clock

Testicular viability is time dependent. History and examination guide urgent exploration; imaging must not delay surgery when suspicion is high.

Septic obstruction: antibiotics plus source control

Resuscitate, take cultures if this does not delay treatment, give prompt IV antibiotics under local guidance and urgently drain the collecting system with stent or nephrostomy.

Renal colic versus dangerous mimics

Aortic aneurysm, ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis and pyelonephritis may mimic stones. Vital signs, examination, pregnancy testing when indicated and tailored imaging are essential.

Catheterisation and neurological causes

Check urethral injury risk after trauma and assess saddle sensation, lower-limb power and bladder symptoms for cauda equina. Monitor for post-obstructive diuresis.

Time-critical genital emergencies

Ischaemic priapism and paraphimosis need immediate urology assessment; delays can cause tissue injury.

Safe discharge and follow-up

Provide clear analgesia advice, return precautions, urine culture follow-up where relevant and definitive stone or catheter management plans.
