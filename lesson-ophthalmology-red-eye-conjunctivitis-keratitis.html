Red Eye, Conjunctivitis & Keratitis

Emergency red flags: reduced vision, severe pain, photophobia, corneal opacity, painful contact-lens red eye, chemical injury or suspected open globe.

The red eye approach

Assess pain, visual acuity, photophobia, laterality, discharge, contact lens use, trauma, chemical exposure and systemic symptoms. Check both eyes.

PLAB takeaway: Reduced vision, significant pain, photophobia, corneal opacity, irregular pupil or severe headache/vomiting are urgent red flags.

Eye anatomy

The conjunctiva lines eyelids and covers sclera. The transparent cornea is densely innervated. The anterior chamber lies between cornea and iris.

PLAB takeaway: Corneal disease is often painful and vision-threatening.

History in PLAB

Ask onset, unilateral/bilateral, itch, gritty sensation, discharge, vision change, photophobia, trauma, contact lenses, prior surgery, HSV and medications.

PLAB takeaway: Always ask whether contact lenses are worn and whether vision has changed.

Initial examination

Check visual acuity in each eye, pupils, ocular movements, conjunctiva, cornea, eyelids and discharge. Fluorescein with blue light identifies epithelial defects when appropriate.

PLAB takeaway: Do not delay urgent referral to complete every test.

Viral conjunctivitis

Often begins unilaterally then spreads, with watery discharge, gritty sensation and concurrent upper respiratory symptoms. Adenovirus is common.

PLAB takeaway: Supportive care and infection-control advice; antibiotics do not treat viral disease.

Bacterial conjunctivitis

Often causes sticky or mucopurulent discharge and eyelids stuck together on waking. Many uncomplicated cases resolve without antibiotics.

PLAB takeaway: Use hygiene, cleaning and appropriate lubricants; consider topical antibiotics only where indicated by local guidance.

Allergic conjunctivitis

Typically bilateral prominent itching, watery discharge and associated allergic rhinitis.

PLAB takeaway: Allergen avoidance, cool compresses, lubricants and topical antihistamine/mast-cell stabiliser when appropriate.

Contact-lens red eye

A contact-lens wearer with painful red eye, photophobia or reduced vision may have microbial keratitis, including Pseudomonas.

PLAB takeaway: Remove lenses, avoid reuse and arrange same-day emergency ophthalmic assessment; do not simply prescribe routine conjunctivitis drops.

Keratitis and corneal ulcer

Pain, photophobia, reduced vision, corneal haze/white infiltrate and fluorescein staining suggest corneal involvement.

PLAB takeaway: Microbial keratitis is sight-threatening and requires same-day specialist assessment and treatment.

Herpes simplex keratitis

May cause unilateral pain, photophobia and dendritic fluorescein staining. Recurrence is possible.

PLAB takeaway: Urgent ophthalmology review; avoid topical steroids unless directed by an ophthalmologist.

Corneal abrasion

Trauma or foreign body can cause sharp pain, tearing and photophobia; fluorescein highlights epithelial injury.

PLAB takeaway: Exclude penetrating injury and retained foreign body; refer urgently if high-risk or contact lens-associated.

Anterior uveitis

Typically painful red eye with consensual photophobia, ciliary flush and sometimes a small or irregular pupil.

PLAB takeaway: Same-day ophthalmology assessment; specialist-led cycloplegics and steroids.

Acute angle-closure glaucoma

Severe painful red eye with blurred vision/haloes, headache, nausea or vomiting, cloudy cornea and a mid-dilated poorly reactive pupil.

PLAB takeaway: Immediate emergency ophthalmology referral; sight-threatening high intraocular pressure.

Scleritis versus episcleritis

Episcleritis usually causes mild sectoral redness and discomfort. Scleritis causes deep severe boring pain and may associate with systemic inflammatory disease.

PLAB takeaway: Suspected scleritis needs urgent ophthalmic assessment.

Chemical injury and trauma

Immediately irrigate a chemically exposed eye with copious water or saline; continue until pH normalises with emergency assistance. For suspected open globe, shield and do not apply pressure.

PLAB takeaway: Never delay irrigation for history or formal visual-acuity testing in chemical exposure.

Counselling and safety net

Explain probable diagnosis, hygiene, no sharing towels, handwashing and when to return. Avoid contact lenses until resolved and cleared when appropriate.

PLAB takeaway: Urgent review for pain, photophobia, reduced vision, worsening swelling, corneal opacity or failure to improve.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Red eye

  1. Introduce yourself, consent, hand hygiene and assess pain.
  2. Ask about vision loss, photophobia, contact lenses, trauma and discharge.
  3. Measure visual acuity in each eye; examine pupils, lids, conjunctiva and cornea.
  4. Explain likely diagnosis and distinguish self-limiting illness from emergency.
  5. Discuss hygiene, treatment, follow-up and urgent safety-netting.

Deep Dive

Why pain matters

The cornea is richly innervated. Severe pain or photophobia raises concern for keratitis, uveitis, scleritis or glaucoma.

Discharge is not enough

Watery versus purulent discharge helps classification but never overrides reduced vision or severe pain.

Contact lenses

Lenses increase microbial keratitis risk; a painful red eye must be considered sight-threatening until assessed.

Fluorescein

Epithelial defects stain; dendritic lesions suggest HSV. Examination requires appropriate equipment and expertise.

Acute angle closure

Rapid intraocular pressure elevation causes corneal oedema, haloes, severe pain and nausea.

Chemical exposure

Immediate irrigation prevents ongoing tissue damage; treatment must not wait for diagnostic paperwork.
