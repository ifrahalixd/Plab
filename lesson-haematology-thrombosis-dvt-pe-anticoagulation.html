Thrombosis, DVT, Pulmonary Embolism & Anticoagulation

Emergency: Suspected PE with shock, syncope, severe hypoxia or haemodynamic instability needs immediate emergency assessment. Major anticoagulant bleeding is also an emergency.

1. Venous thromboembolism

VTE comprises deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). Thrombi usually form in deep leg veins; emboli can travel to pulmonary arteries.

PLAB application: A patient with unexplained unilateral leg swelling needs structured DVT assessment.

2. Virchow triad

Venous stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability contribute to thrombosis. Risk factors include surgery, immobility, cancer, pregnancy, oestrogen exposure and previous VTE.

PLAB application: Ask about recent operations, long travel, hormones and cancer.

3. DVT symptoms and signs

Typical findings are unilateral calf or whole-leg swelling, tenderness, warmth and erythema; some DVTs are clinically silent. Differential diagnoses include cellulitis, ruptured Baker cyst and muscular injury.

PLAB application: Physical findings alone cannot confirm or exclude DVT.

4. DVT Wells score

Use the two-level DVT Wells score to classify DVT as likely (2 or more) or unlikely (1 or less). Include alternative diagnosis at least as likely, which subtracts two points.

PLAB application: Follow NICE NG158 diagnostic pathways rather than relying on gestalt.

5. DVT diagnostic pathway

For likely DVT, arrange proximal leg vein ultrasound within 4 hours where possible; if delayed, D-dimer and interim therapeutic anticoagulation with ultrasound within 24 hours, subject to safety assessment. For unlikely DVT, start with D-dimer.

PLAB application: A negative initial scan may require repeat ultrasound depending on D-dimer and pathway.

6. Pulmonary embolism presentation

PE may cause sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxia, haemoptysis or syncope. Massive PE can cause hypotension and shock.

PLAB application: Assess ABCDE immediately in an unstable patient.

7. PE Wells score

Use the two-level PE Wells score: more than 4 points means PE likely; 4 or less means PE unlikely. Clinical signs of DVT and PE being more likely than another diagnosis are heavily weighted.

PLAB application: Do not use a normal oxygen saturation to rule out PE.

8. PE diagnostic pathway

For likely PE, obtain CTPA promptly (or V/Q imaging when appropriate); if imaging cannot be done immediately, offer interim therapeutic anticoagulation if safe. For unlikely PE, use D-dimer; PERC may help selected very-low-risk patients.

PLAB application: Age-adjusted D-dimer thresholds may be considered for people over 50 under local policy.

9. Unstable PE

Haemodynamic instability suggests high-risk PE. Resuscitate, urgently involve senior/critical care teams, obtain appropriate imaging or bedside assessment and consider reperfusion therapy with specialist input.

PLAB application: Systemic thrombolysis is not routine for stable PE.

10. Initial anticoagulation

For confirmed proximal DVT or PE, anticoagulation is generally offered for at least 3 months. NICE often recommends apixaban or rivaroxaban first-line for eligible adults; exceptions include pregnancy, certain renal problems and antiphospholipid syndrome.

PLAB application: Check renal function, liver function, FBC, bleeding risk and interactions.

11. DOACs and warfarin

DOACs include apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban and dabigatran. Warfarin requires INR monitoring and has multiple food/drug interactions. Dabigatran and edoxaban require initial parenteral anticoagulation for VTE treatment.

PLAB application: Do not interchange dosing schedules; consult current BNF.

12. Heparins and pregnancy

Low-molecular-weight heparin is commonly used for VTE during pregnancy; warfarin and DOACs are generally avoided in pregnancy. Suspected VTE in pregnancy requires urgent obstetric-led investigation.

PLAB application: Pregnancy changes imaging and anticoagulant choices.

13. Duration and recurrence

After 3 months, review whether VTE was provoked or unprovoked, recurrence risk, bleeding risk and patient preference. Cancer-associated VTE and recurrent VTE may need longer treatment.

PLAB application: Do not automatically stop anticoagulation at 3 months.

14. Anticoagulant bleeding

Major bleeding requires urgent assessment, withholding anticoagulants, resuscitation, source control and specialist-directed reversal where appropriate. Reversal agents depend on drug and availability.

PLAB application: Ask about melaena, haematuria, severe headache, trauma and falls.

15. Complications and prevention

DVT can cause post-thrombotic syndrome; PE can cause chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension. Hospitalised patients need individual VTE and bleeding risk assessment and suitable prevention.

PLAB application: Encourage mobilisation where appropriate; prophylaxis is not the same as treatment.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety net

Explain diagnosis and tests, anticoagulant adherence, missed doses according to medicine leaflet, bleeding precautions, interactions, pregnancy plans and follow-up. Advise emergency help for severe breathlessness, chest pain, collapse or major bleeding.

PLAB application: Use teach-back and discuss practical access to medication and monitoring.

PLAB 2 — Suspected DVT consultation

Introduce yourself, confirm identity and consent. Ask about onset, laterality, pain, swelling, PE symptoms, recent immobility or surgery, pregnancy, hormones, cancer, prior VTE, medications and bleeding risks. Examine vital signs and legs sensitively; explain Wells scoring, D-dimer and ultrasound. Discuss interim anticoagulation only after bleeding-risk assessment and arrange appropriate urgent care. Safety-net for breathlessness, chest pain, fainting and major bleeding.

Deep Dive

Wells scores are decision aids

DVT and PE Wells scores differ and are not interchangeable. Use the two-level score appropriate to the suspected condition; testing depends on the resulting probability.

D-dimer interpretation

A negative D-dimer helps exclude VTE in appropriate low-probability pathways. Elevated D-dimer is nonspecific and occurs with infection, cancer, surgery and pregnancy. Do not use D-dimer alone to diagnose VTE.

Antiphospholipid syndrome

Triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome requires specialist treatment planning; NICE recommends a vitamin K antagonist rather than a DOAC for confirmed proximal DVT or PE in this group.

Anticoagulant safety

Baseline tests and bleeding risk are assessed without unnecessarily delaying urgent anticoagulation. Renal function, body weight, interactions and pregnancy influence agent and dose.

Thrombolysis and IVC filters

Consider systemic thrombolysis for PE with haemodynamic instability, not routinely for stable PE. IVC filters are reserved for specific circumstances such as contraindications to anticoagulation, following specialist assessment.

Diagnostic pitfalls

A normal ECG, chest radiograph or oxygen saturation does not exclude PE. Bilateral leg swelling does not exclude DVT; alternative diagnoses require consideration.
