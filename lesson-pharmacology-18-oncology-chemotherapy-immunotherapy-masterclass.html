Deep Dive · Cancer pharmacology and clinical reasoning

Why tumour and normal tissues are both injured

DNA-damaging and antimitotic therapies disproportionately affect proliferating cells, but tumour selectivity is imperfect. Myelosuppression, mucositis, alopecia and gonadal toxicity follow from effects on normal tissue. Tumour biology, drug exposure and host genetics change risk.

Resistance mechanisms

Cancer cells can amplify target kinases, acquire binding-site mutations, activate bypass pathways, increase drug efflux or alter DNA repair. Re-biopsy or circulating tumour DNA testing may identify actionable resistance in selected diseases.

Dose calculations are high risk

Body surface area is often estimated from height and weight; carboplatin uses a renal-function-based formula in many regimens. Do not independently infer a chemotherapy dose from generic equations: verify actual protocol, dosing weight, renal estimation, dose caps and independent pharmacy checks.

Immune tolerance and checkpoint blockade

PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 signalling normally attenuate T-cell activation. Blocking these checkpoints can restore antitumour immunity but also cause auto-inflammatory injury to gut, lung, endocrine organs, liver and myocardium. Organ-specific grading and specialist escalation determine treatment.

Recognition of time-critical complications

Neutropenic sepsis: immediate emergency pathway. TLS: ECG and serial potassium, phosphate, calcium, urate, creatinine; urgent specialist treatment. Suspected spinal cord compression: emergency assessment and imaging. Extravasation: stop infusion and use specific antidote/protocol if indicated.

Communication station: chemotherapy counselling

Explore the patient’s understanding, goals and concerns. Explain intent and alternatives, common toxicities and serious red flags in plain language; discuss contraception/fertility, vaccination and drug interactions. Check teach-back, provide written 24-hour emergency contact information, document consent and coordinate the specialist team.

Cancer therapy is regimen-specific. Always assess intent, biomarkers, organ function, infection risk, fertility, interactions and toxicity monitoring. Fever or sudden deterioration after systemic anticancer treatment demands immediate assessment for neutropenic sepsis; immune checkpoint toxicities can present late and affect any organ.

Oncology prescribing: clinical priorities

Anticancer treatment is prescribed by specialist teams after confirming tumour histology, stage, biomarkers, performance status, comorbidities, patient goals and consent. Distinguish curative, adjuvant, neoadjuvant and palliative intent. A regimen is not just a drug: dose, cycle, route, premedication, hydration, rescue medicines, laboratory thresholds and toxicity follow-up all matter. A patient with fever after chemotherapy needs urgent assessment regardless of whether the temperature seems modest.

Cell cycle and therapeutic index

Cancer drugs target DNA synthesis, mitosis or tumour-specific signalling but many also damage normal rapidly dividing marrow, gut epithelium, hair follicles and germ cells. Cell-cycle-specific agents depend on proliferative phase; other drugs act throughout the cycle. The therapeutic index can be narrow and combination regimens seek complementary activity while balancing overlapping toxicity. Myelosuppression often produces a delayed nadir; exact timing depends on regimen.

Alkylating agents and platinum compounds

Cyclophosphamide forms DNA cross-links after hepatic activation; toxicities include myelosuppression, infertility and haemorrhagic cystitis from acrolein. Mesna and hydration may be prescribed with selected regimens. Cisplatin forms DNA adducts and can cause nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, neuropathy and profound emesis; monitor renal function and electrolytes, especially magnesium. Carboplatin causes relatively more myelosuppression and dosing commonly incorporates renal function. These agents are not interchangeable.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

Antimetabolites: methotrexate, fluoropyrimidines and cytarabine

Methotrexate inhibits folate-dependent nucleotide synthesis; high-dose oncology regimens require protocolised hydration, urine management, methotrexate level monitoring and folinic acid rescue. Fluorouracil and capecitabine inhibit thymidylate synthase and can cause diarrhoea, mucositis, hand-foot syndrome and cardiotoxicity. DPD deficiency increases severe fluoropyrimidine toxicity risk: pretreatment DPYD testing is recommended in relevant UK pathways. Cytarabine is a pyrimidine analogue used in haematological malignancy and can cause myelosuppression and, at high doses, cerebellar toxicity.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

Anthracyclines and topoisomerase inhibitors

Doxorubicin intercalates DNA and inhibits topoisomerase II; cumulative exposure increases risk of cardiomyopathy. Assess baseline cardiac function when indicated and monitor according to regimen. Extravasation can cause tissue injury and requires immediate local protocol response. Etoposide also inhibits topoisomerase II. Irinotecan inhibits topoisomerase I and may cause acute cholinergic diarrhoea and delayed severe diarrhoea, which need different management approaches.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

Microtubule drugs: taxanes and vinca alkaloids

Paclitaxel and docetaxel stabilise microtubules, preventing mitotic function; neuropathy, myelosuppression and hypersensitivity reactions are important. Vincristine blocks microtubule polymerisation and can cause peripheral/autonomic neuropathy, constipation and ileus. CRITICAL: vincristine is for intravenous use only; intrathecal administration is fatal. Specialist administration safeguards are essential.

Endocrine cancer therapies

Tamoxifen is a selective oestrogen-receptor modulator used in hormone-receptor-positive breast cancer; risks include venous thromboembolism and endometrial pathology. Aromatase inhibitors such as letrozole reduce oestrogen synthesis and can worsen bone health and cause arthralgia. Androgen-deprivation therapies for prostate cancer can cause hot flushes, metabolic changes and bone loss. Treatment depends on menopausal status, tumour biology and specialist protocols.

Targeted therapies and tumour biomarkers

Imatinib inhibits BCR–ABL tyrosine kinase in appropriate chronic myeloid leukaemia and certain other molecularly selected cancers. EGFR, ALK, HER2, BRAF and other alterations may guide targeted therapy, but mutation status and approved indications matter. Targeted does not mean non-toxic: kinase inhibitors can cause rash, diarrhoea, hypertension, liver injury, QT prolongation or cardiotoxicity depending on agent. Review interactions and required monitoring.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

Monoclonal antibodies and anti-VEGF therapy

Trastuzumab targets HER2 and can cause cardiac dysfunction; serial cardiac assessment is required under the treatment protocol. Rituximab targets CD20 B cells and may cause infusion reactions and hepatitis B reactivation; screen before treatment. Bevacizumab targets VEGF and can cause hypertension, proteinuria, bleeding and impaired wound healing. Match toxicity surveillance to the specific drug.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors: PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4

Pembrolizumab and nivolumab inhibit PD-1 signalling; atezolizumab targets PD-L1 and ipilimumab targets CTLA-4. These medicines remove inhibitory brakes on antitumour T-cell responses. Immune-related adverse events may involve skin, bowel, liver, lung, pituitary, thyroid, adrenal glands, kidney or heart, sometimes after treatment has stopped. New diarrhoea, cough, breathlessness, headache or profound fatigue warrants prompt oncology assessment. Management depends on grade and organ; severe cases may require holding therapy and systemic corticosteroids under specialist guidance.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

CAR-T and other cellular treatments

CAR-T therapy engineers a patient’s T cells to recognise selected tumour antigens. Major complications include cytokine release syndrome (fever, hypotension, hypoxia) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (confusion, seizures). Treatment is restricted to specialised centres; tocilizumab may be used for cytokine release syndrome according to protocols. Prolonged cytopenias and infections can occur.

Neutropenic sepsis: do not delay

A patient receiving systemic anticancer therapy who becomes unwell may have neutropenic sepsis even without a high fever. Immediately assess ABCDE, obtain observations, lactate, FBC, renal function, cultures and other tests as clinically appropriate, and give empiric IV antibiotics promptly according to local neutropenic sepsis guidance; do not wait for the neutrophil count if suspicion is high. NICE NG151 emphasises immediate assessment and empiric antibiotic treatment. Source control, fluids and escalation may be needed.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

Tumour lysis syndrome

Rapid malignant cell breakdown releases potassium, phosphate and nucleic acids, producing hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperuricaemia and AKI. Risk is high with some haematological malignancies and highly effective treatments. Prevention uses risk assessment, hydration and urate-lowering therapy such as allopurinol or rasburicase under specialist protocols. Established TLS requires urgent electrolyte/ECG monitoring and renal/critical care input; rasburicase is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency.

Extravasation, hypersensitivity and infusion reactions

Pain, burning, swelling or loss of blood return during a vesicant infusion suggests extravasation. Stop the infusion, leave the cannula in place initially for aspiration if directed, and follow the local extravasation protocol; do not flush the line. Infusion reactions range from mild symptoms to anaphylaxis: stop the infusion, assess ABCDE and give emergency treatment as indicated. Premedication and restart decisions are drug-specific.

Nausea, mucositis, diarrhoea and supportive care

Antiemetic prophylaxis is tailored to emetogenic risk, commonly combining a 5-HT3 antagonist, dexamethasone, an NK1 antagonist and/or olanzapine for high-risk regimens according to protocol. Persistent vomiting can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbances. Oral mucositis can compromise nutrition and become an infection portal. Severe diarrhoea after chemotherapy or immunotherapy needs urgent assessment for dehydration, infection and drug-specific toxicity; do not simply recommend loperamide without evaluating red flags.

Clinical prescribing checkpoint: verify the current oncology protocol and BNF for dose, contraindications, organ-function adjustment, monitoring intervals, interactions and patient-specific risk. Escalate unexpected toxicity to the treating oncology team promptly.

Bone marrow suppression, anaemia and bleeding

Cytotoxic therapy may reduce neutrophils, haemoglobin and platelets. Monitor FBC before cycles and when symptoms occur. Fever or new infection signs during neutropenia are emergencies; bleeding with thrombocytopenia may require urgent specialist assessment and transfusion support. G-CSF prophylaxis depends on regimen febrile-neutropenia risk and individual patient factors. Counsel patients on bleeding, infection and when to contact the 24-hour oncology line.

Renal, hepatic, reproductive and interaction safety

Check renal and hepatic function, allergies, performance status, prior toxicities and interacting medicines before each cycle. Some doses depend on body surface area, weight or renal function, but calculations and dose modifications must be independently verified using the exact oncology protocol. Discuss fertility preservation, pregnancy avoidance and breastfeeding restrictions before treatment. Live vaccines and immunosuppressive therapies require specialist review. Avoid herbal or OTC medicines without interaction checks.

Clinical cases and PLAB communication

Case 1: A woman 8 days after chemotherapy has rigors and feels faint: suspected neutropenic sepsis → urgent ABCDE, cultures and prompt IV antibiotics per protocol. Case 2: A patient on pembrolizumab has worsening diarrhoea and abdominal pain: consider immune-mediated colitis, infection and dehydration → urgent oncology assessment. Case 3: A patient on cisplatin has rising creatinine and low magnesium: assess cisplatin nephrotoxicity, hydration and electrolyte replacement. Case 4: A patient with rapidly responding lymphoma develops hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia and AKI: treat tumour lysis syndrome urgently. PLAB 2: explain treatment purpose, common and serious harms, fertility issues, monitoring, emergency contact advice and shared decisions without promising outcomes.

    NICE / BNF · Oncology safety pathways

    Educational algorithms only. Follow current local oncology regimens, emergency protocols, NICE and BNF.

    Pre-treatment safety

    Confirm diagnosis, stage, biomarkers and treatment intent → consent and fertility discussion → baseline FBC, renal, liver and regimen-specific tests → medication and infection review → independently verify prescription → arrange monitoring and emergency contact.

    Suspected neutropenic sepsis

    ABCDE and senior help → urgent observations, cultures and blood tests → prompt IV antibiotics per local protocol without waiting for neutrophil results → fluids, source control and escalation as clinically needed.

    Possible immune-related adverse event

    Identify checkpoint drug and last dose → assess organ-specific symptoms and severity → consider infection and other causes → urgent oncology advice → withhold treatment and start immunosuppression only according to graded specialist pathway.

    Suspected tumour lysis syndrome

    ABCDE, ECG, IV access and serial potassium, phosphate, calcium, urate and renal function → urgent oncology/renal input → hydration and urate-lowering treatment as indicated → manage hyperkalaemia and dialysis indications.
