Gallstones, Acute Cholecystitis & Cholangitis

Surgery · Lesson 11 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Gallstone disease: recognise obstruction, inflammation and sepsis

Emergency: Fever/rigors, jaundice and right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain suggest ascending cholangitis. Hypotension or confusion indicates severe infection: start sepsis resuscitation, IV antibiotics and arrange urgent biliary drainage with senior surgical/gastroenterology teams.

1 · Anatomy and mechanisms

Gallstones form in the gallbladder. Transient cystic duct blockage causes biliary colic; persistent cystic duct obstruction leads to acute cholecystitis. A common bile duct (CBD) stone causes choledocholithiasis, often with cholestatic liver tests and jaundice. An infected obstructed biliary tree causes ascending cholangitis. Stones at the ampulla can trigger acute pancreatitis.

3 · Assessment and investigations

ABCDE; assess observations, pain, vomiting, hydration, fever, jaundice and sepsis. Examine RUQ, Murphy sign and peritonism. Request FBC, CRP, U&E, LFTs (bilirubin, ALP, ALT), lipase/amylase where pancreatitis suspected, blood cultures before antibiotics when this will not delay care, coagulation, pregnancy test where relevant, and lactate in suspected sepsis. RUQ ultrasound is first-line imaging for gallstones and gallbladder inflammation; check duct dilatation. If CBD stones are suspected but not seen on ultrasound, consider MRCP or EUS. CT may assess complications or alternative diagnoses, but is less sensitive for many gallstones.

4 · Biliary colic and symptomatic stones

Give appropriate analgesia (often an NSAID if safe), antiemetics and hydration. If uncomplicated and symptoms settle, discharge with clear safety-netting and arrange surgical assessment. NICE CG188 recommends laparoscopic cholecystectomy for symptomatic gallbladder stones; asymptomatic stones in a normal gallbladder generally do not need treatment.

5 · Acute cholecystitis

Admit, provide analgesia, fluids as needed, antiemetics, monitoring and antibiotics according to clinical severity/local antimicrobial policy. NICE recommends early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within one week of diagnosis where feasible. If surgery is contraindicated or fails in a severely ill patient, specialist gallbladder drainage (e.g. percutaneous cholecystostomy) may be considered. Watch for empyema, gangrene, perforation and peritonitis.

6 · Common bile duct stones and cholangitis

CBD stones may require ERCP for duct clearance, or surgical duct exploration depending on expertise. Cholangitis is a time-critical infected obstruction: give IV antibiotics covering likely biliary pathogens per local protocol, resuscitate, seek urgent senior and endoscopy input for biliary drainage. ERCP with sphincterotomy/stone extraction or stent is common; alternative drainage may be required if ERCP is not possible. Definitive gallbladder treatment follows stabilisation where indicated.

7 · Important complications and traps

Mirizzi syndrome: impacted cystic duct/Hartmann pouch stone compresses common hepatic duct, causing obstructive jaundice. Gallstone ileus: large stone passes via cholecystoenteric fistula and causes mechanical small bowel obstruction (often at terminal ileum); CT may show pneumobilia, ectopic stone and obstruction. Ascending cholangitis: Charcot triad is helpful but insensitive—do not wait for all three features. Pancreatitis: not every case requires urgent ERCP.

Clinical pathways & rapid revision

Pathway A — RUQ pain

ABCDE and sepsis screen → assess duration, fever and jaundice → FBC/CRP/U&E/LFTs ± lipase → RUQ ultrasound → biliary colic (transient, afebrile) vs cholecystitis (persistent inflammatory pain) vs duct stone (cholestasis) vs cholangitis (infected obstruction).

Pathway B — Suspected acute cholecystitis

Admit → analgesia/fluids ± antibiotics → early surgical review → laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 1 week of diagnosis if suitable → consider drainage if unfit for surgery.

Pathway C — Fever + jaundice + RUQ pain

Suspect cholangitis → sepsis resuscitation, cultures where feasible, IV antibiotics → urgent surgical/gastroenterology consultation → biliary decompression (usually ERCP).

Slide 1 — Three different ducts

Cystic duct transient blockage = colic; persistent cystic duct blockage = cholecystitis; infected obstructed CBD = cholangitis.

Slide 2 — Imaging

Ultrasound first; MRCP/EUS for suspected occult CBD stones; ERCP is primarily therapeutic.

Slide 3 — PLAB danger signs

Hypotension, confusion, jaundice, rigors, peritonism or rising lactate warrant immediate escalation.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Post-meal episodic pain

A 36-year-old has recurrent RUQ pain lasting 90 minutes after fatty meals, now settled, afebrile and well. Ultrasound reveals stones without inflammatory changes. Answer: symptomatic gallstones/biliary colic. Analgesia, safety-net and elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy referral.

Case 2 — Persistent RUQ pain and fever

A 49-year-old has 18 hours of RUQ pain, fever, vomiting, positive Murphy sign and inflammatory markers. Answer: acute cholecystitis. Admit, treat symptoms, antibiotics according to severity/local policy, and early cholecystectomy assessment.

Case 3 — Jaundice and septic shock

A 72-year-old has rigors, jaundice, RUQ pain, BP 82/48 and confusion. Answer: severe ascending cholangitis. ABCDE, sepsis management, IV antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage, typically ERCP; involve seniors immediately.

Case 4 — Cholestatic LFTs, no fever

A 58-year-old has jaundice and pale stools; ultrasound shows gallstones and dilated CBD but no definite duct stone. Answer: suspected choledocholithiasis. Discuss MRCP/EUS and specialist management, with ERCP for confirmed/high-likelihood duct stones when appropriate.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute RUQ pain station

Brief: A 55-year-old presents with right upper abdominal pain, fever and yellow eyes. Assess, explain likely diagnosis and immediate management.

Station checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, consent and assess immediate stability.
  2. Ask pain onset, site, radiation, duration, meals, previous episodes and severity.
  3. Ask about fever/rigors, jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, itch, vomiting and confusion.
  4. Review gallstone history, prior surgery/ERCP, alcohol, medication, pregnancy possibility and comorbidities.
  5. Assess observations and sepsis; examine abdomen including RUQ tenderness and Murphy sign.
  6. Explain blood tests including LFTs, inflammatory markers, kidney function, cultures and lipase if indicated.
  7. Explain urgent ultrasound and possible MRCP or ERCP.
  8. Escalate suspected cholangitis immediately; initiate resuscitation, antibiotics and specialist biliary drainage pathway.
  9. Discuss cholecystectomy after appropriate stabilisation when indicated.
  10. Safety-net: worsening pain, fever, jaundice, fainting or confusion requires emergency attention.

Patient explanation: “A gallstone may be blocking the tube that drains bile. Because you also have a fever, we are concerned about infection behind that blockage. We need to treat the infection quickly and may need a camera procedure to relieve the blockage.”

Rapid revision and UK references

  1. Biliary colic = transient cystic duct obstruction, episodic pain without systemic inflammation.
  2. Cholecystitis = persistent pain, fever, RUQ tenderness/Murphy sign.
  3. Ultrasound is first-line for gallbladder stones; MRCP/EUS assesses suspected occult CBD stones.
  4. NICE CG188 recommends early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 1 week of acute cholecystitis diagnosis.
  5. CBD stones can cause jaundice and require specialist duct clearance.
  6. Cholangitis = infected biliary obstruction; treat sepsis and arrange urgent drainage, commonly ERCP.
  7. Charcot triad may be incomplete; hypotension/confusion signal severe illness.
  8. Gallstone pancreatitis needs supportive management; urgent ERCP when cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction warrants it.
