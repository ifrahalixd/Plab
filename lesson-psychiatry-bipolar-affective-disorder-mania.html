Bipolar Affective Disorder & Mania

PLAB priority: Recognise mania, distinguish hypomania, assess immediate risks and arrange specialist care.

01. Overview

Bipolar disorder involves episodes of depression and mania or hypomania, often separated by periods of recovery. Bipolar I involves at least one manic episode; bipolar II involves hypomania and major depression without a history of mania.

PLAB application: Ask about past elevated or irritable mood whenever assessing depression.

02. Mania recognition

Mania is a sustained period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood and increased energy/activity, with marked functional impairment, possible psychosis or admission. Typical symptoms include reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, grandiosity, distractibility and risky behaviour.

PLAB application: A patient sleeping two hours but feeling energetic and spending recklessly may be manic.

03. Hypomania versus mania

Hypomania shares the mood and energy changes but is less severe, without marked functional impairment, psychosis or need for admission. Duration thresholds differ by classification system; assess the complete clinical pattern rather than a duration alone.

PLAB application: Psychosis during an elevated episode points toward mania rather than hypomania.

04. Bipolar depression

Depressive episodes may dominate the illness course. Ask about previous hypomanic or manic episodes, family history and antidepressant-associated switching before labelling recurrent depression as unipolar.

PLAB application: Avoid antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar disorder without specialist assessment.

05. History taking

Establish onset, duration, sleep, energy, speech, thoughts, mood, spending, sexual risk, substance use, adherence, past episodes, psychosis, self-care, collateral history and consequences for work and relationships.

PLAB application: Collateral history from relatives may reveal behavioural change not recognised by the patient.

06. Mental state examination

Assess appearance and behaviour, psychomotor activity, rapport, speech rate, mood and affect, thought form/content, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. Mania may produce pressured speech, flight of ideas and grandiose delusions.

PLAB application: Describe objective findings rather than writing only “manic”.

07. Risk and safeguarding

Assess suicide/self-harm, impulsivity, driving, financial exploitation, aggression, vulnerability, neglect, dependants and capacity for specific decisions. Psychosis or severe disinhibition may require urgent assessment.

PLAB application: Immediate danger or inability to care for self warrants same-day crisis assessment.

08. Differential diagnoses

Consider substance-induced mood symptoms (stimulants, cocaine), corticosteroid effects, hyperthyroidism, schizoaffective disorder, schizophrenia, ADHD, personality-related emotional instability and delirium.

PLAB application: Check physical illness and medication history before attributing behavioural change to bipolar disorder.

09. Physical assessment and investigations

Check observations, physical/neurological examination and targeted tests such as thyroid function, FBC, electrolytes, renal/liver function, glucose and drug/toxicology tests when indicated. ECG and metabolic assessment may be needed before treatment.

PLAB application: Investigations identify mimics and establish safe medication baselines; no blood test diagnoses bipolar disorder.

10. Acute mania management

Use a calm, low-stimulation environment, de-escalation and urgent specialist assessment. NICE CG185 recommends offering haloperidol, olanzapine, quetiapine or risperidone for mania/hypomania when not already taking antipsychotic or mood stabiliser, tailored to patient factors.

PLAB application: Severe mania, psychosis or major risk often requires hospital-based care.

11. Existing medication in mania

Review adherence, substances and interactions. NICE advises considering stopping antidepressants when mania/hypomania emerges; medication changes should be clinically supervised. If already on lithium, check level and optimise as indicated with specialist guidance.

PLAB application: Do not abruptly change complex psychiatric regimens without appropriate review.

12. Bipolar depression treatment

NICE CG185 options include fluoxetine combined with olanzapine or quetiapine alone for moderate/severe bipolar depression, with lamotrigine considered in specified circumstances. Treatment is specialist-led and adapted to history and preference.

PLAB application: Lamotrigine is not a rapid treatment for acute mania.

13. Lithium monitoring

Lithium can prevent relapse but requires renal and thyroid monitoring, serum levels, interaction checks and counselling on hydration, illness and toxicity. ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs and some diuretics can raise lithium levels.

PLAB application: Vomiting, diarrhoea, coarse tremor, ataxia or confusion in someone taking lithium: consider toxicity urgently.

14. Other medicines and pregnancy

Antipsychotics require metabolic and movement-disorder monitoring. Valproate has major reproductive and developmental risks and UK prescribing restrictions; check current MHRA requirements and seek specialist advice. Pregnancy planning requires proactive medication review.

PLAB application: Do not initiate valproate casually; current UK reproductive-risk safeguards apply.

15. Relapse prevention

Develop an individual relapse plan identifying early warning signs, sleep disruption, stressors, medication adherence, psychological interventions, family involvement and rapid-access contacts. Regular sleep and routine can help.

PLAB application: Ask the patient to name their earliest personal sign of mania.

16. PLAB 2 communication

Explain that episodes are treatable, avoid stigmatising language, discuss shared decisions, consent/capacity, confidentiality, support and follow-up. In acute mania, use brief, clear questions and calm boundaries.

PLAB application: Practise explaining why urgent review is needed without arguing with grandiose beliefs.

Deep Dive

Mood episode biology

Bipolar disorder is multifactorial: genetic susceptibility, circadian rhythm disruption, neurotransmitter signalling and environmental stress interact. A single chemical imbalance does not explain the syndrome.

Why sleep matters

A marked reduction in the need for sleep can be both an early warning sign and a factor that destabilises mood. Ask about sleep quantity, fatigue and recent routine changes.

Mania versus psychosis

Psychotic symptoms can occur in severe mania, often with mood-congruent grandiosity. Establish whether psychosis occurs only during mood episodes or independently to guide differential diagnosis.

Antidepressant switching

Antidepressants can be associated with emergence of mania or hypomania in susceptible people. Always assess prior elevation and family history; review any antidepressant when switching occurs.

Lithium pharmacology and safety

Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index and renal elimination. Dehydration, acute illness and interacting medicines can increase toxicity risk. Monitor levels and renal/thyroid function.

Valproate reproductive safeguards

Valproate exposure during pregnancy carries substantial risks to the fetus. UK MHRA restrictions and risk-minimisation requirements evolve; specialist teams must follow current rules for patients of reproductive potential.
