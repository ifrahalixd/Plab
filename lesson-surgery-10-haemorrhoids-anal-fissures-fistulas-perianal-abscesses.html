Haemorrhoids, Anal Fissures, Fistulas & Perianal Abscesses

Surgery · Lesson 10 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Common anorectal conditions: distinguish pain, bleeding and sepsis

Do not label all rectal bleeding as haemorrhoids. Consider colorectal cancer, IBD and other pathology. Severe constant anal pain with fever or systemic illness suggests abscess or deeper infection: urgent surgical assessment.

1 · Focused anorectal history and examination

Ask about bleeding (on paper, dripping, mixed with stool), pain during/after defecation, pruritus, lump/prolapse, discharge, fever, altered bowel habit, weight loss, anaemia symptoms, constipation/straining, pregnancy, anticoagulants, immunosuppression and Crohn's disease. Check observations and assess for sepsis. With consent, chaperone and privacy, inspect perianal skin; perform DRE only if appropriate and tolerated. Do not force a painful DRE in suspected acute fissure or abscess. Arrange proctoscopy/anoscopy or colorectal evaluation when indicated.

2 · Haemorrhoids (piles)

Internal haemorrhoids typically cause painless bright-red bleeding with defecation and may prolapse. External thrombosed haemorrhoids may cause an acutely painful, tender bluish perianal lump. Internal haemorrhoid grades: I bleed without prolapse; II prolapse and reduce spontaneously; III require manual reduction; IV irreducible prolapse. Initial management: fibre and fluids, avoid straining, treat constipation, short-term symptom relief. Persistent symptomatic internal haemorrhoids may be treated with rubber-band ligation; selected advanced/refractory cases require specialist procedural or surgical options. Haemorrhoids do not rule out malignancy; investigate red flags via appropriate NICE pathways.

3 · Anal fissure

A tear in anoderm produces sharp pain during defecation, often lasting hours, with small-volume bright-red bleeding. Most primary fissures are posterior midline; a lateral, multiple or atypical fissure raises concern for Crohn's disease, infection or malignancy. Treat with stool softening, fibre, hydration, warm baths, appropriate analgesia and topical treatment per local guidance (e.g. glyceryl trinitrate 0.4% rectal ointment for chronic fissure when suitable; headache is common). Specialist options for persistent chronic fissure include botulinum toxin or lateral internal sphincterotomy, with continence-risk counselling.

4 · Perianal abscess

Acute severe constant throbbing pain, swelling, tenderness, erythema, sometimes fever; deep abscesses may have little visible swelling. Assess for sepsis, diabetes, immunosuppression and necrotising infection. Prompt incision and drainage is definitive management by the appropriate surgical team. Antibiotics alone do not replace drainage; consider antibiotics for systemic infection, extensive cellulitis, immunocompromise or other specialist indications. Do not delay emergency management for routine imaging in straightforward superficial disease; image suspected deep/complex abscess when guided by specialists.

5 · Anal fistula

Abnormal tract between anal canal and perianal skin, often following an abscess. Presents with recurrent discharge, intermittent swelling, irritation or repeated abscess. Examine for an external opening; avoid blind probing. Refer to colorectal surgery; MRI pelvis may delineate complex tracts, especially in Crohn's disease. Treatment balances eradication with preservation of continence: fistulotomy for suitable simple low tracts, seton or sphincter-sparing options for complex/high tracts. Treat associated Crohn's disease with specialist input.

PLAB traps: An abscess needs drainage; fistula management must protect the sphincter; a fissure is painful whereas uncomplicated internal haemorrhoids usually are not; rectal bleeding needs risk-based assessment.

Clinical pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Bright-red rectal bleeding

ABCDE if unstable → bleeding history and red flags → examination with consent/chaperone → risk-based investigation or suspected-cancer pathway → treat identified cause; never presume piles without assessment.

Pathway B — Anal pain

Pain only with/after stool + small blood → fissure likely → soften stool, analgesia, topical treatment. Constant throbbing pain + swelling/fever → abscess → urgent surgical review and drainage. Sudden tender blue lump → thrombosed external haemorrhoid → analgesia and specialist assessment if severe/early.

Pathway C — Recurrent perianal discharge

Suspect fistula → assess Crohn's disease and previous abscess → colorectal referral → MRI for complex tracts → fistulotomy/seton/sphincter-sparing management according to anatomy.

Slide 1 — Haemorrhoid grades

I no prolapse · II spontaneous reduction · III manual reduction · IV irreducible.

Slide 2 — Pain differentiator

Fissure: pain with defecation; abscess: constant throbbing pain; internal haemorrhoids: typically painless bleeding.

Slide 3 — Emergency triggers

Sepsis, immunocompromise, rapidly spreading perineal infection, severe uncontrolled bleeding or suspected malignancy warrant escalation.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Painless bleeding

A 42-year-old describes fresh blood on tissue after straining, no pain. Examination suggests internal haemorrhoids. Approach: assess red flags, bowel habits and relevant risk; provide fibre, fluids and constipation advice; arrange investigation when indicated and consider banding if persistent.

Case 2 — Painful bowel motions

A 31-year-old has sharp tearing pain on passing stool and streaks of bright blood. A posterior midline fissure is visible. Approach: avoid painful DRE, treat constipation, analgesia and local fissure therapy; review persistence or atypical features.

Case 3 — Fever and painful swelling

A 56-year-old with diabetes has constant worsening anal pain, fever and a tender fluctuant swelling. Approach: assess for sepsis, urgent surgical review and drainage; antibiotics if systemic infection or cellulitis, and assess for deeper infection.

Case 4 — Recurrent discharge

A 27-year-old with Crohn's disease has recurrent purulent perianal drainage after an abscess. Approach: suspect fistula, assess for recurrent abscess, refer colorectal/IBD MDT; consider pelvic MRI and sphincter-preserving management.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute rectal bleeding and anal pain station

Brief: A 38-year-old reports bright-red bleeding and pain during defecation. Take a focused history, explain the likely diagnosis and safe management.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, privacy, consent and offer chaperone for examination.
  2. Assess haemodynamic instability or heavy bleeding first.
  3. Characterise bleeding and pain, including relation to bowel motions, quantity and duration.
  4. Ask about bowel habit changes, weight loss, fatigue, family history, inflammatory bowel disease and medication.
  5. Ask about constipation, straining, discharge, fever, recurrent abscess and continence.
  6. Explain inspection and that DRE may be deferred if very painful; avoid forcing examination.
  7. Discuss likely fissure versus haemorrhoids and need to exclude serious causes when indicated.
  8. Offer stool-softening advice, analgesia and appropriate topical treatment; explain follow-up and referral thresholds.
  9. Safety-net: heavy bleeding, dizziness, fever, worsening constant pain, swelling or unexplained weight loss require prompt review.
  10. Document and provide a structured handover.

Patient explanation: “The pain with bowel movements and small amount of fresh blood may be caused by a tiny tear near the anus. We can usually treat this by keeping stools soft and helping the area heal, but I also want to check for any warning signs that need further tests.”

Rapid revision & references

  1. Internal haemorrhoids: usually painless bright-red bleeding; grades I–IV depend on prolapse.
  2. Fissure: severe defecation-associated pain and small fresh bleeding; posterior midline is common.
  3. Atypical lateral/multiple fissures require evaluation for secondary causes.
  4. Perianal abscess: prompt surgical drainage; antibiotics alone are not definitive.
  5. Fistula: recurrent discharge after abscess; colorectal referral and continence-preserving treatment.
  6. Fournier gangrene and sepsis are emergencies.
  7. Do not assume rectal bleeding is benign; assess NICE colorectal cancer red flags and FIT/referral criteria as applicable.
