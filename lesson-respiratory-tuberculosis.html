Tuberculosis: diagnosis, infection control and treatment

Emergency reminder: Suspected infectious pulmonary TB requires appropriate airborne precautions. Severe haemoptysis, confusion or respiratory failure requires emergency escalation. This lesson is educational, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

TB overview

Tuberculosis is infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex, usually affecting the lungs but potentially any organ. Active pulmonary or laryngeal TB may be infectious.

Clinical application: Differentiate active TB disease from latent TB infection (LTBI), which has no symptoms and is not infectious.

Transmission and risk

TB spreads through airborne particles from people with infectious pulmonary or laryngeal disease. Risk increases with prolonged indoor exposure, crowding, and poor ventilation.

Clinical application: Ask about household contacts, previous TB treatment, travel or residence in high-incidence settings, immunosuppression, HIV and social risk factors without stigma.

Symptoms of pulmonary TB

Persistent cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue and sometimes haemoptysis are common. Some people have few symptoms.

Clinical application: Ask about symptom duration, contacts and systemic features. Haemoptysis or respiratory compromise requires urgent assessment.

Extrapulmonary TB

TB can involve lymph nodes, pleura, meninges, spine, bone, abdomen, genitourinary tract and pericardium. Presentation depends on the organ involved.

Clinical application: Suspected TB meningitis, spinal cord compression or pericardial tamponade needs emergency specialist care.

Initial infection-control actions

Suspected infectious pulmonary TB requires prompt assessment and appropriate airborne precautions according to local infection-prevention policy.

Clinical application: Arrange a suitable isolation environment and respiratory protection where indicated. Notify the relevant TB/infection-control team early.

Chest imaging

Chest radiography may show upper-zone infiltrates, cavitation, miliary changes or pleural effusion, but appearances are variable and a normal film does not always exclude TB.

Clinical application: Use imaging to guide assessment, not as the sole diagnostic test.

Microbiological diagnosis

Obtain appropriate respiratory samples for mycobacterial smear microscopy, culture and molecular testing, including drug-resistance testing where available. Culture supports confirmation and susceptibility testing.

Clinical application: For suspected pulmonary TB, follow NICE NG33 sampling guidance; do not rely on a single negative smear to exclude disease.

Latent TB infection

LTBI means immune sensitisation to TB without evidence of active disease. It is not infectious but can reactivate.

Clinical application: IGRA or tuberculin skin testing can support LTBI assessment in selected groups; exclude active TB before preventive treatment.

HIV and immune suppression

HIV, immunosuppressive drugs and some other conditions increase progression risk and may produce atypical presentations.

Clinical application: Offer HIV testing according to guidance and assess relevant drug interactions and opportunistic disease.

Drug-susceptible TB treatment

Standard treatment for drug-susceptible pulmonary TB commonly begins with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (HRZE) for two months, followed by isoniazid and rifampicin (HR) for four months.

Clinical application: This is a common six-month regimen, not a universal prescription: adjust for site, susceptibility, adverse effects and specialist guidance. Never self-prescribe.

Drug-resistant TB

Rifampicin-resistant and multidrug-resistant TB require specialist regimens based on susceptibility, patient factors and current national guidance.

Clinical application: Do not apply the standard six-month regimen to resistant TB. Arrange expert TB service input.

Treatment monitoring and adverse effects

Monitor adherence, clinical response, drug interactions and adverse effects. Rifampicin can turn body fluids orange and has important interactions; isoniazid may cause neuropathy; pyrazinamide and several agents may cause liver injury; ethambutol can affect vision.

Clinical application: Check baseline and follow-up tests as recommended, counsel on warning symptoms, and consider pyridoxine with isoniazid when indicated.

Public health and contact tracing

TB is a notifiable disease in the UK. Public health teams coordinate notification, contact investigation, screening and support.

Clinical application: Explain that notification protects contacts and is handled confidentially. Follow local statutory reporting arrangements.

Adherence and patient-centred care

TB treatment is lengthy; barriers include adverse effects, housing, language, transport and stigma. Supported treatment or directly observed therapy may help selected patients.

Clinical application: Use shared decision-making, interpreters, social support and clear safety-netting. Never blame patients for adherence difficulties.

BCG vaccination

BCG vaccination is offered selectively in the UK to groups at increased risk, not universally to all adults.

Clinical application: Follow current UKHSA/Green Book eligibility and local TB-service advice.

PLAB 2 TB OSCE

Explore symptoms and exposure respectfully, explain the reason for tests and infection precautions, discuss treatment duration and contact tracing.

Clinical application: Safety-net for worsening breathlessness, significant haemoptysis, severe headache, confusion, weakness or medication toxicity.

PLAB 2: TB counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself and obtain consent; explore symptoms and concerns without stigma.
  2. Ask about exposure, contacts, travel, prior TB, immune suppression and medicines.
  3. Explain sputum testing, imaging and infection precautions.
  4. Discuss treatment duration, adherence support, adverse effects and drug interactions.
  5. Explain confidential notification, contact tracing and emergency warning signs.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Granuloma formation

Cell-mediated immunity may contain organisms in granulomas. Loss of immune control can permit reactivation.

Why culture matters

Culture can identify viable organisms and support phenotypic drug susceptibility, but may take weeks; molecular tests can provide earlier information.

Why multidrug therapy

Using several active drugs reduces the risk of resistance and treats bacterial populations in different metabolic states.

TB and HIV

Immune suppression increases active disease risk and can change symptoms, imaging and treatment interactions.

Public health reasoning

Early identification, appropriate precautions, notification and contact investigation help prevent onward transmission.

Adverse-effect reasoning

Liver injury, visual disturbance, neuropathy and drug interactions require counselling and prompt review.
