Hair, Nail & Pigmentation Disorders

PLAB priority: Distinguish scarring from nonscarring alopecia; recognise kerion and nail melanoma red flags.

01. Structured assessment

Ask about onset, progression, focal versus diffuse change, itch, pain, scaling, hair care practices, medication, recent illness, nutritional intake and family history. Examine scalp, hair shafts, nails, skin and mucosa.

PLAB application: Scarring, inflammation, systemic symptoms and rapid progression change urgency.

02. Hair-cycle physiology

Anagen is active growth, catagen transition and telogen rest. Telogen effluvium follows a shift of follicles into telogen, with shedding typically appearing two to three months after a trigger.

PLAB application: Ask about childbirth, febrile illness, surgery and major stress months before diffuse shedding.

03. Androgenetic alopecia

Pattern hair loss causes progressive follicular miniaturisation: vertex/frontotemporal thinning in men and widening central part in women. The scalp generally lacks inflammation or scarring.

PLAB application: Discuss expectations; topical minoxidil is an option, and oral finasteride may be considered for suitable adult men with counselling.

04. Alopecia areata

Immune-mediated nonscarring hair loss produces smooth, well-defined patches; exclamation-mark hairs and nail pitting may occur. Eyebrows and eyelashes can be affected.

PLAB application: Recognise association with other autoimmune disease but do not order indiscriminate screening without clinical indications.

05. Telogen effluvium

Diffuse shedding follows illness, postpartum change, iron deficiency, thyroid dysfunction, medication or restrictive diet. Follicular openings are preserved.

PLAB application: Investigate potential causes guided by history; reassure that shedding often improves once the trigger resolves.

06. Scarring alopecia

Follicular destruction causes permanent loss of follicular openings, sometimes with erythema, perifollicular scale, pustules or tenderness. Examples include lichen planopilaris and central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia.

PLAB application: Prompt dermatology referral matters because lost follicles cannot regrow.

07. Traction alopecia and hair practices

Repeated tension from tight braids, extensions or hairstyles causes marginal hair loss; early disease may reverse, but chronic traction may scar. Chemical or heat damage can break shafts.

PLAB application: Ask nonjudgmentally about styling and advise reducing traction; refer if scarring suspected.

08. Tinea capitis

Fungal scalp infection may cause scale, broken hairs, black dots, patchy loss and lymphadenopathy. A boggy inflammatory kerion risks scarring.

PLAB application: Obtain appropriate fungal sampling and treat with systemic antifungal therapy; topical shampoo alone is insufficient.

09. Nail anatomy and examination

Inspect all nails for pitting, onycholysis, subungual debris, colour change, clubbing, splinter haemorrhages and surrounding skin disease. Review trauma and occupational wet work.

PLAB application: Nail findings may reveal psoriasis, fungal infection, iron deficiency or systemic illness.

10. Onychomycosis

Fungal nail infection often causes thickening, yellow-white discoloration, distal onycholysis and crumbly subungual debris. Psoriasis and trauma can look similar.

PLAB application: Confirm fungus with nail sampling before prolonged oral antifungals when indicated; consider liver disease and interactions.

11. Nail psoriasis and paronychia

Psoriasis can cause pitting, oil-drop patches and onycholysis. Acute paronychia is painful periungual inflammation, sometimes with abscess; chronic paronychia is associated with irritant wet exposure.

PLAB application: Check for psoriatic arthritis; drain a fluctuant abscess when clinically appropriate.

12. Melanonychia and nail melanoma

A longitudinal pigmented band may be benign, especially with multiple stable bands, but a new or changing single dark band, irregular colour/width, nail destruction or pigment on surrounding skin warrants concern.

PLAB application: Urgently assess suspected subungual melanoma; do not dismiss concerning changes in darker skin.

13. Vitiligo

Acquired depigmented macules result from melanocyte loss. Common sites include face, hands and body folds; Wood lamp can aid assessment. Psychological impact can be substantial.

PLAB application: Consider topical anti-inflammatory therapy for limited active disease and dermatology referral for extensive or distressing disease.

14. Melasma and post-inflammatory pigment change

Melasma causes symmetric facial hyperpigmentation associated with UV/visible light and hormonal influences. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or hypopigmentation follows acne, eczema or trauma and is prominent in darker skin.

PLAB application: Treat the underlying inflammation and advise broad-spectrum sun protection; avoid unregulated skin-lightening products.

15. Other pigment differentials

Pityriasis versicolor produces finely scaling lighter or darker trunk patches; pityriasis alba produces ill-defined pale patches, often in children with eczema. Albinism is congenital, with photoprotection and visual needs.

PLAB application: Scale, distribution and history help distinguish pigment loss from fungal or inflammatory change.

16. Referral and communication

Refer urgently for suspicious nail pigmentation or rapidly destructive disease; refer promptly for scarring alopecia, kerion or significant inflammatory hair loss. Ask about distress, identity and treatment goals.

PLAB application: For PLAB 2, explain diagnostic uncertainty, realistic regrowth times, safety-netting and shared decisions.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Why telogen effluvium is delayed

The precipitating stress shifts many follicles prematurely into resting phase; visible shedding occurs when those hairs are subsequently released. Timing is often more useful than a single laboratory test.

Follicular openings and irreversible loss

Nonscarring alopecias preserve follicular stem-cell architecture. In cicatricial alopecia inflammation destroys the follicle, so early anti-inflammatory specialist management aims to protect remaining hair.

Nail matrix versus nail bed

Matrix injury alters plate formation, producing pitting or ridging. Nail-bed separation causes onycholysis; subungual hyperkeratosis may arise from psoriasis or fungi. Examination helps localise pathology.

Why pigmentation differs in darker skin

Inflammation can increase or disrupt melanin transfer and leave long-lasting colour change even after the rash resolves. Erythema can also be less visually obvious, so symptoms, scale and texture matter.

Immune and infectious hair loss

Alopecia areata targets anagen follicles without scarring, whereas dermatophytes infect keratinised hair shafts and can trigger a destructive kerion inflammatory response.

Counselling and treatment realism

Hair growth is slow; improvement is often assessed over months. Explain limitations, pregnancy considerations for relevant medicines, side effects, cosmetic choices and emotional impact without promising regrowth.
