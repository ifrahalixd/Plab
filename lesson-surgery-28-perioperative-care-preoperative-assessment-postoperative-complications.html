Perioperative care: safe assessment, optimisation and recovery

Red flags: new postoperative hypotension, tachycardia, hypoxia, confusion, severe pain, oliguria, bleeding or fever with deterioration require immediate ABCDE, senior review and investigation. Never dismiss deterioration as routine postoperative recovery.

1 · Preoperative assessment and risk stratification

Confirm procedure, urgency, indication, patient understanding and capacity. Review medical conditions, previous anaesthetic problems, allergies, medications, anticoagulants, alcohol, smoking, functional capacity, frailty, nutritional status, pregnancy possibility and obstructive sleep apnoea. Examine airway, heart, lungs and volume status. Consider ASA physical status and procedure-specific risk; use validated risk tools where indicated. In emergency surgery, stabilise while avoiding harmful delay to source control.

2 · Investigations and optimisation

Order tests based on comorbidity and procedure, not indiscriminately: FBC for anaemia or blood loss risk, U&E for renal/electrolyte issues, ECG for relevant cardiovascular indications, coagulation tests when indicated and group-and-save/crossmatch for anticipated blood loss. NICE NG45 guides routine preoperative testing. Optimise diabetes, blood pressure, anaemia, nutrition, respiratory disease and heart failure when time permits. Assess capacity and obtain informed consent including material risks, alternatives and the option of no treatment; document discussion.

3 · Medication planning

Review anticoagulants/antiplatelets individually against bleeding and thrombotic risk; coordinate interruption and resumption with anaesthesia/surgery and local protocols. Consider renal function, indication and neuraxial procedures. Manage insulin and other glucose-lowering drugs using a perioperative diabetes protocol; SGLT2 inhibitors often need withholding before surgery because of euglycaemic ketoacidosis risk. Review steroids for adrenal suppression and stress-dose requirements. Never advise universal cessation of all medications.

4 · Fasting, fluids and infection prevention

Follow local anaesthetic fasting instructions; commonly clear fluids are allowed until 2 hours and solids until 6 hours before elective anaesthesia, with exceptions and individual risk assessment. Do not use these intervals to delay emergency life-saving surgery. Assess fluid balance, prescribe appropriate maintenance or resuscitation using NICE CG174 and monitor electrolytes. Use indicated prophylactic antibiotics timed to incision, surgical safety checklist, skin preparation, normothermia and glycaemic control to reduce surgical site infections.

5 · WHO surgical safety and VTE prevention

Use WHO surgical safety checklist: Sign In (before anaesthesia), Time Out (before incision) and Sign Out (before leaving theatre). Confirm identity, procedure, site, consent, allergies, airway concerns, antibiotic prophylaxis and equipment. Assess VTE and bleeding risk, encourage early mobilisation and use mechanical/pharmacological prophylaxis where appropriate under NICE NG89. Document reassessment if clinical status changes.

6 · Immediate postoperative care

Structured recovery handover includes procedure, anaesthesia, blood loss, fluids, analgesia, antibiotics, drains, complications and escalation plan. Monitor ABCDE, NEWS2, pain, sedation, nausea, temperature, wound/drains, urine output and fluid balance. Treat pain with multimodal analgesia tailored to renal function, respiratory status and opioid risk. Prevent delirium, pressure injuries, hypothermia and pulmonary complications; mobilise and feed as appropriate.

7 · Postoperative haemorrhage and shock

Consider internal bleeding with tachycardia, hypotension, falling haemoglobin, abdominal distension, increasing drain output or poor perfusion; early haemoglobin can be deceptively normal. Call for senior surgical/anaesthetic help, resuscitate, activate major haemorrhage protocol when indicated, check bloods and arrange urgent operative or interventional source control. Avoid delays for imaging in unstable patients.

8 · Respiratory and thromboembolic complications

Postoperative hypoxia may reflect atelectasis, aspiration, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, opioid-induced hypoventilation or pulmonary embolism. Assess ABCDE, oxygenation, respiratory rate, examination and relevant tests; give oxygen when indicated and treat the cause. Sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia or collapse raises concern for PE; use NICE NG158 diagnostic/treatment pathways and account for recent surgery and bleeding risk.

9 · Infection, anastomotic leak and ileus

Wound erythema/pus suggests surgical site infection; unexplained tachycardia, fever, abdominal pain, peritonism or sepsis after bowel surgery suggests anastomotic leak or intra-abdominal collection. Urgent senior review, antibiotics for suspected serious infection, imaging if stable and source control as required. Postoperative ileus causes distension, nausea, vomiting and reduced bowel function; assess electrolytes, opioids and obstruction, give supportive care, and investigate persistent or worsening symptoms.

10 · Other complications and discharge safety

Acute kidney injury: monitor creatinine, urine output, volume status and nephrotoxins; identify sepsis/obstruction. Urinary retention: bladder scan and catheterise when indicated. Delirium: search for infection, pain, hypoxia, medication effects and metabolic disturbance. Wound dehiscence or evisceration is an emergency: cover exposed bowel with sterile saline-moistened dressings, keep NBM and seek urgent surgery. Discharge only when observations, pain control, mobility, intake, wound plan, medications, VTE plan and safety-netting are appropriate.

Clinical algorithms and high-yield comparisons

Before surgery

Confirm indication/urgency → history, comorbidities, frailty, medications and allergies → targeted investigations → optimise modifiable risks → consent and shared decision-making → anaesthetic/surgical plan.

WHO checklist

Sign In: identity, site, consent, airway/allergies → Time Out: team confirms procedure, antibiotics and anticipated issues → Sign Out: counts, specimens, equipment and postoperative plan.

Postoperative deterioration

ABCDE + NEWS2 → call senior team → check bleeding, hypoxia, sepsis, PE, AKI and medication effects → investigations and resuscitation in parallel → definitive source control when needed.

Postoperative tachycardia

Think pain, hypovolaemia/haemorrhage, sepsis/leak, hypoxia, PE, arrhythmia. Do not attribute persistent tachycardia to anxiety without assessment.

Postoperative abdominal distension

Ileus: diffuse reduced bowel motility, often after abdominal surgery. Mechanical obstruction: colicky pain, transition point on imaging; worsening pain, peritonism or systemic instability → urgent surgical review.

VTE and medication safety

Balance thrombosis against bleeding; individualise anticoagulant interruption/restart and prophylaxis. Early mobilisation, hydration and mechanical measures when appropriate.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Elective hernia repair in diabetes

A 58-year-old with type 2 diabetes takes an SGLT2 inhibitor and apixaban for AF. Approach: assess procedure bleeding risk, renal function and thromboembolic risk; make an individualised perioperative medication plan with the surgical/anaesthetic team, including SGLT2 withholding under protocol.

Case 2 — Early postoperative hypotension

Two hours after colectomy, a patient is pale, tachycardic and hypotensive with increasing bloody drain output. Approach: immediate ABCDE, senior help, bloods/crossmatch, resuscitation and urgent haemorrhage source control.

Case 3 — Day 5 after bowel surgery

A patient develops persistent tachycardia, fever, abdominal pain and new confusion. Approach: treat as possible sepsis/anastomotic leak, urgent surgical review, resuscitation, antibiotics and imaging if stable; arrange source control.

Case 4 — Sudden postoperative breathlessness

On day 4 after hip surgery, a patient develops sudden dyspnoea, tachycardia and pleuritic chest pain. Approach: ABCDE, oxygen if hypoxic, urgent assessment for PE and other causes, and investigations/treatment guided by NICE pathways and bleeding risk.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Preoperative assessment and counselling

Station: A 67-year-old scheduled for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy asks whether surgery is safe because they have diabetes and take anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, ask concerns and expectations; assess understanding and capacity.
  2. Explain indication, expected benefits, alternatives and option of no operation.
  3. Ask about symptoms, functional capacity, cardiovascular/respiratory history, diabetes, previous anaesthesia, allergies, smoking, medications and support at home.
  4. Explain individualised assessment, targeted tests and anaesthetic review; avoid promising zero risk.
  5. Discuss material risks: bleeding, infection, anaesthetic complications, VTE and procedure-specific injury; explain risk mitigation.
  6. Explain anticoagulant and diabetes medication planning by the team; do not suggest self-stopping without instructions.
  7. Describe fasting guidance, consent, WHO safety checks, pain control, early mobilisation and recovery.
  8. Check understanding with teach-back, invite questions and provide clear written instructions and safety net.

Examiner priorities: informed shared decision-making, individualised medication plan, appropriate risk discussion, empathy and safety.

Rapid revision — 12 essentials

  1. Assess surgery urgency, comorbidity, frailty, function and medications.
  2. Preoperative tests should be targeted; consult NICE NG45.
  3. Consent includes material risks, alternatives and no treatment.
  4. Anticoagulant interruption and restart are individualised.
  5. SGLT2 inhibitors may cause perioperative euglycaemic ketoacidosis.
  6. Use local fasting instructions; elective clear fluid/solid intervals commonly 2/6 hours.
  7. WHO checklist has Sign In, Time Out and Sign Out.
  8. Assess both VTE and bleeding risk; follow NICE NG89.
  9. Postoperative persistent tachycardia is a warning sign, not a diagnosis.
  10. Unstable postoperative bleeding needs urgent resuscitation and source control.
  11. Post-bowel-surgery sepsis raises concern for anastomotic leak.
  12. Wound evisceration requires sterile moist coverage and urgent surgery.
