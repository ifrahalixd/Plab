ENT & Ophthalmic Emergencies: Epistaxis, Airway Obstruction, Acute Glaucoma & Sudden Vision Loss

Emergency red flags: stridor, drooling, airway swelling, uncontrolled epistaxis, sudden visual loss, chemical eye injury, suspected open globe or painful red eye with reduced vision.

Emergency approach to ENT and eye complaints

Begin with ABCDE, vital signs and immediate escalation for stridor, respiratory compromise, rapidly progressive facial or neck swelling, severe eye pain or sudden vision loss. Take a focused onset, trauma, anticoagulant and systemic illness history.

PLAB application: Do not delay airway support or specialist referral for routine investigations.

Stridor and upper airway obstruction

Inspiratory stridor indicates possible upper airway narrowing. Causes include anaphylaxis, epiglottitis, foreign body, deep neck infection and post-procedural swelling. Look for drooling, inability to swallow, tripod posture, voice change and exhaustion.

PLAB application: Keep a critically obstructed patient calm, summon senior anaesthetic/ENT support and avoid unnecessary throat examination.

Epiglottitis and supraglottitis

Adults may have severe sore throat out of proportion to oropharyngeal findings, dysphagia, muffled voice, drooling or stridor. Children may deteriorate rapidly. Diagnosis and airway management require experienced teams.

PLAB application: Never force an examination of the throat in a child with suspected critical upper airway obstruction.

Anaphylaxis affecting the airway

Rapid airway swelling, wheeze or circulatory compromise after exposure suggests anaphylaxis even without rash. Follow Resuscitation Council UK guidance: intramuscular adrenaline promptly, call for help, position appropriately and reassess.

PLAB application: Antihistamines and corticosteroids do not replace IM adrenaline.

Epistaxis: first aid

Sit the patient upright and lean forward. Pinch the soft cartilaginous part of the nose continuously for 10–15 minutes; ask them to spit out blood. Assess haemodynamic compromise and medications including anticoagulants.

PLAB application: Do not tilt the head backward or pinch the bony nasal bridge.

Persistent and posterior epistaxis

Persistent bleeding requires reassessment, topical treatment, cautery if a source is visible and appropriate, or nasal packing by trained staff. Posterior bleeding may present with blood in the pharynx and cause significant blood loss.

PLAB application: Uncontrolled bleeding, shock or airway compromise warrants urgent ENT and resuscitation input.

Foreign bodies and deep neck infection

Airway foreign bodies may cause choking, unilateral wheeze or stridor. Peritonsillar abscess causes severe unilateral sore throat, trismus and muffled voice. Retropharyngeal infection can cause neck stiffness, drooling and airway compromise.

PLAB application: Do not blindly sweep the mouth or attempt unsafe extraction of an airway foreign body.

Ear emergencies and mastoiditis

Acute mastoiditis can cause fever, postauricular erythema, swelling and displacement of the pinna. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss is an otological emergency, distinct from conductive loss due to wax or middle-ear fluid.

PLAB application: Urgent ENT assessment is needed for mastoiditis and sudden sensorineural hearing loss.

Eye examination in emergencies

Record visual acuity in each eye, pupils and relative afferent pupillary defect, ocular movements, visual fields and external appearance. Ask about contact lenses, trauma, chemical exposure, headache and neurological symptoms.

PLAB application: Document visual acuity before and after interventions when feasible, without delaying emergency irrigation.

Acute angle-closure glaucoma

Typically presents with severe unilateral painful red eye, blurred vision or halos, headache, nausea and vomiting; the cornea may be hazy and pupil mid-dilated. Intraocular pressure is usually elevated.

PLAB application: Arrange immediate ophthalmology treatment to lower pressure and protect vision.

Central retinal artery occlusion

Painless sudden monocular loss of vision can represent retinal arterial ischaemia. Fundoscopy may reveal retinal pallor and a cherry-red spot, but this may not be evident early.

PLAB application: Treat as a time-critical vascular emergency: immediate emergency/stroke and ophthalmology pathways.

Retinal detachment and vitreous haemorrhage

Flashes, new floaters and a shadow or curtain over vision suggest retinal tear or detachment; vitreous haemorrhage may obscure vision. Examine visual acuity and fields and arrange urgent dilated retinal assessment.

PLAB application: A new curtain over vision warrants same-day emergency ophthalmology assessment.

Optic neuritis and giant cell arteritis

Optic neuritis often causes painful eye movement, reduced colour vision and visual loss. Giant cell arteritis in older adults may cause headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication or visual symptoms.

PLAB application: Suspected GCA with visual symptoms requires immediate specialist assessment and glucocorticoids according to local protocol; do not wait for biopsy.

Chemical eye injury

Immediately irrigate the affected eye with copious water or saline, removing contact lenses if possible and checking ocular pH when available. Continue irrigation until pH normalises per protocol. Alkalis can penetrate deeply.

PLAB application: Irrigation starts immediately, before a full history or formal visual acuity test.

Penetrating eye injury and orbital compartment syndrome

Suspected globe rupture after trauma needs a rigid eye shield, no pressure or tonometry, analgesia and urgent ophthalmology. Orbital compartment syndrome causes proptosis, tense orbit, reduced vision and afferent pupillary defect; sight-saving decompression is time critical.

PLAB application: Never apply a pressure patch to a suspected open globe; do not delay emergency decompression for imaging when clinically indicated.

PLAB 2 communication, referral and safety netting

Explain likely diagnosis and uncertainty without false reassurance. Obtain consent, address pain, ask about anticoagulants and driving safety, document visual acuity or airway findings, and hand over using SBAR. Give specific return advice for worsening breathing, bleeding, pain or vision.

PLAB application: Prioritise urgent escalation over completing a lengthy consultation when red flags are present.

PLAB 2 — Emergency ENT and eye consultation

Introduce yourself, check identity and consent, assess immediate stability, take a focused history and perform targeted examination. Communicate urgency calmly, arrange the appropriate specialist pathway, and provide an SBAR handover and clear safety-netting.

Diagnostic traps

Do not label acute painful red eye as conjunctivitis without assessing acuity and corneal features. A normal external eye does not rule out retinal vascular occlusion. Severe sore throat with minimal visible inflammation may still be supraglottitis. Stable observations do not guarantee that a posterior nosebleed is harmless.
