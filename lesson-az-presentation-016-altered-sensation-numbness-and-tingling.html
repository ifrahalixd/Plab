Altered sensation: first localise, then identify the cause

Paraesthesia means an abnormal sensation such as pins and needles; numbness is reduced sensation; dysaesthesia is an unpleasant abnormal sensation; allodynia is pain from a normally non-painful stimulus. The PLAB approach is to determine onset, distribution, associated weakness, reflex findings and systemic context before ordering tests.

Article audio
Emergency red flags: Sudden unilateral numbness (stroke/TIA), new sensory loss with significant weakness, a sensory level with bladder dysfunction (cord compression/myelitis), saddle anaesthesia with urinary symptoms (cauda equina), or rapidly ascending symptoms with weakness/breathing difficulty (Guillain–Barré syndrome). Use emergency pathways; do not wait for routine outpatient investigations.

1. Understand the sensory pathway

Peripheral sensory receptors send information through peripheral nerves and dorsal roots into the spinal cord. Dorsal columns carry vibration, fine touch and joint position; fibres decussate in the medulla. Spinothalamic tracts carry pain and temperature; fibres cross within a few spinal segments. Signals travel to the thalamus and sensory cortex. A neurological examination combines light touch, pinprick, vibration, proprioception and reflexes, with comparison of both sides.

3. History with clinical purpose

  • Onset: seconds/minutes suggests vascular events; hours–days with progression suggests inflammatory or compressive problems; months–years suggests metabolic neuropathy.
  • Distribution: unilateral versus bilateral, distal versus proximal, one nerve versus dermatome versus sensory level.
  • Quality: pins and needles, burning, electric shocks, numbness, painful sensitivity or loss of position sense.
  • Associated symptoms: weakness, diplopia, speech change, ataxia, neck/back pain, bowel/bladder disturbance, autonomic symptoms and respiratory difficulty.
  • Triggers: pressure, posture, repetitive wrist movement, hyperventilation, trauma or exertion.
  • Risks: diabetes, alcohol, B12 deficiency, thyroid disease, renal disease, chemotherapy, HIV and other infections, family history, dietary restrictions and medication exposure.

4. Examination: do not just test sensation

Record observations and a focused cardiovascular/neurological examination as appropriate. Assess gait, Romberg test, tone, power by myotome, deep tendon reflexes (including plantar responses), cranial nerves and cerebellar function. Test pinprick and light touch bilaterally, plus vibration/proprioception at distal joints. Map reproducible sensory findings rather than simply recording 'decreased sensation'. Reduced reflexes support peripheral nerve/root pathology; brisk reflexes with distal sensory disturbance can indicate brain/spine pathology and require referral in line with NICE NG127.

5. Five urgent presentations

Stroke/TIA. Sudden focal hemisensory symptoms may be vascular even without weakness. Establish last-known-well and urgently follow stroke/TIA protocols; check glucose to exclude a mimic.
Cauda equina syndrome. New saddle sensory change, bladder dysfunction, altered sexual function or progressive bilateral leg neurology with back pain requires emergency spinal assessment and MRI pathway. Normal anal tone does not reliably exclude it.
Spinal cord compression or inflammatory myelopathy. Sensory level, new gait disturbance, hyperreflexia or sphincter symptoms warrants urgent imaging and specialist assessment; cancer history increases concern for malignant cord compression.
Guillain–Barré syndrome. Progressive symmetric weakness with reduced reflexes, often following infection, may progress to bulbar, respiratory or autonomic involvement. Urgent hospital referral and monitoring are essential.
Acute limb ischaemia. Painful cold pale limb, reduced pulses and neurological deficit suggests threatened circulation rather than primary neuropathy; activate vascular emergency management.

6. Common non-emergency causes

Diabetic polyneuropathy typically begins symmetrically at the toes and progresses proximally. It can involve small fibres (burning pain and temperature loss) or large fibres (vibration/proprioception loss). Examine feet for ulcers, deformity and footwear risk. Vitamin B12 deficiency can cause sensory ataxia, reduced vibration and, when severe, myelopathy; anaemia may be absent. Carpal tunnel syndrome produces intermittent median-distribution symptoms, particularly at night. Radiculopathy causes dermatomal symptoms often with spinal pain; progressive neurological deficit changes urgency. Meralgia paraesthetica gives numbness/burning over the anterolateral thigh without motor loss.

7. Investigations: guided by localisation

  • Suspected distal peripheral neuropathy: consider glucose/HbA1c, B12, thyroid function, renal function, full blood count, ESR and other tests as indicated by NICE NG127; assess alcohol intake and medicines. No routine blood test replaces examination.
  • Suspected mononeuropathy: clinical provocative/distribution assessment; nerve conduction studies if diagnosis uncertain or surgery considered.
  • Suspected brain or spinal cause: urgent MRI/CT as dictated by syndrome, onset and local pathway, not automatic imaging for every paraesthesia.
  • Suspected demyelination: neurological assessment and MRI when there are objective focal findings, recurrent attacks or other evidence; vague isolated tingling does not establish MS.
  • Suspected inflammatory neuropathy: specialist nerve studies, targeted investigations and sometimes CSF after acute clinical assessment.

8. Management and safety-netting

Correct the cause when possible: optimise diabetes management, treat documented nutritional deficiencies, address offending medication and reduce compression. Sensory foot care and falls prevention can prevent harm. For established neuropathic pain (excluding trigeminal neuralgia and sciatica-specific management), NICE CG173 recommends discussing options such as amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin while considering comorbidity, adverse effects, misuse/dependence risk and review. These are not automatic prescriptions for all numbness. Avoid assuming new neuropathic symptoms are benign when progression, weakness or autonomic signs are present.

9. Five worked PLAB cases

Case 1 — Sudden left hemibody tingling. A 72-year-old develops abrupt left face/arm/leg sensory loss. DDx: stroke/TIA versus migraine aura or metabolic mimic. Action: emergency stroke evaluation, blood glucose and urgent imaging/pathway.
Case 2 — Burning feet. A 58-year-old with diabetes has months of bilateral toe burning and reduced ankle reflexes. DDx: distal symmetric neuropathy. Action: full foot assessment and reversible-cause screen; tailor symptom management.
Case 3 — Night-time hand tingling. A 36-year-old with repetitive wrist work has thumb/index/middle-finger tingling at night, relieved by shaking. DDx: median entrapment. Action: examine strength and distribution; consider night splint and referral based on severity.
Case 4 — Back pain and retention. A 45-year-old reports worsening bilateral sciatica, new saddle numbness and difficulty voiding. DDx: cauda equina. Action: emergency spinal MRI pathway, not routine physiotherapy.
Case 5 — Progressive leg tingling and weakness. After gastroenteritis, a 29-year-old develops ascending weakness and absent knee reflexes. DDx: Guillain–Barré syndrome. Action: urgent hospital referral, respiratory/autonomic monitoring.

10. PLAB 2 structure

Introduce yourself, obtain consent, clarify symptom onset/duration/distribution and red flags, then explain the examination using simple language. Demonstrate light-touch and pinprick assessment, power, reflexes, vibration and gait as indicated. Summarise your localisation hypothesis, explain why additional tests may or may not be needed, and provide specific safety-net advice for sudden symptoms, weakness, gait deterioration or bladder changes.

Exam pearl: “Numbness” is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Localisation and time course narrow the DDx more effectively than broad screening panels. An apparently isolated sensory complaint can still represent stroke or spinal disease.

Deep Dive · sensory neuroanatomy and localisation

Why the sensory distribution matters

1. Pathways and decussation

Fine touch, vibration and proprioception ascend ipsilaterally in posterior columns then cross in the medulla. Pain and temperature fibres enter the dorsal horn, cross near their level of entry and ascend contralaterally in the anterolateral system. Consequently, a hemicord lesion may create differing ipsilateral and contralateral sensory findings.

2. Peripheral nerve versus nerve root

A mononeuropathy follows a peripheral nerve territory, whereas radiculopathy follows a dermatome and often a myotome/reflex pattern. Overlap makes symptoms imperfect maps; repeated examination and objective weakness matter more than a textbook diagram alone.

3. Peripheral fibre types

Large myelinated sensory fibres carry vibration and proprioception and support tendon reflex arcs. Thinly myelinated Aδ and unmyelinated C fibres carry aspects of pain and temperature. Pure small-fibre neuropathy can have marked burning pain with relatively normal conventional nerve conduction studies.

4. Metabolic nerve injury

Chronic hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, microvascular injury and mitochondrial dysfunction contribute to diabetic neuropathy. Vitamin B12 deficiency can damage myelin and produce posterior column dysfunction and sensory ataxia. Underlying causes may coexist, so an established diabetes diagnosis should not prevent investigation of atypical findings.

5. Why reflexes change

Peripheral neuropathy and certain radiculopathies impair the reflex arc and may reduce reflexes. Upper motor neuron lesions may produce hyperreflexia below the lesion. A combination of distal altered sensation and brisk reflexes is therefore a potential CNS localisation and is explicitly addressed by NICE NG127.

6. Red flag mechanisms

Sudden focal CNS ischaemia causes abrupt negative sensory symptoms; spinal compression can interrupt long tracts and autonomic pathways; GBS targets peripheral nerves and roots with progressive weakness and respiratory risk; cauda equina injury damages sacral roots required for perineal sensation and bladder function.

7. Diagnostic pitfalls

  • Do not dismiss isolated sudden numbness as anxiety before a vascular assessment.
  • Do not assume chronic tingling always means diabetic neuropathy.
  • Normal rectal tone does not safely exclude cauda equina syndrome.
  • Normal nerve studies do not fully exclude small-fibre neuropathy.
  • Recurrent vague tingling alone does not establish multiple sclerosis.
