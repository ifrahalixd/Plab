Acute Abdominal Emergencies: Appendicitis, Peritonitis, Bowel Obstruction & Gastrointestinal Bleeding

Emergency rule: Shock, rigid abdomen, persistent GI bleeding, suspected bowel ischaemia or obstructed bowel with peritonism requires immediate resuscitation and senior/surgical escalation.

First approach to the acute abdomen

Use an ABCDE assessment before detailed abdominal examination. Identify shock, sepsis, haemorrhage, peritonism and an obstructed or perforated viscus. Record vital signs, pain onset and progression, vomiting, bowel motions, urinary symptoms, pregnancy possibility, medication use and previous operations.

PLAB application: Do not let a normal initial blood pressure falsely reassure you about intra-abdominal bleeding.

Immediate red flags

Severe constant pain with guarding or rigidity, syncope, haematemesis with shock, bilious vomiting, marked distension, absent urine output or severe pain out of proportion to findings needs urgent senior and surgical input. Consider aortic aneurysm, mesenteric ischaemia, ectopic pregnancy and testicular torsion as important mimics.

PLAB application: Treat the patient first; imaging must not delay resuscitation.

Focused abdominal history

Use SOCRATES for pain, ask about fever, anorexia, diarrhoea, constipation, flatus, melaena, fresh rectal bleeding, jaundice and weight loss. Check anticoagulants, NSAIDs, alcohol, prior abdominal surgery and inflammatory bowel disease. In relevant patients ask sensitively about pregnancy, vaginal bleeding and pelvic pain.

PLAB application: Pain migration and associated symptoms guide the differential, but no single symptom confirms a diagnosis.

Abdominal examination

Assess general appearance and hydration; inspect for scars, distension and hernias; auscultate if useful; gently palpate for focal tenderness, guarding, rebound and masses. Examine groins and external genitalia when indicated, and perform rectal or pelvic examination only with clinical indication, explanation, consent and chaperone.

PLAB application: Document whether peritonism is present and examine hernial orifices.

Investigations and imaging

Request FBC, U&E, CRP, LFTs, lipase, glucose, venous blood gas/lactate when unwell, coagulation and group-and-save/crossmatch if bleeding. Obtain urinalysis and pregnancy test where relevant. Ultrasound, CT or endoscopy depends on presentation and local pathway. Lactate may be normal early in ischaemia.

PLAB application: A negative test cannot override clear signs of an acute surgical abdomen.

Acute appendicitis

Typical appendicitis causes central abdominal pain migrating to the right iliac fossa, anorexia, nausea and focal tenderness, but presentations vary with age, pregnancy and appendix position. Seek urgent surgical assessment and use risk assessment and imaging according to local guidance. Antibiotics and operative or nonoperative decisions are specialist-led.

PLAB application: Perforation can cause diffuse peritonitis and sepsis.

Peritonitis and perforated viscus

Peritoneal inflammation causes severe pain worsened by movement, involuntary guarding or rigidity and systemic illness. Perforation can follow peptic ulcer disease, diverticulitis or other bowel pathology. Resuscitate, keep nil by mouth, obtain IV access, provide analgesia, start appropriate IV antimicrobials when infection suspected and arrange urgent surgical review.

PLAB application: Do not wait for free air on a plain X-ray to escalate suspected perforation.

Small bowel obstruction

Look for colicky abdominal pain, vomiting, distension and failure to pass flatus or stool; early partial obstruction may still permit stool passage. Adhesions and hernias are common causes. Obtain CT where indicated; give IV fluids, correct electrolytes, keep nil by mouth, and consider nasogastric decompression with surgical advice.

PLAB application: Continuous pain, fever, peritonism or acidosis suggests possible strangulation or ischaemia.

Large bowel obstruction and volvulus

Large bowel obstruction may arise from colorectal cancer, volvulus or severe constipation. Distension and obstipation can predominate. CT defines level and complications. Sigmoid volvulus may be treated by endoscopic decompression if stable without perforation, whereas caecal volvulus generally requires surgery.

PLAB application: Peritonitis or suspected bowel ischaemia requires emergency surgical intervention.

Mesenteric ischaemia

Acute mesenteric ischaemia may present with sudden severe pain out of proportion to examination, particularly in atrial fibrillation, vascular disease or low-flow states. Lactate is neither sensitive nor specific early. Seek urgent surgical/vascular input and CT angiography when suspected; resuscitation and treatment should proceed in parallel.

PLAB application: A soft abdomen does not exclude catastrophic bowel ischaemia.

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding

Haematemesis or coffee-ground vomiting and melaena suggest an upper GI source. Assess haemodynamic stability, obtain two large-bore IV lines when significantly unwell, send FBC, clotting and crossmatch, and use blood products according to clinical status and major-haemorrhage guidance. Arrange endoscopy after resuscitation according to urgency.

PLAB application: Suspected variceal bleeding needs urgent specialist care and specific vasoactive/antibiotic protocols.

Lower gastrointestinal bleeding

Fresh rectal bleeding can result from diverticular disease, haemorrhoids, colitis, malignancy or brisk upper GI haemorrhage. Prioritise haemodynamic assessment and resuscitation; consider CT angiography for ongoing major bleeding and endoscopic or interventional management according to local guidance.

PLAB application: Never assume a haemodynamically unstable patient has only haemorrhoids.

Pancreatitis and biliary emergencies

Acute pancreatitis commonly causes severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, vomiting and elevated lipase. Gallstones and alcohol are common causes. Cholecystitis causes right upper quadrant pain and tenderness; ascending cholangitis may cause fever, jaundice and pain with sepsis. Provide fluids as indicated, analgesia, imaging and urgent specialist review for cholangitis.

PLAB application: Charcot triad is not always present; recognise biliary sepsis early.

Special populations and dangerous mimics

In older people, immunosuppressed patients and pregnancy, peritonism or fever may be subtle. Always consider ectopic pregnancy in reproductive-age patients with abdominal pain; check pregnancy status promptly. Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm, DKA, inferior myocardial infarction, renal colic and testicular torsion may mimic abdominal pathology.

PLAB application: Unstable abdominal pain plus possible pregnancy demands immediate ectopic pregnancy assessment.

Analgesia, fluids, antibiotics and reassessment

Give appropriate early analgesia and antiemetics; analgesia does not invalidate examination. Keep patients nil by mouth if surgery or obstruction is likely. Reassess pain, vitals, urine output, fluid response and abdominal signs. Give antibiotics promptly for suspected intra-abdominal sepsis following local protocols, with early source control.

PLAB application: Avoid indiscriminate fluid loading in frail patients or heart failure; reassess frequently.

PLAB 2 communication and SBAR

Explain the possibility of a surgical cause without prematurely declaring a diagnosis. Seek consent for examination and tests, address pain, tell the patient why fasting or urgent review is needed, and communicate changes with SBAR. Document escalation, differential diagnosis, investigations, treatments and repeat examinations.

PLAB application: State clearly when a patient must not be discharged and give explicit return precautions when safe for discharge.

PLAB 2 station: acute abdominal pain

Introduce yourself, confirm identity, seek consent, assess ABCDE and analgesia needs, take a focused history, perform a respectful abdominal examination and explain urgent tests and escalation. If unstable, stop routine questioning and call for immediate help.

Deep Dive — Acute abdominal emergencies

Appendicitis is not always textbook

Children, older adults and pregnant patients can have atypical tenderness and limited fever. Risk scores support but do not replace clinical judgement; consider ultrasound or CT according to patient group and local pathway.

Bowel obstruction: strangulation clues

Adhesions, hernias and malignancy are common. Persistent rather than colicky pain, tachycardia, fever, guarding, metabolic acidosis and rising lactate increase concern for compromised bowel. Normal lactate does not exclude early ischaemia.

GI bleeding resuscitation and risk

Haemodynamic instability, syncope, ongoing haematemesis, anticoagulant use and comorbid illness raise urgency. Use a validated upper GI bleeding risk tool where appropriate, but resuscitation and early specialist input come first.

Peritonitis and source control

A rigid abdomen is a clinical emergency. Start ABCDE, fluids or blood products according to cause, early appropriate antibiotics for infection and urgent source-control discussion. Avoid unnecessary delays for low-yield tests.

Mesenteric ischaemia and vascular catastrophes

Pain out of proportion to the examination, atrial fibrillation or vascular risk factors warrant urgent CT angiography and surgical/vascular advice. In older adults with back or abdominal pain and collapse, consider ruptured AAA.

Safety and human factors

Use closed-loop communication, SBAR, serial observations, urine output and documented repeat examinations. Consider pregnancy, safeguarding and consent. Escalate whenever clinical deterioration occurs despite initial treatment.
