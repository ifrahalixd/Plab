Testicular Torsion, Scrotal Swellings & Male Genital Emergencies

Surgery · Lesson 32 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Acute scrotum and male genital emergencies — PLAB clinical approach

Time-critical: sudden severe unilateral testicular pain is torsion until proven otherwise. Call urology immediately; do not delay surgical exploration for Doppler ultrasound when suspicion is high. A normal cremasteric reflex or Doppler result does not reliably exclude torsion.

1 · Assessment of acute scrotal pain

Ask exact onset, duration, previous self-resolving attacks, trauma, fever, urinary symptoms, sexual history and nausea/vomiting. Check observations for sepsis, inspect and palpate abdomen, groins and scrotum with consent and chaperone. Assess testicular position, tenderness, swelling, erythema and inguinal hernia. Offer prompt analgesia, keep nil by mouth if surgery possible and urgently discuss suspected torsion with urology.

2 · Testicular torsion

Twisting of spermatic cord obstructs venous then arterial blood flow, risking infarction. Typical findings: abrupt severe unilateral pain, nausea/vomiting, high-riding or horizontal testis, absent cremasteric reflex. These signs are not universally present; torsion can occur in neonates and adults. Urgent scrotal exploration and detorsion with bilateral orchidopexy if viable; orchidectomy if nonviable. Salvage probability decreases rapidly with time, often quoted highest within 4–6 hours, but late presentation still needs urgent assessment. Ultrasound is useful only when it does not delay definitive management.

3 · Intermittent torsion

Recurrent sudden pain resolving spontaneously may represent torsion–detorsion; examination and Doppler may be normal between attacks. Arrange urgent specialist assessment because recurrent torsion may cause testicular loss. Avoid dismissing as benign based on symptom resolution.

4 · Epididymitis and epididymo-orchitis

Usually gradual unilateral pain and swelling, sometimes dysuria, urethral discharge or fever. In sexually active patients assess for chlamydia/gonorrhoea; in older men or urinary abnormalities consider enteric organisms. Perform urinalysis and urine culture when indicated, NAAT for STIs and offer partner notification/testing as appropriate. Treat using current UKHSA/BASHH/local antimicrobial guidance, analgesia and scrotal support. Do not use Prehn sign as a reliable torsion discriminator. If uncertain, prioritise torsion exclusion.

5 · Torsion of appendix testis

Often prepubertal, with focal superior-pole tenderness and sometimes a blue-dot sign. If torsion of the testis cannot be confidently excluded, urgent surgical review remains necessary. Otherwise supportive analgesia and observation may suffice.

6 · Hydrocele

Typically painless fluid swelling surrounding testis that transilluminates; ultrasound when diagnosis uncertain or testis cannot be palpated. New or atypical hydrocele should not conceal testicular tumour. Infant communicating hydroceles may resolve; persistent/symptomatic adult hydroceles may need urology review and surgery.

7 · Varicocele

Bag-of-worms dilated pampiniform plexus, usually left-sided, more evident standing/Valsalva and reducing supine. May be associated with discomfort or infertility. A sudden-onset, isolated right-sided or non-reducing varicocele warrants consideration of secondary causes and specialist assessment; imaging based on clinical context. Management includes reassurance or repair/embolisation for selected pain or fertility indications.

8 · Epididymal cyst/spermatocele

Usually painless, smooth extratesticular cystic swelling; distinguish from a solid intratesticular mass. Ultrasound if uncertain. Reassurance unless troublesome; specialist discussion for symptomatic lesions.

9 · Testicular cancer and scrotal masses

A painless firm intratesticular lump or change in shape/texture needs urgent ultrasound and suspected cancer referral per NICE NG12. Tumour markers AFP, beta-hCG and LDH are specialist investigations; normal markers do not exclude cancer. Avoid trans-scrotal biopsy. A hydrocele does not exclude malignancy.

10 · Incarcerated/strangulated inguinoscrotal hernia

Groin/scrotal swelling with severe pain, vomiting, obstruction or systemic illness suggests incarcerated or strangulated hernia. Urgent surgical review, resuscitation and imaging only if appropriate without delay; avoid forceful reduction when strangulation suspected.

11 · Fournier gangrene

Necrotising infection of perineum/genitals: severe pain disproportionate to findings, rapid swelling/erythema, crepitus, skin discolouration or shock. Diabetes and immunosuppression are risk factors but not required. Emergency sepsis management, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and immediate surgical debridement; do not delay for imaging if clinically clear.

12 · Priapism

Persistent erection, especially painful rigid erection >4 hours, is an emergency. Ischaemic (low-flow) priapism risks tissue necrosis; causes include sickle cell disease, medication and haematological disorders. Urgent urology assessment, corporal blood gas and aspiration/irrigation with intracavernosal phenylephrine by trained clinicians as indicated. Non-ischaemic priapism is usually less painful and managed differently.

13 · Paraphimosis and phimosis

Paraphimosis: retracted foreskin trapped behind glans causing painful swelling and vascular compromise; urgent reduction with analgesia and oedema reduction, escalate for dorsal slit if unsuccessful. Phimosis: foreskin cannot retract; avoid forced retraction. Treat balanitis or inflammatory disease and consider topical steroids or circumcision where indicated.

14 · Penile fracture and trauma

Penile fracture classically presents with a snap, sudden detumescence, swelling and bruising after trauma to erect penis; emergency urological surgical repair is generally indicated. Blood at urethral meatus, haematuria or voiding difficulty suggests urethral injury; do not blindly catheterise if injury suspected, seek urgent urological advice.

15 · Clinical safety-net

Escalate sudden testicular pain, rapidly progressive genital infection, persistent painful erection, irreducible painful foreskin, penile fracture, or new hard testicular mass. Reassess if symptoms persist despite presumed infection treatment.

Clinical algorithms and revision frameworks

Sudden painful testis

ABCDE and analgesia → examine with consent/chaperone → torsion suspected? → immediate urology and urgent exploration; do not wait for Doppler → bilateral fixation if viable / orchidectomy if nonviable.

Gradual scrotal pain with urinary/STI symptoms

Exclude torsion first → urine dip/culture + STI NAAT when appropriate → antibiotics guided by UK/local guidance → analgesia/support → review response and safety-net.

Painless scrotal swelling

Determine intratesticular versus extratesticular → transillumination, position, cough impulse and reducibility → ultrasound if uncertain → urgent cancer pathway for suspicious solid intratesticular mass.

Scrotal/perineal pain + systemic toxicity

Suspect Fournier gangrene → sepsis resuscitation, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics → emergency surgical debridement and critical-care input.

Painful erection lasting >4 hours

Urgent urology → distinguish ischaemic from non-ischaemic (history and blood gas) → immediate ischaemic priapism treatment to prevent erectile dysfunction.

Penile/foreskin emergency

Paraphimosis → urgent reduction. Penile fracture → urgent surgical assessment. Possible urethral injury → avoid blind urethral instrumentation.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Sudden pain at football practice

A 16-year-old has abrupt left testicular pain, vomiting and a high-riding testis for 2 hours. Action: suspect torsion; immediate urology contact, analgesia, nil by mouth and urgent exploration without delaying for ultrasound.

Case 2 — Gradual pain and dysuria

A 24-year-old has 3 days of unilateral swelling, dysuria and urethral discharge. Action: consider epididymo-orchitis, but assess for torsion; send STI NAAT and relevant urine testing, treat per current local STI guidance and arrange partner management.

Case 3 — Painless enlarging swelling

A 29-year-old has a firm, non-tender intratesticular lump; no fever. Action: urgent scrotal ultrasound and suspected testicular cancer referral; discuss fertility considerations if diagnosis confirmed.

Case 4 — Severe perineal pain and shock

A 58-year-old with diabetes has rapidly spreading genital swelling, skin discoloration and hypotension. Action: Fournier gangrene: immediate sepsis resuscitation, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics and emergency surgical debridement.

PLAB 2 OSCE — acute scrotal pain

Station: A 19-year-old presents with severe sudden right testicular pain for 3 hours. Explain your assessment and management.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, gain consent and offer privacy and chaperone.
  2. Ask onset, severity, radiation, nausea/vomiting, prior intermittent episodes, trauma, urinary and sexual symptoms.
  3. Check observations, offer prompt analgesia and examine abdomen, groins and scrotum with consent.
  4. Recognise torsion as time-critical even without every classic sign; explain that blood supply may be compromised.
  5. Call senior/urology immediately, keep nil by mouth, arrange IV access as appropriate and urgent surgical exploration.
  6. Explain Doppler may help when diagnosis uncertain but must not delay operation if suspicion high.
  7. Explain surgery aims to untwist and preserve the testis, often fixing both testes; removal may be required if nonviable.
  8. Address fertility and hormonal concerns sensitively, check understanding and provide reassurance without guaranteeing salvage.

Examiner focus: urgency, no diagnostic imaging delay, sensitive genital examination, communication and escalation.

Rapid review — 12 exam essentials

  1. Acute severe unilateral testicular pain is torsion until proven otherwise.
  2. Do not delay urgent surgical exploration for ultrasound when torsion suspicion is high.
  3. Torsion may have nausea, vomiting, high-riding testis and absent cremasteric reflex; none is mandatory.
  4. Late presentation still requires urgent assessment.
  5. Intermittent pain resolving spontaneously can be torsion–detorsion.
  6. Epididymo-orchitis is often gradual and may have urinary or STI symptoms; exclude torsion.
  7. Hydroceles transilluminate but may conceal a tumour.
  8. Varicocele is classically a left-sided bag-of-worms swelling.
  9. Firm intratesticular lump requires urgent ultrasound and cancer referral.
  10. Fournier gangrene requires immediate antibiotics and surgical debridement.
  11. Ischaemic priapism is an emergency, particularly after four hours.
  12. Paraphimosis needs urgent reduction; penile fracture needs urgent surgical assessment.
