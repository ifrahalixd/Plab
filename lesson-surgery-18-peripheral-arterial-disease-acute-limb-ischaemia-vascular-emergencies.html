Peripheral Arterial Disease, Acute Limb Ischaemia & Vascular Emergencies

Surgery · Lesson 18 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Peripheral vascular surgery: recognise the threatened limb

Emergency: sudden painful, pale, cold, pulseless limb with sensory or motor deficit = acute limb ischaemia (ALI). Immediate vascular referral, ABCDE, analgesia, IV access and urgent assessment. Unless contraindicated, systemic IV unfractionated heparin is typically initiated on specialist advice; do not delay revascularisation for routine tests.

1 · Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)

Atherosclerotic narrowing of limb arteries; risks: smoking, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, age and CKD. Intermittent claudication: reproducible exertional calf/thigh/buttock pain relieved by rest. Examine skin, ulcers, temperature, capillary refill, femoral/popliteal/foot pulses and bruits; check for neuropathy and other causes of leg pain.

2 · Ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI)

ABPI = highest ankle systolic pressure (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial) / highest brachial systolic pressure. Values below 0.9 support PAD; 0.9–1.4 is often considered normal/borderline depending on context; above 1.4 suggests incompressible calcified vessels, common in diabetes/CKD, and may be falsely reassuring. Use toe pressures or vascular laboratory assessment if ABPI unreliable. A normal resting ABPI does not always exclude exertional disease.

3 · Management of stable PAD

Offer smoking cessation, supervised exercise, cardiovascular risk reduction (lipid lowering, BP/diabetes control) and antiplatelet therapy when indicated according to NICE and specialist assessment. Treat foot problems, provide appropriate footwear and wound care. If lifestyle-limiting claudication persists despite optimal therapy, consider imaging and revascularisation (angioplasty/stenting or bypass) with vascular MDT. Avoid assuming all patients need immediate surgery.

4 · Chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI)

Persistent ischaemic rest pain, non-healing ulcer or gangrene with objective arterial disease. Often nocturnal forefoot pain relieved by dangling the leg; look for tissue loss. Requires urgent vascular assessment and limb-salvage planning. Infection, diabetes and renal disease increase amputation risk.

5 · Acute limb ischaemia: six Ps

Pain, pallor, pulselessness, poikilothermia (cold), paraesthesia, paralysis. Not all are present; sensory loss and especially weakness signal threatened viability. Causes: embolus (atrial fibrillation, cardiac thrombus), in-situ thrombosis on PAD, graft/stent occlusion, trauma and dissection. Assess time of onset, bilateral pulses, Doppler signals, sensory and motor function; classify viability with vascular team (Rutherford).

6 · ALI investigations and treatment

Bloods: FBC, U&E, coagulation, glucose, CK, lactate/VBG, group and save; ECG for AF; assess renal function and contrast risk. Duplex or CT angiography helps define anatomy if it does not delay care. Give analgesia, NBM, IV access and fluid support as appropriate; urgent vascular surgeon and anaesthetic input. Specialist-directed heparin unless contraindicated; definitive options include catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, embolectomy, angioplasty or bypass. Monitor for reperfusion injury, hyperkalaemia, rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome; fasciotomy may be needed.

7 · Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

AAA may be asymptomatic or cause abdominal/back pain. Rupture may present with collapse, hypotension and abdominal/back pain; the classic triad is often incomplete. Suspected rupture: activate emergency vascular pathway immediately, obtain IV access, group/crossmatch and blood products as needed; use permissive hypotension only in an appropriate specialist-led protocol. Bedside ultrasound can detect an aneurysm but does not exclude rupture; CT angiography in stable enough patients if it does not delay surgery. Repair may be open or endovascular.

8 · Aortic dissection and other vascular emergencies

Sudden severe chest/back pain, pulse/BP asymmetry, neurological deficits or shock suggest dissection. Emergency CTA and cardiothoracic/vascular referral; proximal (Stanford A) dissection usually needs emergency surgery, while uncomplicated distal (B) is commonly managed initially with specialist BP/HR control. Do not reflexively anticoagulate suspected dissection. Also recognise ruptured peripheral aneurysm, infected vascular graft, acute compartment syndrome and vascular trauma.

9 · Venous disease: distinguishing DVT

DVT often causes swollen, warm, tender limb with preserved arterial perfusion, unlike cold pulseless ALI. Massive phlegmasia can threaten limb viability and needs emergency specialist assessment. Follow NICE VTE pathways for suspected DVT; never let a DVT work-up delay treatment of suspected ALI.

Clinical decision frameworks

Sudden cold painful leg

ABCDE → immediate vascular call → document onset, six Ps, motor/sensory function and Doppler signals → analgesia/IV access, bloods and ECG → IV heparin if appropriate on specialist advice → urgent revascularisation plan. Motor deficit means minutes matter.

Exertional calf pain

Vascular history and pulse examination → ABPI (toe pressure if non-compressible) → smoking cessation, supervised exercise and cardiovascular prevention → consider vascular imaging/intervention if persistent disabling symptoms.

Rest pain, ulcer or gangrene

Suspect CLTI → assess infection and perfusion → urgent vascular and multidisciplinary foot review → limb salvage with revascularisation and wound care.

Collapse with abdominal/back pain

Suspect ruptured AAA → resuscitation and immediate vascular activation → rapid bedside assessment, blood products and imaging only if stable enough → emergency repair.

Sudden tearing chest/back pain

Consider aortic dissection → urgent specialist assessment and CTA → type A: emergency cardiothoracic surgery; type B: specialist medical/endovascular approach as indicated.

Four applied clinical cases

Case 1 — Embolic acute limb ischaemia

A 76-year-old with atrial fibrillation has abrupt severe left leg pain, a cold pale foot, absent Doppler signals and new toe weakness. Action: emergency vascular referral and immediate limb viability assessment; initiate resuscitation and anticoagulation when appropriate without delaying revascularisation.

Case 2 — Stable claudication

A 61-year-old smoker gets calf pain after walking 150 metres that resolves in minutes. ABPI 0.68. Action: PAD management with smoking cessation, supervised exercise and cardiovascular risk reduction; review need for specialist intervention if refractory.

Case 3 — Threatened diabetic foot

A 70-year-old with diabetes has forefoot rest pain, a non-healing toe ulcer and weak pulses. Action: urgent vascular and multidisciplinary diabetic-foot assessment for CLTI and infection; plan limb salvage.

Case 4 — Ruptured AAA

A 73-year-old collapses with sudden back pain, pallor and BP 78/45. Action: suspect ruptured AAA, activate emergency vascular team, obtain blood products and prepare for definitive repair. Do not wait for the full classic triad.

PLAB 2 OSCE — Sudden painful cold leg

Station: A 74-year-old with atrial fibrillation develops sudden severe leg pain and numbness in the emergency department.

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, assess immediate distress and call for senior help.
  2. Establish exact onset, progression, pain, numbness, weakness, previous claudication and anticoagulant use.
  3. ABCDE; inspect both legs and check colour, temperature, capillary refill and all pulses.
  4. Assess sensation, toe/ankle movement and handheld Doppler signals; document side and findings.
  5. Explain clearly: “The blood supply to your leg may be suddenly blocked. This is an emergency because the tissues can be damaged.”
  6. Initiate analgesia, IV access, blood tests and ECG; keep NBM and seek immediate vascular review.
  7. Discuss urgent blood-thinning treatment if appropriate and possible operation or catheter procedure to restore blood flow.
  8. Explain risks of permanent nerve/muscle injury, reperfusion complications and possible amputation if tissue cannot be saved.
  9. Use empathic, concise language, check understanding and document escalation and timing.

Examiner focus: recognising a threatened limb, checking motor and sensory function, immediate escalation and avoiding imaging-related delays.

Rapid revision: 12 essentials

  1. PAD typically causes reproducible exertional pain relieved by rest.
  2. ABPI below 0.9 supports PAD; high ABPI may be falsely reassuring in calcified vessels.
  3. Stable claudication: supervised exercise, smoking cessation and vascular risk reduction.
  4. Rest pain, ulcer or gangrene suggests chronic limb-threatening ischaemia.
  5. ALI is recognised by the six Ps; weakness is a late and serious sign.
  6. AF is an important source of arterial emboli.
  7. Suspected ALI requires immediate vascular referral; do not wait for routine imaging.
  8. IV unfractionated heparin is commonly used if appropriate and not contraindicated.
  9. ALI treatment may involve embolectomy, thrombolysis or bypass.
  10. Reperfusion can cause hyperkalaemia, rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome.
  11. Ruptured AAA may cause shock and back/abdominal pain without a palpable mass.
  12. Stanford type A dissection is generally a surgical emergency.
