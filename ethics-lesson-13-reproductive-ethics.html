Reproductive Ethics & Medical Law

PLAB scope: UK-wide ethical principles; legislation differs by nation. This is exam-focused educational material, not a substitute for current local clinical protocols or legal advice.

Learning objectives

  • Apply valid consent and confidentiality to contraception, abortion and fertility care.
  • Assess adolescent competence and safeguarding.
  • Distinguish abortion law in Great Britain from Northern Ireland.
  • Explain ethical issues in sterilisation and assisted reproduction.
  • Manage conscientious objection without disadvantaging patients.

1. Consent, autonomy and confidentiality

Offer unbiased information on benefits, material risks, reasonable alternatives and doing nothing. Obtain voluntary, decision-specific consent; patients may withdraw it. Do not require a partner’s permission for a capable patient’s reproductive decisions. Use private conversations to detect coercion.

2. Contraception and young people

Under-16s may consent when Gillick competent. Fraser guidelines specifically inform contraceptive advice. Assess understanding, safety, exploitation, pressure and relevant STI/pregnancy risks. Encourage supportive adult involvement when appropriate, but do not automatically disclose to parents.

3. Abortion

In England, Wales and Scotland, the Abortion Act 1967 (as amended) sets statutory grounds and certification requirements; two doctors’ opinions are generally required, with emergency exceptions. Legal grounds and gestational rules vary by circumstance. Northern Ireland has its own framework under the Abortion (Northern Ireland) Regulations 2020, as amended. Confirm current local rules and clinical pathways.

Doctors with conscientious objections must be open about this and ensure patients can access appropriate care without delay; they must not impose their beliefs or abandon emergency responsibilities.

4. Sterilisation and assisted reproduction

Discuss irreversibility, potential regret, failure, alternatives, risks and the patient’s own preferences. Fertility treatment, donor gametes and embryo storage are regulated through the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act framework and HFEA requirements, including specific consent rules.

5. High-yield PLAB traps

  • Partner consent is not a prerequisite to contraception or abortion.
  • Being under 16 does not automatically mean inability to consent.
  • Confidentiality is not absolute where serious safeguarding risks exist.
  • A doctor’s conscientious objection must not obstruct timely access to care.
  • Do not apply Great Britain abortion rules automatically to Northern Ireland.

Clinical decision pathways

A · Young person requesting contraception

  1. See patient privately; establish reason for attendance and immediate needs.
  2. Assess understanding and Gillick competence; consider Fraser criteria.
  3. Screen for exploitation, coercion, abuse, pregnancy and STI risk.
  4. Explain suitable methods, risks, alternatives and confidentiality limits.
  5. Agree plan, document reasoning, safety-net and follow-up.

B · Abortion request

  1. Confirm patient’s wishes, pregnancy details and clinical urgency.
  2. Assess capacity, voluntariness, safety and safeguarding.
  3. Explain balanced options and applicable national legal pathway.
  4. Arrange timely referral and care, including where clinician objects.
  5. Document discussion, consent, advice and follow-up.

C · Fertility and sterilisation

  1. Explore goals and values without assumptions.
  2. Explain options, success/failure, risks and reversibility.
  3. Check decision-specific consent and any HFEA requirements.
  4. Refer to appropriate specialist and document.

Four PLAB clinical cases

Confidential contraception at 15

A 15-year-old requests contraception and fears parental disclosure. Recommended approach: Assess Gillick competence, apply Fraser guidance for contraceptive care, encourage trusted adult support and assess coercion/exploitation. Keep information confidential unless justified safeguarding disclosure is necessary.

Abortion and partner disagreement

A capable 27-year-old wants an abortion; her partner demands a veto. Recommended approach: Speak with patient privately, assess coercion, confirm her own wishes, provide balanced information and lawful pathways. Partner agreement is not required.

Request for sterilisation

A 30-year-old requests permanent sterilisation after two children. Recommended approach: Avoid discriminatory assumptions about age or marital status. Discuss permanence, alternatives, procedural risks and failure; establish voluntary informed consent and arrange appropriate referral.

IVF and embryo consent

A couple asks about creating and storing embryos before cancer treatment. Recommended approach: Discuss urgency, fertility preservation options and refer promptly to specialist services; explain that HFEA-regulated consent and storage requirements apply.

PLAB 2 OSCE · Confidential contraception consultation

Station: A 15-year-old requests contraception and asks that her parents not be informed.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, ensure privacy, establish rapport.
  2. Explore reason for request and concerns about confidentiality.
  3. Explain confidentiality and its safeguarding limits in clear language.
  4. Assess understanding, competence and voluntariness.
  5. Explore partner age, pressure, exploitation and safety sensitively.
  6. Discuss contraceptive options, emergency contraception where relevant, STI prevention and testing.
  7. Encourage a trusted adult if appropriate without coercion.
  8. Agree next steps, safety-net, document and seek safeguarding support if indicated.

Example wording: “I usually keep what you tell me private. If I become worried that you or someone else is at serious risk of harm, I may need to share limited information to keep you safe. I would try to explain that to you first.”

Examiner pitfalls

Do not automatically contact parents; do not promise absolute secrecy; do not skip safeguarding or pressure the patient into a method.
