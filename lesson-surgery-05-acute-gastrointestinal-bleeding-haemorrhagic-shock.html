Acute Gastrointestinal Bleeding & Haemorrhagic Shock

Surgery · Lesson 05 · UK PLAB 1 & PLAB 2

Acute GI bleeding: recognise, resuscitate, localise

PLAB priority: Haematemesis, melaena or major haematochezia with shock requires ABCDE resuscitation, urgent senior input, blood products when indicated and timely definitive haemostasis. Do not wait for haemoglobin to fall: early Hb may be misleading.

1 · Upper versus lower GI bleeding

PresentationLikely source / caveat
Fresh blood vomited (haematemesis)Upper GI: peptic ulcer, varices, erosions, Mallory–Weiss tear
Coffee-ground vomitUpper GI bleeding, possibly slower or ceased
Black tarry stool (melaena)Usually upper GI; sometimes small bowel/right colon
Bright red rectal bleeding (haematochezia)Usually lower GI; brisk upper GI bleed can also cause it

2 · Immediate assessment and resuscitation

  1. ABCDE; check BP, pulse, mental status, perfusion, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, NEWS2; look for syncope, ongoing bleeding and signs of shock.
  2. Call senior emergency/gastroenterology/surgical help early; anaesthetics/critical care if airway risk or unstable.
  3. Two large-bore IV cannulas if possible, FBC, U&Es, LFTs, coagulation, venous blood gas/lactate, group and save/crossmatch. Repeat Hb and observations; monitor urine output in severe cases.
  4. Give judicious IV crystalloid while preparing blood; activate major haemorrhage protocol for life-threatening bleeding and give blood components according to clinical condition and local transfusion protocol.
  5. Review anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, liver disease, previous ulcers/varices, alcohol and comorbidities; reversal decisions depend on drug and severity, with specialist input.

3 · Upper GI bleed management (NICE CG141)

Use Glasgow–Blatchford score at first assessment to assess risk, and full Rockall score after endoscopy. Patients with unstable severe upper GI bleeding should undergo endoscopy immediately after resuscitation; others admitted with upper GI bleeding generally require endoscopy within 24 hours. Do not give a proton pump inhibitor before endoscopy for suspected non-variceal upper GI bleeding; after endoscopic evidence of recent haemorrhage from a non-variceal source, give PPI according to protocol. Endoscopic haemostasis may use clips, thermal therapy and/or injection in combination: adrenaline injection alone is not adequate.

4 · Suspected variceal haemorrhage

Suspect in cirrhosis/portal hypertension. Start terlipressin and prophylactic antibiotics at presentation as per NICE CG141, alongside resuscitation and urgent endoscopy. Endoscopic band ligation is preferred for oesophageal varices; specialist rescue options include tamponade as a bridge and TIPS in selected uncontrolled cases.

5 · Lower GI bleeding

Common causes include diverticular bleeding, angiodysplasia, haemorrhoids, colorectal malignancy and colitis. Stable patients need risk assessment and investigation. In haemodynamically unstable lower GI bleeding, CT angiography can rapidly identify active bleeding and guide interventional radiology; if CTA does not identify a source, consider urgent upper GI endoscopy because a brisk upper bleed may present as rectal blood.

6 · Haemorrhagic shock

Tachycardia, hypotension, cool peripheries, oliguria and altered consciousness indicate poor perfusion. Elderly patients or those taking beta blockers may not mount tachycardia. Treat the cause and restore circulating volume; escalate for endoscopic, radiological or surgical haemostasis. Avoid falsely reassuring single normal haemoglobin values.

Exam traps: Do not delay resuscitation for scoring or endoscopy; unstable patients need endoscopy after resuscitation. Suspected varices need antibiotics and vasoactive therapy. Do not assume fresh rectal blood always means a lower source.

Clinical pathways & revision slides

Pathway A — Any major GI bleed

Haematemesis/melaena/haematochezia → ABCDE + shock assessment → senior help + IV access + crossmatch + resuscitation → identify likely source → urgent endoscopic/IR/surgical haemostasis and monitoring.

Pathway B — Upper GI bleed

Resuscitate → Glasgow–Blatchford risk score → unstable severe bleed: endoscopy immediately after resuscitation; other admitted upper GI bleeds: endoscopy within 24 hours → treat lesion and reassess.

Pathway C — Variceal bleeding

Cirrhosis + haematemesis → ABCDE + blood products as indicated → terlipressin + prophylactic antibiotics → urgent endoscopy and band ligation → consider rescue TIPS if uncontrolled.

Slide 1 — Three bleeding clues

Haematemesis = upper; melaena = usually upper; haematochezia = usually lower but can be brisk upper.

Slide 2 — Two risk scores

Glasgow–Blatchford at initial assessment; full Rockall after endoscopy.

Slide 3 — Time-critical decisions

Resuscitate first; obtain haemostasis promptly; investigate ongoing instability and rebleeding.

Four clinical cases

Case 1 — Bleeding peptic ulcer

A 64-year-old on ibuprofen presents with melaena, dizziness and BP 90/55. Approach: ABCDE, IV access, group and crossmatch, resuscitation, urgent gastroenterology review and endoscopy after stabilisation; endoscopic therapy for high-risk ulcer.

Case 2 — Oesophageal varices

A 52-year-old with cirrhosis vomits a large volume of fresh blood. Approach: airway/shock assessment, senior and anaesthetic help, blood products as indicated, terlipressin and antibiotics, urgent endoscopic band ligation.

Case 3 — Major lower GI bleed

A 77-year-old has painless large-volume red rectal bleeding, tachycardia and hypotension. Approach: major bleed resuscitation and senior review; CTA when indicated to localise bleeding, with interventional radiology or endoscopy for haemostasis.

Case 4 — Anticoagulant-associated bleed

A 71-year-old taking apixaban presents with melaena and syncope. Approach: ABCDE, resuscitation, assess last dose/renal function, urgent specialist advice about withholding/reversal and endoscopic control; balance thrombosis risk.

PLAB 2 — Eight-minute haematemesis station

Brief: You are an FY2 in ED. A 59-year-old reports vomiting blood and dizziness. Take a focused history, recognise danger signs and explain immediate management.

Checklist

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity, seek consent; identify instability immediately.
  2. ABCDE, observations, NEWS2 and early senior escalation.
  3. Ask about volume/colour of blood, melaena, rectal bleeding, syncope and abdominal pain.
  4. Ask about ulcer disease, NSAIDs, anticoagulants, alcohol, cirrhosis and prior bleeding.
  5. Assess hydration, perfusion, abdomen and stigmata of chronic liver disease as appropriate.
  6. Explain IV cannulas, bloods, crossmatch, fluids/blood products and nil by mouth.
  7. Explain risk scoring, urgent endoscopy after resuscitation and likely treatments.
  8. Safety-net, reassess, document and SBAR handover.

Patient explanation: “Bleeding in your digestive tract can sometimes become serious. We need to monitor and support your circulation, find the source and arrange a camera test to stop the bleeding if needed.”

Rapid revision & references

  1. Resuscitation and haemostasis come before routine investigation.
  2. Early haemoglobin may not reflect the full extent of acute blood loss.
  3. Glasgow–Blatchford before endoscopy; full Rockall after.
  4. Unstable severe upper GI bleed: endoscopy immediately after resuscitation; others admitted: within 24 hours.
  5. Suspected varices: terlipressin + antibiotics; band ligation for oesophageal varices.
  6. Do not use adrenaline injection alone for non-variceal endoscopic haemostasis.
  7. Unstable haematochezia may be brisk upper GI bleeding.
