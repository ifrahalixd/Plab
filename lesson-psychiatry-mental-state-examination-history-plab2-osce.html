Mental State Examination, Psychiatric History & PLAB 2 OSCE

PLAB priority: Structured psychiatric history, objective MSE, personalised safety assessment, consent and compassionate communication.

01. Consultation opening

Introduce yourself, confirm patient identity, explain the purpose and obtain consent. Ask how the patient wishes to be addressed; offer an interpreter and consider a chaperone where appropriate. Ensure privacy and a calm environment.

PLAB application: Open with an invitation such as “What has been troubling you recently?”

02. Presenting complaint and history

Explore onset, duration, progression, triggers, associated symptoms, impact on functioning, previous episodes and treatments. Establish the patient’s own explanation, concerns and expectations.

PLAB application: Use open questions first, then targeted symptom enquiry.

03. Mood and biological symptoms

Ask about low mood, anhedonia, irritability, energy, sleep, appetite, weight, concentration, guilt and psychomotor change. Ask about duration and daily variation.

PLAB application: Screen for depressive symptoms and assess impairment and safety.

04. Mania and hypomania screen

Ask about episodes of elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, grandiosity, racing thoughts, spending and risky behaviour. Ask about consequences and duration.

PLAB application: Screen for bipolarity before considering antidepressant treatment.

05. Psychosis enquiry

Ask sensitively about voices, visions, unusual beliefs, thought interference and paranoia. Clarify conviction, distress, impact and whether voices give commands. Do not affirm a delusion.

PLAB application: Acknowledge distress: “That sounds frightening.”

06. Anxiety, trauma and compulsions

Ask about persistent worry, panic attacks, avoidance, intrusive thoughts, rituals, traumatic experiences and re-experiencing. Seek permission before sensitive questions; do not require detailed trauma narratives.

PLAB application: Distinguish obsessions from delusions and explore function.

07. Suicide and self-harm assessment

Ask directly about thoughts of death, self-harm, suicidal thoughts, intent, plans, access to means, recent actions, protective factors and immediate safety. Consider dynamic circumstances and previous episodes.

PLAB application: Do not use a risk score to decide discharge; make a collaborative safety plan.

08. Past psychiatric and medical history

Record diagnoses, admissions, previous self-harm, psychological treatments, medication response, side effects and adherence. Consider physical illness, delirium, epilepsy, endocrine disease and neurological causes.

PLAB application: Exclude relevant medical and substance-induced causes.

09. Medication, substances and allergies

Ask about prescribed and non-prescribed drugs, alcohol, cannabis, stimulants and other substances; include withdrawal, dependence and recent changes. Check allergies and adverse drug reactions.

PLAB application: Ask about steroids and other medicines that can alter mood or behaviour.

10. Personal, developmental and social history

Explore upbringing, education, work, relationships, housing, finances, culture, spirituality if relevant, supports, trauma and safeguarding. Ask about children or dependent adults and caring responsibilities.

PLAB application: Assess functional impact and protective supports without assumptions.

11. Family history and collateral information

Ask about familial mood disorders, psychosis, substance problems and suicide. With consent, seek collateral history when it clarifies change from baseline; in emergencies follow confidentiality and safeguarding duties.

PLAB application: Collateral history is especially helpful in mania, psychosis and cognitive change.

12. MSE: appearance and behaviour

Describe observed grooming, dress, hygiene, eye contact, rapport, psychomotor activity, agitation, retardation and abnormal movements. Record objective observations without stigmatising language.

PLAB application: “Dishevelled, limited eye contact, slowed movements” is more precise than “looks depressed”.

13. MSE: speech, mood and affect

Assess speech rate, volume, amount, rhythm and coherence. Ask the patient to describe their mood (subjective); observe affect (range, reactivity, congruence and stability).

PLAB application: Mood is reported; affect is observed.

14. MSE: thought and perception

Describe thought form (flight of ideas, loosening, thought blocking), thought content (delusions, obsessions, hopelessness, suicidal ideas) and perception (hallucinations or illusions).

PLAB application: A hallucination is a perception without an external stimulus.

15. MSE: cognition, insight and judgement

Assess orientation, attention and memory when indicated; use validated cognitive tools where appropriate. Explore insight into difficulties and treatment preferences. Capacity is decision-specific and time-specific.

PLAB application: Do not equate disagreement or psychiatric diagnosis with incapacity.

16. Closing, formulation and handover

Summarise findings, check understanding, discuss differential diagnoses, physical investigations and next steps. Agree an appropriate plan and safety-net. In an OSCE, present a concise biopsychosocial formulation and immediate risks.

PLAB application: Use SBAR for urgent handover and clearly state the disposition.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Mood, affect and congruence

A person can report sadness while smiling nervously. Document subjective mood separately from affect and whether observed affect fits the described emotional state.

Thought form versus thought content

Form describes organisation of thinking (e.g., tangentiality or flight of ideas); content describes what is believed or thought (e.g., persecutory delusions or suicidal ideas).

Distinguishing hallucinations and intrusive thoughts

A hallucination is experienced as perception; an intrusive thought is experienced as a thought or image. Ask about location, quality, insight and control rather than assuming.

Dynamic risk formulation

Risk changes with intoxication, recent losses, access to means and supports. NICE advises against predictive risk scales or categories for decisions about treatment or discharge after self-harm.

Capacity and consent

A psychiatric diagnosis does not automatically remove capacity. Support understanding and communication, then assess the specific decision and relevant legal framework.

OSCE communication under pressure

Use short open questions, signpost sensitive topics, reflect emotion, avoid leading or stigmatising language, summarise and state the next action clearly.
