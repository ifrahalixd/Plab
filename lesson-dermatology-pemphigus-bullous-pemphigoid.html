Blistering Disorders: Pemphigus & Bullous Pemphigoid

PLAB priority: Flaccid painful oral erosions suggest pemphigus vulgaris; tense itchy bullae in an older adult suggest bullous pemphigoid. Severe widespread blistering is an emergency.

01. Recognising a blistering disorder

Ask about onset, itch versus pain, oral or eye symptoms, drug exposure, fever and skin fragility. Examine whether blisters are tense or flaccid, the extent of erosions and mucosal involvement. Assess hydration, infection and systemic illness.

PLAB application: Painful erosions, extensive detachment or ocular involvement warrant urgent specialist assessment.

02. Anatomy determines blister appearance

Intraepidermal cleavage creates fragile, flaccid blisters that rupture easily. Subepidermal cleavage preserves the blister roof and usually produces tense bullae. This distinction helps narrow the differential but does not confirm diagnosis.

PLAB application: Flaccid blisters suggest pemphigus vulgaris; tense itchy bullae suggest bullous pemphigoid.

03. Pemphigus vulgaris: mechanism

IgG autoantibodies against desmoglein adhesion proteins cause acantholysis (loss of keratinocyte cohesion). The split is intraepidermal, producing fragile bullae and erosions. Oral mucosal disease may precede skin involvement.

PLAB application: Persistent painful oral erosions with fragile skin blisters should prompt urgent dermatology referral.

04. Pemphigus vulgaris: presentation

Typically affects adults with painful oral erosions and flaccid blisters on otherwise normal-looking skin. Blisters rupture to leave raw, painful areas; nutritional intake may be impaired. Nikolsky sign may be positive but is neither specific nor a test to perform aggressively.

PLAB application: Do not dismiss recurrent painful mouth erosions as simple aphthous ulcers when skin fragility is present.

05. Bullous pemphigoid: mechanism

Autoantibodies target hemidesmosomal proteins BP180 and BP230 at the dermoepidermal junction. This creates a subepidermal split with a relatively robust blister roof. It commonly affects older adults.

PLAB application: Think bullous pemphigoid in an older patient with severe itch and tense blisters.

06. Bullous pemphigoid: presentation

Severe pruritus can precede blistering for weeks or months, sometimes with urticarial or eczematous plaques. Tense bullae often affect trunk and flexures; mucosal involvement is less common than in pemphigus vulgaris.

PLAB application: Pre-bullous itchy plaques can mimic eczema or urticaria.

07. Diagnosis: two biopsies

Dermatology commonly takes one biopsy of an active lesion for routine histology and a separate perilesional sample for direct immunofluorescence (DIF). DIF shows intercellular IgG/C3 in pemphigus and linear basement-membrane deposition in pemphigoid. Serum autoantibody assays can support diagnosis.

PLAB application: Do not put the DIF specimen in formalin; follow laboratory transport instructions.

08. Distinguishing common conditions

Dermatitis herpetiformis causes intensely itchy grouped papulovesicles on extensor surfaces and is associated with coeliac disease. Linear IgA bullous dermatosis may cause annular clusters. Friction blisters, burns, bullous impetigo and drug reactions can also blister.

PLAB application: Distribution, itch, mucosal disease and medication timeline are key discriminators.

09. Medication-associated blistering

Certain drugs are associated with bullous pemphigoid, including DPP-4 inhibitors in some patients; a medication review is essential. Severe drug reactions such as SJS/TEN typically involve painful skin, systemic illness and mucosal injury.

PLAB application: Never assume all blisters in a patient taking a new medicine are pemphigoid.

10. Initial assessment and safety

Check observations, hydration, pain, nutritional intake and extent of skin loss. Consider bacterial superinfection. If widespread blistering, fever, hypotension, inability to eat or ocular symptoms occur, arrange urgent hospital assessment.

PLAB application: Large denuded areas can cause fluid loss, infection and serious morbidity.

11. Pemphigus treatment principles

Pemphigus vulgaris usually requires dermatologist-led systemic therapy. Corticosteroids and rituximab-based regimens are important options, with steroid-sparing immunosuppressants in selected patients. Support wound care, oral hygiene and infection prevention.

PLAB application: Systemic immunosuppression needs screening, monitoring and shared decision-making.

12. Bullous pemphigoid treatment principles

Potent topical corticosteroids are often effective, including in widespread disease when feasible. Oral doxycycline may be considered in selected patients as a safer initial strategy, depending on disease severity and specialist plan. Systemic steroids or other agents may be needed.

PLAB application: Balance blister control against frailty, diabetes, infection and treatment adverse effects.

13. Supportive skin and mouth care

Use non-adherent dressings, gentle cleansing, emollients, analgesia and appropriate infection assessment. In oral pemphigus, address oral pain, soft diet, hydration and dental/oral-medicine support. Avoid unnecessary blister trauma.

PLAB application: Ask specifically whether the patient can drink, eat and swallow.

14. Complications and monitoring

Risks include skin infection, sepsis, dehydration, malnutrition and treatment-related complications. Monitor disease activity, new lesions, healing and adverse effects of steroids or immunosuppressants. Vaccination and infection-risk counselling may be relevant.

PLAB application: Long-term treatment safety is as important as short-term blister control.

15. Patient explanation and PLAB 2 counselling

Explain that autoimmune blistering diseases are not contagious. Describe the need for biopsy and specialist care without promising a rapid cure. Discuss wound care, medicine adherence, steroid risks and when to seek urgent help.

PLAB application: Use empathetic language for painful visible skin disease and impacts on eating or sleep.

16. Referral and emergency red flags

Refer suspected autoimmune blistering disease promptly to dermatology. Escalate immediately for rapidly progressive widespread erosions, systemic illness, mucosal compromise, eye involvement or suspected SJS/TEN.

PLAB application: The safest exam answer for unstable blistering disease is urgent ABCDE assessment and senior/specialist escalation.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Desmosomes versus hemidesmosomes

Desmosomes join keratinocytes to one another, while hemidesmosomes anchor basal cells to the basement membrane. The anatomical target explains flaccid versus tense blisters.

Why direct immunofluorescence matters

Routine histology shows the level of separation, whereas DIF visualises the location and pattern of immunoglobulin or complement deposits. Together with clinical findings, these distinguish autoimmune blistering diseases.

The pre-bullous pemphigoid trap

Intense itch with urticarial plaques can precede obvious blisters. In an older patient with treatment-resistant pruritus, reconsider the diagnosis and seek dermatology assessment.

Why oral disease is clinically important

Oral pemphigus may impair swallowing, hydration and nutrition and can precede skin disease. Asking about mouth pain changes urgency and supportive management.

Steroid risk and therapeutic trade-offs

Widespread topical steroid treatment may be effective in pemphigoid; systemic treatment can bring hyperglycaemia, infection, osteoporosis and other risks. Individualise with specialist input.

Severe drug reaction differential

SJS/TEN produces painful skin detachment and mucosal damage, often with systemic illness. A new medication timeline and urgent assessment are essential; do not label it routine autoimmune blistering.
