Pneumonia: diagnosis, severity and treatment

Emergency reminder: Severe hypoxaemia, shock or new confusion may signal sepsis or respiratory failure. Escalate urgently.

Pneumonia overview

Pneumonia is infection and inflammation of lung parenchyma, commonly causing cough, fever, breathlessness and focal chest signs.

Clinical application: Assess ABCDE and identify sepsis, respiratory failure and other life-threatening illness.

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)

CAP develops outside hospital or soon after admission. Common pathogens include Streptococcus pneumoniae; viruses and atypical organisms are also possible.

Clinical application: Ask about onset, fever, pleuritic pain, sputum, comorbidities, travel, aspiration and recent antibiotics.

Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)

HAP develops 48 hours or more after hospital admission and was not incubating on admission. Resistant organisms may be more likely depending on local epidemiology.

Clinical application: Review recent antibiotics, cultures, ward outbreaks and local antimicrobial guidance.

Aspiration pneumonia

Aspiration of oropharyngeal or gastric contents can cause chemical pneumonitis or bacterial pneumonia. Risks include dysphagia, reduced consciousness and neurological disease.

Clinical application: Assess swallowing, airway protection and aspiration risk; do not assume every aspiration event needs antibiotics.

History and examination

Look for cough, sputum, fever, rigors, pleuritic pain, dyspnoea, confusion and reduced oral intake. Older patients may present atypically.

Clinical application: Record RR, pulse, BP, temperature, SpO2, mental state, hydration and chest findings such as crackles or bronchial breathing.

Investigations

Chest X-ray can show new consolidation; pulse oximetry guides severity. Consider blood count, renal function, CRP and microbiology according to severity and setting.

Clinical application: Do not delay urgent treatment in an unstable patient. A normal early X-ray does not completely exclude pneumonia.

CURB-65 in hospital

CURB-65 includes Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate ≥30/min, low Blood pressure (systolic <90 or diastolic ≤60 mmHg), and age ≥65 years.

Clinical application: Use CURB-65 alongside clinical judgement, oxygenation, frailty and comorbidities; it is not a substitute for recognising sepsis.

CRB-65 in primary care

CRB-65 omits urea and can support decisions outside hospital. The score is one component of severity assessment.

Clinical application: A low score does not guarantee safety in a hypoxaemic, frail or rapidly deteriorating patient.

CAP antibiotics

Antibiotic choice depends on severity, allergy, pregnancy, recent exposure and local resistance. NICE NG250 provides adult CAP regimens and review guidance.

Clinical application: Follow current NICE and local formulary for exact agents, doses and duration; review IV therapy promptly when clinically improving.

HAP antibiotics

HAP treatment depends on severity, risk of resistant bacteria, recent antimicrobial exposure and local susceptibility data.

Clinical application: Obtain cultures where appropriate, use local antimicrobial policy and review therapy against microbiology results.

Sepsis and emergency escalation

Severe infection may cause hypotension, altered mental state, poor perfusion, hypoxaemia and organ dysfunction.

Clinical application: Escalate immediately, assess lactate and cultures when indicated, give prompt treatment according to sepsis pathways and reassess frequently.

Complications

Parapneumonic effusion, empyema, lung abscess, respiratory failure and sepsis can complicate pneumonia.

Clinical application: Persistent fever, pleuritic pain or poor response should prompt reassessment and consideration of imaging or specialist input.

Supportive care and review

Assess oxygen needs, hydration, analgesia, mobility, thrombosis risk and comorbidity management.

Clinical application: Give oxygen to an appropriate individual target, monitor response and switch from IV to oral antibiotics when appropriate.

Discharge and follow-up

Clinical stability, safe oral intake, improving oxygenation and a support plan guide discharge. Some people need follow-up imaging, especially with persistent symptoms or lung-cancer risk.

Clinical application: Explain expected recovery, medication adherence, smoking cessation and return precautions for worsening breathlessness, confusion or fever.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain pneumonia, expected investigations and why antibiotics may be prescribed; discuss uncertainty and potential complications.

Clinical application: Use plain language, check understanding and safety-net clearly; ask about allergies and adherence.

PLAB 2: Pneumonia counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and ask about symptoms and concerns.
  2. Explore cough, fever, breathlessness, confusion, comorbidities and aspiration risks.
  3. Explain chest X-ray, blood tests and severity assessment.
  4. Discuss antibiotic choice, allergies, expected recovery and possible complications.
  5. Give follow-up and clear emergency safety-net advice.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Consolidation

Inflammatory exudate fills alveoli, reducing gas exchange and producing crackles or bronchial breathing.

Hypoxaemia

Ventilation–perfusion mismatch and shunt impair oxygenation; oxygen targets should be individualised.

CURB-65 limits

A score helps predict risk but does not replace judgement about frailty, oxygenation or rapidly worsening illness.

Aspiration pneumonitis versus pneumonia

Chemical injury after aspiration is not identical to bacterial infection; clinical course guides treatment.

Why cultures matter

Severe illness or resistant-pathogen risk may justify microbiology to target antibiotics.

Non-response

Consider resistant organisms, empyema, alternative diagnosis, inadequate source control or complications.
