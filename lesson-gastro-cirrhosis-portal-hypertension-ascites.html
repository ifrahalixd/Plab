Cirrhosis, Portal Hypertension & Ascites

Emergency: Haematemesis, fever with ascites, new confusion or oliguria in cirrhosis requires urgent assessment.

Cirrhosis overview

Cirrhosis is advanced hepatic fibrosis with regenerative nodules and distorted liver architecture. It can cause portal hypertension and impaired synthetic, metabolic and detoxification function.

PLAB application: Compensated cirrhosis may be asymptomatic; decompensation includes ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy or jaundice.

Causes and risk factors

Common causes include alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, chronic hepatitis B/C, autoimmune hepatitis and cholestatic liver disease.

PLAB application: Ask sensitively about alcohol, metabolic risks, viral exposure, medications and family history.

History and examination

Ask about fatigue, pruritus, jaundice, abdominal distension, GI bleeding, confusion, reduced urine output and infection. Look for spider naevi, palmar erythema, muscle wasting, splenomegaly and ascites.

PLAB application: Check vital signs and assess for asterixis, oedema and signs of infection.

Investigations and severity

Obtain FBC, U&E, creatinine, liver blood tests, albumin, INR and aetiology screen. Ultrasound with Doppler assesses morphology, ascites and portal flow. Non-invasive fibrosis assessment may aid diagnosis.

PLAB application: Child–Pugh and MELD-related scores support prognosis and specialist decisions, but do not replace clinical assessment.

Portal hypertension

Increased resistance to portal blood flow produces portosystemic collaterals, varices, splenomegaly and ascites. Thrombocytopenia may reflect hypersplenism.

PLAB application: Varices can bleed catastrophically; patients need specialist assessment for prevention and surveillance.

Ascites recognition

Ascites presents with abdominal distension, shifting dullness and sometimes breathlessness. Differential diagnoses include malignancy and cardiac or renal disease.

PLAB application: New-onset or hospitalised cirrhotic ascites warrants diagnostic paracentesis to establish cause and exclude infection.

Ascitic fluid analysis

Send ascitic cell count/differential, albumin and total protein; culture when infection is suspected, ideally inoculating blood culture bottles at bedside. Calculate serum–ascites albumin gradient (SAAG).

PLAB application: SAAG ≥11 g/L supports portal-hypertensive ascites; neutrophils ≥250 cells/mm³ suggest spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

Uncomplicated ascites treatment

Specialist management typically includes sodium restriction, spironolactone-based diuretics with or without furosemide, and careful monitoring of weight, renal function and electrolytes.

PLAB application: Avoid routine fluid restriction unless there is a specific indication such as significant hyponatraemia.

Large-volume and refractory ascites

Therapeutic paracentesis relieves tense ascites. Intravenous albumin replacement is indicated following large-volume drainage according to local guidance. Consider TIPS or transplant assessment for selected patients.

PLAB application: Avoid NSAIDs and monitor for acute kidney injury after fluid shifts.

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

SBP may present with fever, pain, encephalopathy, renal impairment or no obvious symptoms. Diagnosis is ascitic neutrophils ≥250 cells/mm³ even if culture is negative.

PLAB application: Urgent antibiotics according to local resistance guidance; specialist-directed albumin may reduce renal complications in eligible patients.

Acute variceal haemorrhage

Haematemesis or melaena with shock in cirrhosis requires ABCDE resuscitation, senior help, blood products as indicated, vasoactive therapy, antibiotics and urgent endoscopy.

PLAB application: Endoscopic variceal band ligation is standard for oesophageal variceal bleeding; refractory cases may need rescue TIPS.

Hepatic encephalopathy

Encephalopathy ranges from sleep disturbance and impaired attention to asterixis, confusion and coma. Triggers include infection, bleeding, constipation, dehydration and sedatives.

PLAB application: Identify and treat triggers. Lactulose is commonly first line; rifaximin may be used for recurrent episodes under specialist care.

Hepatorenal syndrome and AKI

Acute kidney injury in cirrhosis can result from hypovolaemia, sepsis, nephrotoxins or hepatorenal syndrome. Check creatinine trends, urine output and medications.

PLAB application: Stop nephrotoxins, assess volume carefully and seek urgent hepatology/renal input; specialist vasoconstrictor and albumin therapy may be needed.

Surveillance and prevention

Offer aetiology-specific treatment, alcohol support, vaccination where indicated, nutrition assessment, hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance and variceal assessment according to UK pathways.

PLAB application: Ultrasound-based HCC surveillance is commonly performed every 6 months in eligible cirrhotic patients.

Transplant and prognosis

Decompensation, recurrent complications or worsening synthetic function should prompt early specialist transplant consideration when appropriate. Discuss supportive and palliative needs alongside active treatment.

PLAB application: Avoid relying on one blood test; trends, frailty, complications and patient goals matter.

PLAB 2 communication

Explain that liver scarring changes blood flow and function, and discuss causes, abstinence/support where relevant, medications, monitoring and urgent warning signs.

PLAB application: Safety-net for vomiting blood, black stools, fever with ascites, new confusion, severe pain or reduced urine output.

PLAB 2: cirrhosis counselling OSCE

  1. Introduce yourself and explore symptoms, alcohol use without judgement, and concerns.
  2. Explain liver scarring, impaired function and increased portal pressure.
  3. Discuss blood tests, ultrasound, ascitic tap and specialist review.
  4. Explain treatment of the underlying cause, monitoring, nutrition and possible transplant assessment.
  5. Safety-net bleeding, fever, new confusion, severe pain and reduced urine output.

Deep Dive

Fibrosis and vascular resistance

Activated hepatic stellate cells deposit collagen; architectural distortion raises intrahepatic vascular resistance.

SAAG physiology

A high serum–ascites albumin gradient reflects hydrostatic pressure from portal hypertension rather than simply low serum albumin.

SBP pathogenesis

Bacterial translocation can infect ascitic fluid without an obvious surgically treatable source.

Encephalopathy

Gut-derived ammonia and inflammation contribute to cerebral dysfunction; precipitating factors are often reversible.

Hepatorenal syndrome

Advanced circulatory dysfunction in cirrhosis can cause renal vasoconstriction and functional renal impairment.

TIPS

A transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt decompresses portal pressure but can worsen encephalopathy.
