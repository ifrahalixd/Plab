Psoriatic Arthritis

PLAB priority: Psoriasis plus inflammatory joint symptoms, dactylitis, enthesitis or nail changes suggests psoriatic arthritis. A hot swollen joint may be septic; a painful red photophobic eye needs urgent assessment.

01. Definition

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory musculoskeletal disease associated with psoriasis; joint disease can precede skin lesions.

PLAB application: Consider PsA in anyone with psoriasis and persistent joint pain, stiffness, swelling or tendon pain.

02. Epidemiology and associations

PsA affects a subset of people with psoriasis. Family history, nail disease and obesity increase suspicion; severity of skin disease does not reliably predict arthritis severity.

PLAB application: Ask about psoriasis in relatives even when the patient has no obvious rash.

03. Clinical patterns

PsA may cause asymmetric oligoarthritis, symmetric polyarthritis, distal interphalangeal (DIP) arthritis, axial disease or arthritis mutilans. Patterns can overlap or evolve.

PLAB application: Do not rule out PsA because arthritis resembles rheumatoid arthritis.

04. Inflammatory joint symptoms

Swollen tender joints, prolonged morning stiffness and improvement with movement favour inflammatory arthritis. Hands, feet, knees and ankles are often involved.

PLAB application: Differentiate inflammatory symptoms from mechanical osteoarthritis.

05. Dactylitis

Dactylitis is diffuse swelling of an entire finger or toe (sausage digit), reflecting inflammation of multiple structures.

PLAB application: Look at every finger and toe, not just the individual joints.

06. Enthesitis

Enthesitis is inflammation where tendons or ligaments insert into bone, commonly at Achilles tendon or plantar fascia.

PLAB application: Heel pain with psoriasis may represent inflammatory enthesitis.

07. Skin and nail examination

Check scalp, behind ears, umbilicus, gluteal cleft and extensor surfaces for plaques. Nail pitting, onycholysis and oil-drop changes support PsA.

PLAB application: Examine hidden sites sensitively and ask permission.

08. Axial and extra-articular disease

Inflammatory back pain, sacroiliitis, uveitis and inflammatory bowel disease may occur. A painful red photophobic eye requires urgent ophthalmic assessment.

PLAB application: Ask specifically about back pain, eye symptoms and bowel symptoms.

09. Assessment and investigations

Diagnosis is clinical with specialist assessment. CRP/ESR may be raised or normal; RF and anti-CCP are often negative but neither test alone confirms or excludes PsA. X-ray, ultrasound or MRI may help.

PLAB application: Do not delay referral for normal inflammatory markers or negative serology.

10. CASPAR classification

CASPAR criteria are classification tools for research, not a substitute for clinical diagnosis. Features include psoriasis, nail changes, negative RF, dactylitis and juxta-articular new bone formation.

PLAB application: Avoid treating CASPAR as a screening test that must be met before referral.

11. Differential diagnoses

Consider rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, gout, CPPD, reactive arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis and septic arthritis. Acute hot swollen joints require infection exclusion.

PLAB application: A single hot swollen joint needs urgent assessment for septic arthritis.

12. Initial management

Rheumatology-led care combines exercise, physiotherapy, smoking cessation, weight management and cardiovascular risk reduction. NSAIDs may relieve symptoms after checking risks.

PLAB application: Symptom relief is not equivalent to disease control.

13. Disease-modifying treatment

Persistent peripheral arthritis usually needs a conventional synthetic DMARD such as methotrexate, sulfasalazine or leflunomide selected by the specialist. Biologics and targeted therapies may be used for active disease according to UK guidance.

PLAB application: Methotrexate is generally taken once weekly with monitoring; check pregnancy and infection risks.

14. Choosing treatment by disease domain

Peripheral arthritis, axial inflammation, enthesitis, dactylitis, skin psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease may require different therapies. Some medicines can worsen coexisting IBD.

PLAB application: Ask about all disease domains before discussing treatment choices.

15. Monitoring and complications

Monitor disease activity, function, medication toxicity, cardiovascular risk, metabolic syndrome, depression and impact on work. Untreated disease can cause erosions and disability.

PLAB application: Offer coordinated dermatology and rheumatology care when both skin and joints are active.

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety-netting

Explain PsA as an inflammatory condition that can be controlled with treatment; discuss medication monitoring, vaccinations, activity, joint protection and when to seek urgent care.

PLAB application: Urgent red flags: fever with a hot joint, severe eye pain or visual loss, or new neurological symptoms.

Deep Dive

Why PsA affects different tissues

Immune-mediated inflammation targets synovium, entheses, skin and sometimes axial skeleton; clinical domains can coexist.

Enthesis–joint relationship

Inflammation near tendon insertions may contribute to pain, dactylitis and joint structural change.

PsA versus RA

PsA may involve DIP joints, dactylitis, enthesitis, nails and asymmetric patterns; RA often presents with symmetric small-joint synovitis. Overlap exists.

Imaging and structural change

Ultrasound can show synovitis and enthesitis; MRI may detect axial inflammation; radiographs can show erosions with new bone formation in established disease.

Why treatment is individualised

Therapy choice depends on dominant clinical domains, severity, prior response, infection risks, pregnancy considerations and comorbid IBD or uveitis.

Long-term outcomes

Early recognition and sustained inflammation control help preserve function; monitor cardiovascular and mental health alongside joints.
