Allergies: recognise the mechanism, identify risk and prevent harm

Allergy occurs when the immune system reacts inappropriately to a trigger. PLAB cases often test the distinction between mild local symptoms and life-threatening anaphylaxis, the difference between IgE and non-IgE disease, interpretation of allergy investigations, and safe drug-allergy documentation.

Article audio
Never miss anaphylaxis. Sudden airway swelling, stridor, breathing difficulty, or circulatory collapse after a potential allergen requires immediate emergency help, ABCDE and intramuscular adrenaline into the anterolateral thigh. A rash can be absent. Antihistamines and steroids must not delay adrenaline.

1. Immunological mechanisms

IgE-mediated reactions typically begin within minutes to about 2 hours after an exposure. Allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils, triggering histamine, leukotrienes and other mediators. This causes itching, wheals, mucosal swelling, bronchoconstriction and vasodilation. Severity is unpredictable: an earlier mild reaction does not guarantee a future mild reaction.

Non-IgE-mediated food allergy may present hours to days later, particularly with gastrointestinal symptoms and eczema; investigation differs. Mixed-mechanism disease includes some forms of atopic dermatitis and eosinophilic oesophagitis. Non-allergic hypersensitivity can resemble allergy, such as NSAID-related COX-1 reactions and nonimmune mast-cell activation.

2. How to take an allergy-focused history

  • Trigger: precise food ingredient, drug name/dose, insect sting, latex, animal, pollen or occupation. Ask about co-factors (exercise, alcohol, NSAIDs and infection).
  • Timing: minutes, hours or days since exposure; first or repeat exposure; intermittent versus persistent disease.
  • Phenotype: urticaria, pruritus, angioedema, wheeze, throat tightness, vomiting, fainting, diarrhoea, rhinitis or eczema.
  • Severity: breathing/circulatory symptoms, hospital admissions, adrenaline, resuscitation and biphasic recurrence.
  • Background: asthma control, atopy, previous drug reactions, family history, occupation and effect on diet or daily life.
  • Management: what was taken, whether it worked, existing adrenaline autoinjector and understanding of its use.

4. Anaphylaxis — recognise and treat

Suspect anaphylaxis when there is acute onset of serious airway, breathing or circulation compromise, particularly after exposure to a likely allergen. Call the emergency team, remove the trigger if possible, and give IM adrenaline into the anterolateral thigh promptly, following current Resuscitation Council UK age-based dosing and local protocols. Reassess ABCDE and repeat IM adrenaline after around 5 minutes if life-threatening symptoms persist. Position the patient according to physiology (usually lying flat with legs raised if tolerated; pregnant patients left lateral), avoid sudden standing, give oxygen, IV access/fluids when indicated and prepare for expert airway support. Stridor, persistent hypotension or bronchospasm despite treatment require senior/critical care escalation.

Antihistamines may help cutaneous itching after stabilisation but do not reverse airway or cardiovascular compromise. Corticosteroids are not routine first-line anaphylaxis therapy. Observe and investigate according to severity, clinical course, current NICE NG258 recommendations and local pathways. Explain recurrence risk, adrenaline autoinjector training when indicated and specialist allergy follow-up.

5. Common outpatient allergic disorders

Allergic rhinitis: frequent sneezing, rhinorrhoea, itching, sometimes conjunctivitis. Intranasal corticosteroids are effective for persistent/moderate symptoms; consider saline and non-sedating antihistamines. Evaluate troublesome nasal obstruction or unilateral symptoms. Urticaria: wheals are transient; identify triggers but extensive blanket food panels are usually unhelpful. If present longer than 6 weeks, consider chronic spontaneous urticaria. Atopic eczema: barrier dysfunction and inflammation require moisturisation and appropriate topical therapy; a dietary exclusion is not automatically justified.

6. Food allergy assessment

Obtain reproducible history including timing, portion size and co-factors. For suspected IgE-mediated food allergy, request skin-prick testing and/or specific IgE directed only to plausible allergens and interpreted with the history. A positive test shows sensitisation, not necessarily clinical allergy. For suspected non-IgE reactions, specialist-supervised elimination and planned reintroduction may be considered; avoid prolonged unbalanced diets. Oral food challenge is a supervised specialist test, not a home experiment where severe reactions are possible. Refer children with severe, complex, multiple-food or growth-related concerns.

7. Drug allergy: record what actually happened

Document suspected medicine name, formulation, dose, time-to-reaction, symptoms, severity, number of doses, treatment and alternative tolerated drugs. Differentiate predictable side effects (for example nausea) from immune allergy. A vague “penicillin allergy” label can force less appropriate antibiotics: when a history is unclear, seek specialist assessment and delabelling where safe. Suspected severe delayed reactions (SJS/TEN, DRESS), anaphylaxis and severe NSAID hypersensitivity require specialist pathways. Never conduct unsupervised re-exposure after a serious reaction.

8. Investigations and clinical interpretation

  • Skin-prick testing: immediate IgE sensitisation; only in suitable supervised settings.
  • Specific IgE: useful for targeted allergens, not as a broad general screening panel.
  • Serum tryptase: may support a recent mast-cell activation event after anaphylaxis; normal values do not rule it out. Follow current sampling guidance.
  • Complement C4 and C1 inhibitor: consider in recurrent angioedema without urticaria, particularly family history or abdominal attacks.
  • Peak flow/spirometry: consider coexisting asthma and assess control, especially after severe reactions.
  • Patch testing: specialist evaluation for delayed contact allergy; different from skin prick testing.

9. Five worked PLAB clinical cases

Case 1 — Peanut exposure with faintness. A teenager suddenly develops wheeze, throat tightness, urticaria and dizziness after eating peanuts. Reasoning: anaphylaxis with airway/breathing/circulatory involvement. Action: emergency team, IM adrenaline, ABCDE, monitoring; later allergy referral and emergency plan.
Case 2 — Itchy wheals only. A well adult develops migrating itchy raised lesions after a viral illness, with normal breathing and BP. Reasoning: acute urticaria without anaphylaxis. Action: evaluate triggers, use a non-sedating H1 antihistamine as appropriate and explain red flags.
Case 3 — Swelling without a rash. A 58-year-old taking an ACE inhibitor develops progressive tongue swelling without urticaria. Reasoning: bradykinin-mediated angioedema is possible. Action: urgent airway assessment and stop the offending medicine; do not rely on routine antihistamines or steroids to control it.
Case 4 — “Penicillin allergy” label. An adult reports nausea when taking amoxicillin aged 8 but no rash, swelling or breathing symptoms. Reasoning: side effect versus true allergy; label may be incorrect. Action: document precise history and consider formal delabelling pathway, not home re-challenge.
Case 5 — Child reacting to milk. An infant has eczema, vomiting and stool changes, sometimes delayed after cow's milk intake, with normal growth. Reasoning: consider non-IgE-mediated cow's milk protein allergy but alternative diagnoses are common. Action: allergy-focused history and structured dietetic/specialist advice; avoid unnecessary restriction.

10. PLAB 2 examination and counselling

Start by assessing immediate danger. For a stable patient, explain the provisional trigger, why tests are selective, uncertainty in predicted severity and the role of avoidance. Demonstrate adrenaline autoinjector use when prescribed, ask the patient to teach back, discuss carrying devices and individual action plans, and communicate with school or workplace if relevant. Provide written records of suspected drug allergy and clear advice about emergency symptoms and follow-up.

Three key distinctions

Anaphylaxis versus urticaria: wheals alone are not anaphylaxis; sudden breathing or circulatory compromise is the critical discriminator. IgE allergy versus sensitisation: a positive specific IgE test does not prove clinical allergy without compatible history. Histamine versus bradykinin angioedema: absence of urticaria, ACE inhibitor use or recurrent familial attacks suggests the latter.

Deep Dive · immune mechanisms and diagnostic pitfalls

Why allergic reactions look so different

1. Antigen recognition and IgE

After sensitisation, allergen-specific IgE binds FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. Subsequent allergen exposure cross-links surface IgE and produces rapid mediator release. Sensitisation without symptoms is possible, so tests must be interpreted against exposure history.

2. Mediators and organ responses

Histamine increases vascular permeability, causes wheal/flare reactions and contributes to bronchospasm; leukotrienes and prostaglandins amplify respiratory and vascular effects. Generalised vasodilation and capillary leak help explain distributive shock. A rash can be absent despite severe mediator effects.

3. Why adrenaline works

Alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction counteracts hypotension and airway oedema, beta-1 supports cardiac output and beta-2 promotes bronchodilation. The intramuscular anterolateral thigh provides reliable delivery. Delay in adrenaline is a modifiable risk in severe anaphylaxis.

4. Delayed reactions are different

T-cell-mediated reactions to drugs can take days or weeks and can involve liver, kidneys and mucous membranes. Immediate IgE tests cannot replace assessment of suspected SJS/TEN or DRESS. Some non-IgE food syndromes involve delayed GI inflammation rather than immediate wheals or wheeze.

5. Why histamine therapy fails in some angioedema

ACE inhibitors increase bradykinin, which increases vascular permeability without the mast-cell mechanism typical of histaminergic swelling. Important clues include absence of itch or hives, and facial, tongue or laryngeal swelling. Airway protection and withdrawal of the drug are central; routine antihistamines are not reliably effective.

6. Tryptase interpretation

Acute and baseline serum tryptase can support mast-cell activation, but sampling timing, baseline variability and a normal measurement limit interpretation. Treatment is a clinical emergency decision and must never await results.

7. PLAB diagnostic traps

Do not call nausea alone a penicillin allergy. Do not order broad specific-IgE panels for vague symptoms. Do not reassure someone with hypotension because skin is normal. Do not send a patient with severe delayed drug rash for unsupervised challenge. Do not mistake a positive test for a diagnosis or forget written safety plans after anaphylaxis.
