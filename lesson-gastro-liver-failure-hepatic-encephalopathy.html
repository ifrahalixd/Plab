Liver Failure & Hepatic Encephalopathy

Emergency: New encephalopathy with coagulopathy or severe decompensation requires ABCDE assessment, glucose testing and urgent specialist escalation.

01. Definitions and urgency

Acute liver failure (ALF) is acute severe liver injury with coagulopathy (commonly INR ≥1.5) and hepatic encephalopathy in someone without pre-existing cirrhosis, usually within 26 weeks of illness. Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is acute deterioration in chronic liver disease with organ failure; definitions vary. Both require urgent senior and specialist involvement.

PLAB application: A patient with new confusion, jaundice and prolonged INR needs emergency assessment, not routine outpatient review.

02. Liver functions and what fails

The liver synthesises clotting factors and albumin, metabolises drugs and toxins, handles bilirubin, supports glucose homeostasis and immunity. Liver failure can cause bleeding tendency, hypoglycaemia, encephalopathy, renal injury and circulatory instability.

PLAB application: INR reflects synthetic dysfunction but does not reliably predict bleeding risk in cirrhosis.

03. Causes of acute liver failure

Important causes include paracetamol toxicity, acute viral hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury, autoimmune hepatitis, ischaemic hepatitis, Wilson disease and pregnancy-related disorders such as acute fatty liver of pregnancy. History must include prescribed, over-the-counter and herbal drugs.

PLAB application: Always ask specifically about paracetamol, including staggered or uncertain ingestion.

04. Causes of decompensation and ACLF

In cirrhosis, infection (including SBP), gastrointestinal bleeding, alcohol-related hepatitis, dehydration, constipation, sedatives and renal injury can precipitate decompensation or encephalopathy.

PLAB application: Look for a reversible precipitant while supporting airway and circulation.

05. Hepatic encephalopathy: clinical features

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is brain dysfunction associated with liver insufficiency and/or portosystemic shunting. It ranges from subtle impaired attention and sleep reversal to disorientation, asterixis, somnolence and coma. Asterixis is not specific to HE.

PLAB application: Do not assume all confusion in cirrhosis is HE: check glucose, sepsis, stroke, intoxication and intracranial causes.

06. West Haven clinical grading

Grade I: subtle attention or sleep changes; II: lethargy, disorientation to time, personality change or asterixis; III: marked confusion, somnolence but arousable; IV: coma. Grading is approximate and must be interpreted clinically.

PLAB application: Grade III–IV HE may compromise airway protection and warrants urgent critical care review.

07. Initial ABCDE assessment

Assess airway, breathing, circulation, disability and exposure. Check bedside glucose immediately, GCS, observations, fluid balance, temperature and signs of bleeding/infection. Obtain senior help and consider ICU when encephalopathy is advanced or physiology unstable.

PLAB application: Treat hypoglycaemia promptly; do not wait for laboratory confirmation.

08. Blood tests and investigations

Request FBC, U&E/creatinine, LFTs, bilirubin, albumin, PT/INR, glucose, blood gas/lactate and infection tests guided by presentation. Consider paracetamol level, viral serology, autoimmune markers, pregnancy test and ultrasound/Doppler according to context.

PLAB application: Ammonia can support assessment in selected cases but is not diagnostic alone; normal ammonia should prompt reconsideration of HE.

09. Imaging and infection screen

Assess for pneumonia, urinary infection, SBP and GI bleeding. In a patient with ascites and suspected infection or new decompensation, perform prompt diagnostic paracentesis. Brain imaging is guided by trauma, focal signs, seizures or alternative diagnoses.

PLAB application: Ascitic neutrophils ≥250 cells/mm³ support SBP and require urgent treatment.

10. Managing acute liver failure

Emergency admission, early hepatology/transplant-centre discussion, close neurological and haemodynamic monitoring, correction of hypoglycaemia, cause-specific treatment and avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs are central. N-acetylcysteine is essential for suspected paracetamol toxicity and may be considered in some non-paracetamol ALF under specialist guidance.

PLAB application: Do not delay referral while waiting for the full cause work-up.

11. Managing overt hepatic encephalopathy

Identify and treat triggers; give lactulose, titrated to approximately 2–3 soft stools per day, avoiding dehydration. Rifaximin may be added for recurrent overt HE under local guidance. Assess swallowing and aspiration risk; involve seniors for severe HE.

PLAB application: Excess lactulose can worsen dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities.

12. Nutrition and medication safety

Avoid routine protein restriction: malnutrition and sarcopenia worsen outcomes. Ensure appropriate nutrition, review benzodiazepines/opioids and other sedating medicines, and correct potassium or sodium abnormalities carefully.

PLAB application: A cirrhotic patient with HE generally needs adequate protein, not a low-protein diet.

13. Complications and monitoring

Watch for cerebral oedema (especially ALF), sepsis, bleeding, renal failure, hypoglycaemia, respiratory failure and multiorgan dysfunction. Reassess mental state, glucose, electrolytes, INR, urine output and lactate frequently according to severity.

PLAB application: Deteriorating consciousness is a medical emergency even if bilirubin is unchanged.

14. Transplant referral and prognosis

Acute liver failure needs early transplant-centre assessment. Prognostic tools such as King’s College Criteria may guide specialist decisions but must not replace early referral. In chronic liver disease, MELD-based assessment and clinical decompensation inform transplant discussions.

PLAB application: Do not wait for formal criteria to be met before seeking specialist advice.

15. PLAB 2: communication and counselling

Explain that the liver is not clearing substances normally and this can affect thinking. Speak to relatives with consent or best-interest framework when capacity is impaired. Discuss treatment of triggers, medicines, avoiding alcohol where relevant and follow-up.

PLAB application: Assess decision-making capacity for the specific decision and document clearly.

16. Exam traps and safety-netting

ALF is not the same as isolated high ALT; HE is a clinical diagnosis with alternatives; lactulose is titrated, not given to induce severe diarrhoea; paracetamol toxicity can occur without a clear single overdose history. Escalate for drowsiness, bleeding, fever, severe abdominal pain or reduced urine output.

PLAB application: Prioritise ABCDE, glucose, sepsis evaluation and urgent specialist escalation.

Deep Dive

Ammonia and astrocytes

Gut-derived ammonia bypasses or overwhelms hepatic detoxification. Astrocyte glutamine accumulation contributes to altered neurotransmission and swelling; inflammation and other toxins also matter.

ALF versus cirrhosis physiology

ALF can cause rapid cerebral oedema and profound metabolic disturbance. Chronic cirrhosis often produces portosystemic shunting and recurrent HE with triggers.

Why INR matters

The liver makes procoagulant and anticoagulant proteins. INR is useful for ALF recognition and prognosis but poorly predicts spontaneous bleeding risk in cirrhosis.

West Haven limitations

Grades are descriptive and interobserver variation occurs. Always document specific orientation, alertness, asterixis and GCS rather than grade alone.

Lactulose and rifaximin mechanisms

Lactulose reduces colonic ammonia absorption and increases stool transit; rifaximin alters gut bacterial ammonia production. Avoid excessive catharsis.

Transplant and prognostic tools

King’s College Criteria and MELD-based scores inform specialist assessment, but no score should delay urgent referral or replace clinical judgement.
