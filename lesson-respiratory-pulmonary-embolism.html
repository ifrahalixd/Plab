Pulmonary Embolism: recognition, diagnosis and treatment

Emergency reminder: Suspected PE with shock, persistent hypotension or collapse requires immediate emergency assessment. Educational content, not a prescribing protocol.

Uses browser text-to-speech where supported.

What is pulmonary embolism?

A pulmonary embolus is obstruction of a pulmonary artery, most often by thrombus originating from deep veins of the legs or pelvis. It may cause impaired gas exchange, right ventricular strain and cardiovascular collapse.

Clinical application: Suspect PE with otherwise unexplained acute dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxaemia, haemoptysis or syncope.

Risk factors and Virchow triad

Venous stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability predispose to venous thromboembolism. Risk factors include recent surgery, immobility, active cancer, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen exposure and previous VTE.

Clinical application: Ask about recent travel, surgery, hospitalisation, leg symptoms, malignancy and prior VTE; absence of risk factors does not exclude PE.

Initial assessment

Assess ABCDE, respiratory rate, pulse, blood pressure, oxygen saturation and signs of shock. Look for unilateral leg swelling and features of alternative diagnoses.

Clinical application: Haemodynamic instability or suspected massive PE requires immediate senior/emergency input, monitoring and resuscitation.

Clinical probability: Wells score

NICE NG158 uses the two-level PE Wells score to classify PE as likely (>4 points) or unlikely (4 points or less). Clinical judgement remains essential.

Clinical application: Wells likely: offer immediate CTPA where suitable. Wells unlikely: obtain D-dimer unless an appropriate rule-out strategy applies.

PERC rule

In carefully selected patients with low clinical suspicion, the Pulmonary Embolism Rule-out Criteria may help avoid unnecessary investigations. It is not a substitute for clinical assessment and is not for moderate/high suspicion.

Clinical application: Use only within an appropriate local pathway and with all relevant criteria met.

D-dimer

D-dimer is sensitive but non-specific. It rises in infection, pregnancy, cancer and after surgery. A negative result in an appropriately low-probability patient can help exclude PE.

Clinical application: Do not use a negative D-dimer to rule out PE in a high-probability patient; follow NG158 for age-adjusted thresholds and local assays.

CT pulmonary angiography

CTPA is the principal imaging test for suspected PE when indicated. It may show intraluminal filling defects and signs of right heart strain.

Clinical application: Consider contrast allergy, renal impairment and pregnancy-specific imaging decisions. Alternative imaging may include V/Q scanning.

Other investigations

ECG may show sinus tachycardia or right heart strain, but a normal ECG does not exclude PE. Chest X-ray is often non-specific. Troponin, BNP and echo may assist risk stratification in selected patients.

Clinical application: Use investigations to assess severity and alternatives, not as standalone rule-out tests.

DVT and PE

Unilateral leg swelling, pain or tenderness may indicate DVT. A confirmed proximal DVT in a patient with suspected PE may guide management when chest imaging is unsuitable.

Clinical application: Perform appropriate leg assessment and follow a validated diagnostic pathway.

Anticoagulation

Anticoagulants prevent clot propagation and recurrence while endogenous fibrinolysis resolves thrombus. NICE NG158 supports a DOAC such as apixaban or rivaroxaban for many adults with confirmed proximal DVT/PE, subject to suitability.

Clinical application: Check bleeding risk, renal function, pregnancy status, interactions and contraindications. Start interim therapeutic anticoagulation when indicated if diagnostic imaging is delayed, following NG158 and local policy.

Duration of treatment

Confirmed PE generally requires at least 3 months of anticoagulation. Continuing beyond this depends on whether the event was provoked, recurrence risk, bleeding risk, cancer and patient preference.

Clinical application: Arrange reassessment before stopping; never apply a fixed duration without considering clinical context.

High-risk PE

PE with persistent hypotension or shock is life-threatening and may require reperfusion treatment such as systemic thrombolysis or specialist catheter/surgical therapy.

Clinical application: Activate emergency/critical-care and PE response pathways. Thrombolysis is not routine for haemodynamically stable PE.

Pregnancy and postpartum

Pregnancy and the postpartum period increase VTE risk. Diagnosis and treatment require pregnancy-specific pathways; LMWH is commonly used, while DOACs are generally avoided during pregnancy.

Clinical application: Escalate to obstetric/medical specialists and use appropriate imaging and anticoagulation protocols.

Complications and follow-up

Possible complications include recurrence, bleeding from anticoagulation and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH). Persistent dyspnoea warrants reassessment.

Clinical application: Give clear follow-up, medication safety advice and red flags for worsening breathing or major bleeding.

Differential diagnoses

Acute coronary syndrome, pneumothorax, pneumonia, asthma/COPD exacerbation, aortic dissection and musculoskeletal pain can resemble PE.

Clinical application: A focused differential prevents anchoring; use clinical context and investigations.

PLAB 2 explanation

Explain the suspected clot in the lung, why tests are needed, how anticoagulation helps and what bleeding precautions mean.

Clinical application: Discuss adherence, interactions, pregnancy planning if relevant, follow-up and emergency return precautions.

PLAB 2: PE counselling OSCE

  1. Assess symptoms and red flags; explain urgent testing.
  2. Explore risk factors, pregnancy, prior VTE and bleeding risk.
  3. Explain Wells score, D-dimer and CTPA in plain language.
  4. Discuss anticoagulant benefits, adherence, interactions and bleeding precautions.
  5. Give emergency return advice and follow-up arrangements.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Virchow triad

Stasis, endothelial injury and hypercoagulability help explain VTE risk.

V/Q mismatch

Occluded vessels are ventilated but poorly perfused, contributing to hypoxaemia.

Right ventricular failure

Acute rise in pulmonary vascular resistance strains the right ventricle and may reduce cardiac output.

Why D-dimer is limited

Fibrin breakdown products rise in many inflammatory, physiological and postoperative states.

Risk stratification

Blood pressure, RV function, cardiac biomarkers and comorbidities guide risk and disposition.

Anticoagulant safety

Check renal function, bleeding risk, interactions, pregnancy status and follow-up arrangements.
