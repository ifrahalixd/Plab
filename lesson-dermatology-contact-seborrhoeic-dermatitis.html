Contact Dermatitis, Seborrhoeic Dermatitis & Other Eczematous Disorders

PLAB priority: Distinguish irritant from allergic contact dermatitis, recognise seborrhoeic distribution, and know when to patch test or refer.

01. Clinical approach

Dermatitis describes inflamed skin with itch, dryness, scaling, erythema, fissures or vesicles. Ask about onset, distribution, exposure, work, hobbies, products, recurrence and sleep.

PLAB application: Inspect the pattern and ask what contacts that exact area.

02. Irritant contact dermatitis

Direct skin barrier injury from repeated water, soaps, detergents, solvents or friction; no prior sensitisation is required. Hands are commonly affected.

PLAB application: Healthcare workers with repeated handwashing and painful dry fissures often have irritant dermatitis.

03. Allergic contact dermatitis

Delayed type IV hypersensitivity after sensitisation. Common triggers include nickel, fragrances, preservatives, rubber accelerators and hair dye chemicals. Eruption may extend beyond contact area.

PLAB application: A rash under jewellery or after hair dye suggests allergy; consider patch testing.

04. Distinguishing irritant and allergic causes

Irritant dermatitis often burns or stings and relates to cumulative exposure; allergic dermatitis is often intensely itchy and appears hours to days after exposure. Overlap is common.

PLAB application: History and patch testing—not appearance alone—separate many cases.

05. Distribution clues

Hands: wet work or gloves; eyelids: cosmetics, nail products or transferred allergens; ears: earrings; feet: footwear chemicals; face: skincare or fragrance.

PLAB application: Ask about products used elsewhere that transfer via hands.

06. Skin of colour

Inflammation may appear violaceous, grey, darker or less visibly red. Lichenification and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation may be prominent.

PLAB application: Do not underestimate severity because redness is subtle.

07. Management: exposure control

Identify and avoid the irritant or confirmed allergen; substitute gentle fragrance-free products; use protective gloves appropriately, with cotton liners when needed; limit prolonged occlusion.

PLAB application: Avoidance is foundational; medication alone will not fix ongoing exposure.

08. Emollients and soap substitutes

Apply fragrance-free emollients frequently, especially after washing; use gentle cleansers. Explain that contaminated emollient-soaked fabrics can catch fire readily.

PLAB application: Give a practical hand-care plan for work and home.

09. Topical corticosteroids

Use a site- and severity-appropriate potency for short flare courses; thinner skin of face and flexures requires greater caution. Review response and adherence.

PLAB application: Avoid prolonged unsupervised potent steroids on eyelids or face.

10. When to refer or patch test

Refer persistent, recurrent, occupational or unexplained dermatitis for dermatology assessment and patch testing. Patch testing identifies delayed contact allergy; it is not a routine test for irritant dermatitis.

PLAB application: Persistent hand eczema despite avoidance merits specialist review.

11. Seborrhoeic dermatitis

Chronic relapsing inflammation in sebaceous areas: scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, ears and chest; greasy scale and itch are common. Malassezia-associated inflammation contributes.

PLAB application: Greasy scale around eyebrows and scalp suggests seborrhoeic dermatitis.

12. Seborrhoeic dermatitis treatment

Use antifungal shampoos such as ketoconazole for scalp disease and appropriate topical antifungals for facial areas; short courses of mild topical corticosteroids may calm inflamed flares.

PLAB application: Explain maintenance and relapse rather than promising a permanent cure.

13. Infantile seborrhoeic dermatitis

Cradle cap causes greasy scalp scale in infants, often without marked itch. Gentle emollient and brushing may help; avoid aggressive scale removal.

PLAB application: Differentiate from itchy atopic eczema and assess feeding/general wellbeing.

14. Discoid and asteatotic eczema

Discoid eczema forms itchy coin-shaped plaques, often on limbs; asteatotic eczema causes dry cracked “crazy paving” skin, often on older adults’ shins.

PLAB application: Check for tinea before treating an annular scaly lesion with steroid alone.

15. Differentials and complications

Consider tinea, psoriasis, scabies, atopic eczema, rosacea and secondary infection. Increasing pain, warmth, purulence, fever or rapid spread warrants assessment.

PLAB application: Steroids can mask dermatophyte infection (tinea incognito).

16. PLAB 2 counselling and safety net

Ask about occupation, protective equipment, handwashing, skincare and impact on life; demonstrate application and explain flare management. Seek help for systemic illness, spreading painful infection or failure to improve.

PLAB application: Use shared decisions and realistic exposure-reduction advice.

Mechanisms and clinical reasoning

Deep Dive

Barrier injury and wet work

Frequent water exposure removes protective lipids and increases transepidermal water loss. Detergents and friction amplify barrier disruption; irritant dermatitis can develop without a specific allergy.

Delayed hypersensitivity

Allergic contact dermatitis begins with sensitisation and re-exposure recruits antigen-specific T cells. This explains the delay after exposure and why patch testing is useful.

Pattern recognition and transfer

The location of dermatitis can reflect direct contact, airborne exposures or transfer from fingers. Eyelid dermatitis may come from nail cosmetics rather than an eye product.

Why seborrhoeic dermatitis recurs

Inflammatory response to Malassezia in sebum-rich areas contributes to relapsing scale. Antifungals reduce yeast-associated inflammation but may not prevent all recurrences.

Safe steroid selection

Skin thickness, anatomical site, age and severity guide potency and duration. Use appropriate quantities and reassess persistent disease rather than escalating indefinitely.

Mimics and diagnostic traps

Tinea can resemble discoid eczema; steroids may obscure fungal signs. Psoriasis, scabies and bacterial infection also alter management, so reassess unexpected nonresponse.
