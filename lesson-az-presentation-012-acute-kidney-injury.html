Acute Kidney Injury: recognise, reverse, protect

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is an abrupt deterioration in kidney excretory function, assessed through a rise in serum creatinine and/or reduced urine output. It is a syndrome rather than a single diagnosis: rapidly identify whether the cause is reduced perfusion (pre-renal), intrinsic kidney damage or urinary outflow obstruction (post-renal).

Article audio
Do not miss: life-threatening hyperkalaemia, pulmonary oedema, severe acidosis, sepsis/shock, obstructed infected kidney, and rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. A normal initial creatinine does not rule out evolving AKI, especially when muscle mass is low or injury is very recent.

1. Diagnose and stage AKI

NICE NG148 detection criteria: creatinine increases by ≥26 μmol/L within 48 hours, rises by ≥50% from baseline within 7 days, or urine output falls below 0.5 mL/kg/hour for more than 6 hours in adults. Compare with previous results. In established CKD, a new acute rise is still AKI (AKI on CKD).

Stages are assigned by the most severe criterion. Creatinine often lags behind kidney injury; oliguria may be an early sign but can also reflect other clinical factors. eGFR equations are unreliable in rapidly changing kidney function and should not be used to stage AKI.

4. Examination and initial severity assessment

Perform ABCDE and vital signs. Evaluate perfusion (mental state, capillary refill, pulse and BP), hydration (mucosa, postural symptoms, fluid history), and signs of overload (raised JVP, crackles, peripheral oedema). Inspect for purpura or rash; examine abdomen for a distended bladder, masses and costovertebral tenderness. Record weight, intake/output and urine output accurately. A bladder scan may promptly detect retention.

Fluid pitfall: AKI does not automatically mean dehydration. Repeated large fluid boluses can harm patients with pulmonary oedema or cardiogenic shock. Resuscitate only when clinically indicated, with frequent reassessment.

5. Core investigations and their interpretation

Immediately: U&Es including creatinine, potassium and bicarbonate; FBC, glucose, urine dipstick and ECG where hyperkalaemia is possible. Consider blood gas and lactate in acutely unwell patients; CK if rhabdomyolysis suspected; inflammatory markers and cultures if infection suspected. Compare trends rather than relying on a single creatinine.

Urine dip: blood plus protein without infection should prompt consideration of acute nephritis and nephrology advice. Blood on dip with few red cells and high CK suggests myoglobinuria. Protein may be seen with glomerular disease; assess proteinuria as directed.

Imaging: urgent renal tract ultrasound within 24 hours if AKI cause is unclear or obstruction risk is present (NICE NG148). Suspected infected obstruction/pyonephrosis requires immediate specialist evaluation and rapid imaging/source control. An unobstructed early scan does not explain every case; reassess if suspicion remains.

6. Management: address the cause, not the creatinine number

  1. Stabilise: ABCDE, monitor observations, ensure appropriate IV access and escalate deterioration. Treat sepsis and shock promptly.
  2. Fluid strategy: assess volume and haemodynamics; use titrated isotonic crystalloid if hypovolaemic, reassessing lungs, perfusion and urine output. Treat fluid overload by cause; avoid indiscriminate fluids.
  3. Medication review: stop nephrotoxins where appropriate and review doses of renally cleared drugs. Decisions about ACEi/ARB/diuretic interruption depend on volume status, indication, haemodynamics and local policy; plan restart once stable.
  4. Obstruction: bladder scan and catheterisation if retention, urological referral for upper tract obstruction or infected obstructed kidney, and urgent drainage when indicated.
  5. Monitor: repeat U&Es and acid–base results based on severity, ECG, strict fluid balance, weight and urine output. Do not use loop diuretics to 'treat AKI' routinely; they may be appropriate for fluid overload.
  6. Follow-up: document cause, recovery, medicines restart and reassessment of renal function, proteinuria and blood pressure; AKI can lead to CKD.

7. When to ask for urgent dialysis / kidney replacement therapy

Remember AEIOU as a memory aid, not a strict numerical rule: Acidosis that is refractory and clinically significant; dangerous Electrolyte disturbance (notably refractory hyperkalaemia); selected dialysable Intoxications; refractory fluid Overload/pulmonary oedema; and symptomatic Uraemia (for example, encephalopathy or pericarditis). NICE recommends immediate nephrology/critical-care referral when complications fail medical management. Dialysis decisions depend on the whole patient, not a solitary urea/creatinine value.

Case A: gastroenteritis + ibuprofen60-year-old, poor intake, hypotension, creatinine 90→172 μmol/L. Think hypovolaemic AKI compounded by NSAIDs; assess perfusion, titrate fluids if indicated, review medicines and repeat tests.
Case B: unable to pass urine77-year-old, suprapubic pain, enlarged bladder, anuria. Suspect outlet obstruction; assess, bladder scan and decompress when appropriate, then monitor post-obstructive diuresis.
Case C: creatinine rise + potassium 6.8Weakness, ECG changes. Start immediate monitored hyperkalaemia protocol and urgent senior/renal involvement; treat cardiac membrane risk and lower potassium without waiting for imaging.
Case D: cola urine + haemoptysisRising creatinine with blood/protein on urine dip. Consider rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis or pulmonary-renal vasculitis; urgent nephrology and targeted work-up.

9. PLAB 2 OSCE: explain AKI to a patient

“Your kidneys are suddenly not filtering waste as effectively as they usually do. We are checking your blood tests, urine output, medicines, fluid balance and whether urine flow is blocked. Some causes improve quickly when treated, but complications such as high potassium need urgent action. We'll monitor the trend and arrange follow-up even if your tests improve.”

Safety-net: new breathlessness, palpitations, worsening confusion, markedly reduced urine, severe weakness, fever or deterioration requires urgent medical review.

High-yield discrimination

Low BP or dehydration suggests hypoperfusion, but exclude congestive cardiorenal syndrome before giving repeated fluids. Blood + protein on dip without infection suggests intrinsic glomerular disease. Anuria raises suspicion for complete obstruction or severe renal injury. Always retrieve the baseline creatinine, medication list and urine output.

Advanced renal physiology and diagnostic traps

Deep Dive: why kidneys fail acutely

1. Renal perfusion and autoregulation

The glomerular filtration rate depends on adequate renal plasma flow and glomerular capillary pressure. Afferent arteriolar prostaglandin-mediated dilation and efferent arteriolar angiotensin II-mediated constriction help maintain filtration under some low-flow conditions. NSAIDs impair the former; ACE inhibitors and ARBs reduce the latter. These medicines can be appropriate chronically but require careful reassessment during acute hypoperfusion.

2. Tubular damage and cast formation

Sustained ischaemia and some toxins injure renal tubular cells. Their detachment and cell debris obstruct tubules; inflammatory signalling and back-leak of filtrate contribute to sustained filtration loss. “Muddy brown” granular casts can suggest acute tubular injury, but absence of casts does not exclude it.

3. Why creatinine is a delayed marker

After an abrupt fall in GFR, serum creatinine takes time to accumulate toward a new steady state. Creatinine generation varies with muscle mass, diet and medication effects. A frail patient may have serious AKI despite only a modest absolute concentration. In dynamic AKI, automated eGFR is not physiologically reliable.

4. Potassium and acid–base disturbance

Decreased renal potassium excretion, cellular shifts and tissue breakdown may cause hyperkalaemia. Severe cases threaten ventricular arrhythmia. Metabolic acidosis may reflect reduced acid excretion and the underlying shock state. ECG assessment and emergent treatment depend on measured potassium, clinical status and current UKKA protocols.

5. Why the urine dip matters

Haematuria with proteinuria raises glomerular inflammation; leukocytes can point toward urinary tract infection or interstitial nephritis; a haem-positive dip without microscopy red cells may reflect myoglobin. No single finding fully distinguishes causes.

6. Why ‘give fluids to all AKI’ is wrong

Improving effective circulating volume may reverse hypoperfusion; however, increased hydrostatic pressure in congestive heart failure can worsen kidney function despite apparent total-body fluid excess. Fluid assessment must be individualised with repeated examination and monitoring.

7. AKI recovery and long-term risk

Even transient AKI is associated with increased risk of CKD and recurrent AKI. Follow-up includes medication reconciliation, kidney function, albuminuria and cardiovascular risk management. Document who is responsible for repeat blood tests.

8. Five PLAB traps

  1. Using a single ‘normal’ creatinine to rule out evolving AKI.
  2. Giving large fluid boluses despite pulmonary oedema.
  3. Missing bladder outlet obstruction in an oliguric patient.
  4. Waiting for ultrasound before treating dangerous hyperkalaemia.
  5. Assuming dialysis is determined by creatinine alone.
Clinical summary: confirm an acute change → identify immediate risks → determine mechanism → treat cause and complications → document monitoring and follow-up.
