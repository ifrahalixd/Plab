Gallstones, Cholecystitis & Cholangitis

Emergency: Fever with jaundice, hypotension, confusion or peritonitis requires urgent hospital assessment.

Gallstone disease overview

Gallstones form in the gallbladder, commonly from cholesterol or pigment. Many are asymptomatic and do not require treatment.

PLAB application: Distinguish incidental gallstones from symptomatic biliary disease.

Biliary colic

A stone transiently obstructs the cystic duct, causing episodic steady right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, often after eating, without persistent fever or systemic illness.

PLAB application: Pain can last hours; recurrent symptoms prompt ultrasound and surgical assessment.

Acute cholecystitis

Persistent cystic duct obstruction causes gallbladder inflammation. Typical features include prolonged right upper quadrant pain, fever, tenderness and a positive Murphy sign.

PLAB application: Admit, provide analgesia, IV fluids as indicated, antibiotics according to local guidance and early surgical review.

Ascending cholangitis

Infection of an obstructed biliary tree, often from a common bile duct stone, can rapidly cause sepsis.

PLAB application: Charcot triad: fever, RUQ pain and jaundice; hypotension or confusion signals severe disease.

Choledocholithiasis

Common bile duct stones can cause obstructive jaundice, cholangitis or gallstone pancreatitis.

PLAB application: Look for elevated bilirubin/ALP/GGT and duct dilation; use MRCP or EUS when indicated.

Risk factors

Gallstones are associated with increasing age, female sex, pregnancy, obesity, rapid weight loss, family history and some haemolytic disorders.

PLAB application: Risk factors guide suspicion but do not establish diagnosis.

Focused history and examination

Ask about pain onset and duration, radiation, meals, fever, rigors, jaundice, pale stools, dark urine, vomiting, previous episodes and pregnancy.

PLAB application: Assess observations, hydration, abdominal guarding and signs of sepsis.

Laboratory investigations

FBC and CRP may support inflammation; liver tests help identify obstruction; lipase/amylase assesses pancreatitis; renal function and coagulation inform severity.

PLAB application: Blood cultures are appropriate for suspected sepsis before antibiotics if this does not delay treatment.

Ultrasound first-line

Abdominal ultrasound is the first-line imaging test for suspected gallstone disease; it may show stones, wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid or duct dilation.

PLAB application: A normal ultrasound does not fully exclude common bile duct stones.

MRCP, EUS and ERCP

MRCP and endoscopic ultrasound can clarify suspected duct stones. ERCP is primarily therapeutic, allowing duct clearance and drainage.

PLAB application: Do not use diagnostic ERCP routinely when non-invasive testing is suitable.

Biliary colic management

Offer analgesia, assess for complications and refer patients with symptomatic gallbladder stones for laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

PLAB application: Safety-net fever, persistent pain, jaundice or vomiting.

Acute cholecystitis management

Supportive care, antibiotics when indicated and early laparoscopic cholecystectomy are usual approaches.

PLAB application: NICE CG188 recommends early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 1 week of diagnosis when feasible.

Acute cholangitis management

Recognise sepsis, start ABCDE resuscitation, obtain cultures, give prompt IV antibiotics per local protocol and arrange urgent biliary decompression.

PLAB application: ERCP is usually the preferred drainage method; timing depends on severity and specialist assessment.

Gallstone pancreatitis

A migrating stone may obstruct the ampulla and cause pancreatitis. Treat pancreatitis supportively and assess for ongoing biliary obstruction or cholangitis.

PLAB application: Urgent ERCP is indicated for cholangitis or persistent obstruction, not routinely for every case.

Complications and differentials

Complications include empyema, gangrene, perforation, abscess, cholangitis and pancreatitis. Differentials include hepatitis, peptic ulcer disease, renal colic and cardiac disease.

PLAB application: Peritonism, hypotension or confusion warrants emergency escalation.

PLAB 2 counselling

Explain the distinction between stones, gallbladder inflammation and duct infection. Discuss ultrasound, surgery, possible ERCP, consent and recovery.

PLAB application: Use teach-back and give red-flag advice for fever, jaundice, persistent pain or collapse.

PLAB 2: biliary disease counselling

  1. Introduce yourself, confirm identity and ask permission.
  2. Explore pain, fever, jaundice, vomiting and patient concerns.
  3. Explain gallbladder stones versus bile duct obstruction and infection.
  4. Discuss ultrasound, MRCP, surgery or ERCP as appropriate.
  5. Safety-net jaundice, rigors, severe pain, confusion or collapse.

Mechanisms and exam reasoning

Deep Dive

Bile composition and stones

Cholesterol supersaturation and altered gallbladder motility contribute to cholesterol stone formation.

Cystic versus common duct

Cystic duct blockage causes colic or cholecystitis; CBD obstruction can cause jaundice, cholangitis and pancreatitis.

Ascending infection

Obstruction increases duct pressure and permits bacterial infection and systemic inflammation.

Why drainage matters

Antibiotics alone may not control infection until the obstructed biliary system is decompressed.

Imaging choices

Ultrasound detects gallbladder stones; MRCP/EUS improve evaluation of CBD stones; ERCP can treat obstruction.

Clinical prioritisation

Biliary colic is often non-emergent; cholangitis and perforated/gangrenous cholecystitis demand urgent escalation.
